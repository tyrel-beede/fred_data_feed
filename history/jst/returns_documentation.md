## (b) General definitions

### b.1 The four asset classes (RORE §1.1, pp.2–3)

**Bill returns** (p.2): "The canonical risk-free rate is taken to be the yield on Treasury bills, i.e., short-term, fixed-income government securities. The yield data come from the latest vintage of the long-run macrohistory database (Jordà, Schularick, and Taylor, 2017). Whenever data on Treasury bill returns were unavailable, we relied on either money market rates or deposit rates of banks from Zimmermann (2017). Since short-term government debt was rarely used and issued in the earlier historical period, much of our bill rate data before the 1960s actually consist of deposit rates." Footnote 2: total returns on deposits are hard to compute "because of uncertainty about losses during banking crises"; comparisons with Zimmermann (2017) deposit data "indicate that the interest rate differential between deposits and our bill series is very small, with deposit rates on average roughly 0.7 percentage points below bills—a return close to zero in real terms. The returns on government bills and deposits are also highly correlated over time."

**Bond returns** (p.2): "conventionally the total returns on long-term government bonds." Focus on "bonds listed and traded on local exchanges and denominated in local currency" (more comparable with bills, equities, housing; larger sample; bonds more likely held by the representative household). Gaps filled with listings on major global exchanges where domestic markets were thin (e.g. Australian bonds in New York or London). "Throughout the sample we target a maturity of around 10 years." Post-WW2 maturity "generally accurately defined"; pre-WW2 "we sometimes had to rely on data for perpetuals, i.e., very long-term government securities (such as the British consol)."

**Equity returns** (p.2): sources include "articles in economic and financial history journals, yearbooks of statistical offices and central banks, stock exchange listings, newspapers, and company reports." Aim: "indices weighted by market capitalization of individual stocks, and a stock selection that is representative of the entire stock market." For some periods "indices weighted by company book capital, stock market transactions, or weighted equally" are used "due to limited data availability."

**Housing returns** (p.3): combines the long-run house price series of Knoll, Schularick, and Steger (2017) with "a novel dataset on rents drawn from the unpublished PhD thesis of Knoll (2017)." Rents mostly from "the rent components of the cost of living of consumer price indices constructed by national statistical offices", combined with other sources back to the late 19th century. Returns "include both rented housing and owner-occupied properties" (fn 3: in line with Piketty et al. 2018 and Rognlie 2015). Where possible, house price and rental indices include owner-occupied properties and their imputed rents; imputed rents "are not directly observed and hence less precisely measured than market rents, and are typically not taxed."

**Composite returns** (p.3): "We compute the rate of return on safe assets, risky assets, and aggregate wealth, as weighted averages of the individual asset returns. To obtain a representative return from the investor's perspective, we use the outstanding stocks of the respective asset in a given country as weights." Weights use "new data on equity market capitalization (from Kuvshinov and Zimmermann, 2018) and housing wealth for each country and period in our sample, and combine them with existing estimates of public debt stocks".

### b.2 Formulas as written (RORE §1.2, pp.3–4)

"The total annual return on any financial asset can be divided into two components: the capital gain from the change in the asset price P, and a yield component Y, that reflects the cash-flow return on an investment. The total nominal return R for asset j in country i at time t is calculated as:"

- Eq. (1) Total return: R^j_{i,t} = (P^j_{i,t} − P^j_{i,t−1}) / P^j_{i,t−1} + Y^j_{i,t}

"In addition to individual asset returns, we also present a number of weighted 'composite' returns aimed at capturing broader trends in risky and safe investments, as well as the 'overall return' or 'return on wealth.'"

- Eq. (2) Safe return: R^safe_{i,t} = (R^bill_{i,t} + R^bond_{i,t}) / 2 — "For safe assets, we assume that total public debt is divided equally into bonds and bills since there are no data on their market shares (only for total public debt) over our full sample."
- Eq. (3) Risky return: R^risky_{i,t} = R^equity_{i,t} × w^equity_{i,t} + R^housing_t × w^housing_{i,t} — "The weights w represent the share of asset holdings of equity and of housing stocks in the respective country i and year t, scaled to add up to 1. We use stock market capitalization and housing wealth to calculate each share". (As printed, the housing return carries subscript t only, not i,t.)
- Eq. (4) Return on wealth: R^wealth_{i,t} = R^risky_{i,t} × w^risky_{i,t} + R^safe_{i,t} × w^safe_{i,t} — "The weights w here are the asset holdings of risky and safe assets in the respective country i and year t, scaled to add to 1."
- Footnote 4 (p.4): "For comparison, we also supply a series of the equally-weighted risky return, and the equally-weighted rate of return on wealth, both calculated as simple averages of housing and equity, and housing, equity and bonds respectively." (Note: the equally-weighted wealth return is described as the average of housing, equity and bonds — bills are not listed.)

R6 (p.10) restates eqs. (2)–(4) for the 2016–2020 extension with the same structure; its eq. (3) is printed as R^risky = R^equity × w^equity + **R^bond** × w^housing (an apparent typo for R^housing), and the weight constraint is written w^equity + w^housing + w^bill + w^bond = w^risky + w^safe = 1.

### b.3 Housing returns — the rent-price approach (RORE §1.3, pp.4–5; §2.2 p.8)

Starts from "a benchmark rent-price ratio (RI_0/HPI_0) estimated in a baseline year (t = 0). For this ratio we rely on net rental yields from the Investment Property Database (IPD)." Footnote 5: "These net rental yields use rental income net of maintenance costs, ground rent, and other irrecoverable expenditure. … We use net rather than gross yields to improve comparability with other asset classes." The time series is built from a country house price index (HPI_t/HPI_0) and rent index (RI_t/RI_0); "This method assumes that the indices cover a representative portfolio of houses. Under this assumption, there is no need to correct for changes in the housing stock, and only information about the growth rates in prices and rents is necessary."

- Eq. (5): RI_t / HPI_t = [ (RI_t/RI_0) / (HPI_t/HPI_0) ] × RI_0/HPI_0
- Eq. (6): R^housing_{t+1} = RI_{t+1} / HPI_t + (HPI_{t+1} − HPI_t) / HPI_t

(Note the timing in eq. (6): the rental-yield component is next year's rent index over this year's price index.)

Cross-checks (p.5): "Our rent-price approach is sensitive to the choice of benchmark rent-price-ratios and cumulative errors from year-by-year extrapolation." Verified against (i) the balance-sheet approach, eq. (7): RY^BS_t = Net rental income_t / Housing Wealth_t (national accounts rental income over housing wealth; can be added to capital gains for a balance-sheet total return); (ii) "point-in-time estimates of net rental yields from contemporary sources such as newspaper advertisements." "Wherever the rent-price approach estimates diverge from these historical sources, we make adjustments to benchmark the rent-price ratio estimates to these alternative historical measures of the rental yield." Footnote 6: the balance-sheet approach "can be imprecise due to measurement error in total imputed rent and national housing wealth estimates. Newspaper advertisements are geographically biased and only cover gross rental yields, so that the net rental yields have to be estimated."

§2.2 (p.8): the benchmark "net rent-price ratio—adjusted down for maintenance and other costs" is taken "in the year 2012, 2013 or 2014" and extrapolated back. Adjustment policy: "If the rent-price approach estimate differs substantially from those in the alternative sources, we adjust it so that the estimates are in line with each other. We do not adjust the series when these differences are small, or we have good reasons to doubt the quality of the alternative estimates. When we do adjust, we either benchmark our series to historical net rent-price ratios from alternative sources, or adjust the growth in the rental index by a multiplicative factor". In Figures 1–16 the series used are the "Rent-price ratio, final series" (green); raw unadjusted series are orange. Modern alternative benchmarks come from Numbeo.com (one- and three-bedroom apartments, city centre and rest of country), "adjusted down by us to proxy the impact of running costs and depreciation"; where cost data were unavailable, costs are taken as "about one-third of gross rent". For Australia and the US, transaction-level benchmark estimates are also used. "In two countries—Australia and Belgium—we judge one of these alternative modern-day benchmarks to be more reliable than the IPD ratio, and use it to construct our final baseline net rent-price ratio series."

### b.4 Rent-index methodology (RORE §2.3, pp.28–29)

"Rent indices measure the change in 'pure' rents for primary residences, i.e., net of house furnishings, maintenance costs, and utilities." Challenges listed: national vs. city coverage; housing-type coverage; contract rent vs. market rent (fn 9); quality improvement bias. Subsidized/controlled rents are typically included (exception fn 10: Canada excludes subsidized dwellings); sudden increases in the regulated share (wars, immediate post-war) can bias the index downward (fn 11: Australia CPI rent post-WW2). Owner-occupied housing: most statistical offices use the *rental equivalent approach* (fn 13: US, Japan, Denmark, Germany, Netherlands, Norway, Switzerland); the *user cost approach* is used (partially) in Canada, Finland, Sweden, UK (fn 15); the *net acquisitions approach* in Australia (fn 16). Fn 12: "Imputed rents of owner-occupied housing are excluded in Belgium and France."

### b.5 Nominal vs. real, and units

- Eq. (1) is explicitly "The total nominal return". **Neither document gives a formula for real returns or names a deflator.** Real returns are only mentioned narratively (e.g. RORE p.16 Tilly's "real net returns"; p.24 Real (1950) "real returns"; p.2 fn 2 "close to zero in real terms").
- **Units of the stored columns are not stated in either document.** What the documents do show: all formulas are ratios (decimal fractions); every rent-price ratio is quoted as a decimal (0.020, 0.032, 0.033, 0.045, 0.034, 0.054, 0.028, 0.047, 0.038, 0.056, 0.044, 0.037, 0.033, 0.025, 0.036, 0.040, 0.032, 0.049, 0.075 — RORE pp.9–27); Figures 1–16 plot rent-price ratios on 0–0.1/0.15/0.18/0.4/0.6 axes; R6 Figures 1–2 (p.7) plot "Total return" and "Capital gain return" on axes running −.6, −.2, .2, .6, 1, i.e. decimal fractions. Percent language appears only in narrative comparisons ("8 percent", "6%–8%"). **[inferred]**: the series are decimal fractions per year (0.05 = 5%); verify against the data file itself.

Caveats stated by the documents that apply across variables:

- Bills: pre-1960s mostly deposit rates (p.2); deposit rates ~0.7 pp below bills on average (fn 2).
- Bonds: pre-WW2 sometimes perpetuals; maturity varies (Table 1: e.g. Finland 5y 1926–1991, Spain 7–8y 1995–2015, UK 20y/15y); foreign-exchange listings used to fill gaps; some periods have no data (Germany 1923–25 hyperinflation, 1944–47, 1968; Japan 1941–1965 "government bond yield data (not total returns)"; Spain 1937–1939).
- Equity: weighting varies (book capital, transaction volume, equal weights in some periods, Table 2); exchange closures (Netherlands Aug 1944–Apr 1946, Switzerland mid-1914–mid-1916, Portugal 1975, Spain 1937–39, Japan 1946–47 no data).
- Housing: sensitive to benchmark and cumulative extrapolation error (p.5); rent indices lack quality/composition correction (every country section in §2.4); house-price and rent indices often cover different geographies/dwelling types ("we need to assume that changes in rents of different types of houses are strongly correlated", repeated for every country); rent controls bias rent indices downward in wars/post-war; UK rental expenditure "subject to large uncertainty" (ONS 2016 revision up to a factor of three, p.25); US series "should be viewed as conservative" (p.27); UK yields "somewhat conservative" (p.26).
- Composites: 50/50 bill-bond assumption; R6 weights missing for some country-years (see (f)).

### Australia
- Bills: 1870–1928 Deposit rate; 1929–1944 Money market rate; 1948–2015 Government bill rate. R6 2016–2020: Interbank rate (3-months rates and yields), OECD Main Economic Indicators.
- Bonds (type): 1900–1968 Long maturity, central gov't; 1969–2015 Approx. 10y, central gov't. Sources (Table 19, p.48): 1900–1925 total return on Australian government bonds listed in Sydney, Moore (2010b), converted from pound sterling to AUD; 1926–1968 Australian bonds listed in London — 1926–1929 from Meyer, Reinhart, and Trebesch (2015) (shared by Josefin Meyer), 1930–1968 constructed by the authors; 1969–1987 implied capital gain + yield from the RBA 10-year government bond yield series, capital gain from monthly yield movements, "Spliced with London listings data over 1968–1969"; 1988–2015 average of total returns on individual Australian government bonds, targeting 10-year maturity. R6: micro-level Australian government bonds, 10-year target, Datastream (re-estimated 1988–2020).
- Equity: 1870–1881 Listed abroad, Market cap; 1882–2015 Broad, Market cap. Sources: 1870–1881 sum of capital gains, dividends and gains/losses from stock operations for Australian shares listed in London, market-cap weighted, from *Investor Monthly Manual* (IMM) (http://som.yale.edu/imm-issues); 1882–2008 with-dividend return from Brailsford, Handley, and Maheswaran (2012) ("we use these series rather than the alternative from NERA Economic Consulting (2015) due to greater consistency with the IMM historical series"); 2009–2013 NERA Economic Consulting (2015); 2014–2015 ASX 200 total return index, RBA Statistics Table F7. R6: Fama-French (Kenneth French Data Library).
- Housing coverage: 1901–2015 Urban. Rents (Table 3, p.31): 1901–1920 ABS CPI A series as published in Stapledon (2012) — urban, all dwellings, average rents; 1921–1939 ABS CPI C series in Stapledon (2012) — urban, houses with 4–5 rooms; 1940–1989 Stapledon (2007) census-based index — urban, all kinds (used because the CPI rent index "increases much less than the index based on census data during the immediate post-WW2 decades", attributed to wartime rent controls introduced 1939 and lifted gradually after 1949, fn 20); 1990–2015 ABS CPI series — urban, all kinds, stratification. A series 1901–1938, 6 capital cities; C series from 1920, 30 towns (fn 18); since 1961 CPI rent based on 8 capital cities; correlation 0.75 between census and CPI index pre-WW2 (fn 19). Benchmark (p.9): Fox and Tulip (2014) gross rental yield 4.2%, running costs 1.1%, depreciation 1.1% → net rent-price ratio 0.02 for 2014 (used instead of IPD, which gives 0.032 for 2013, MSCI 2016). Adjustment: "structural break in 1949/1950" after lifting wartime price controls (introduced 1942); series benchmarked to the balance-sheet estimate in 1949. Balance sheet: OECD (2016a), Stapledon (2007), ABS (2014), Butlin (1985); housing wealth Stapledon (2007), Piketty and Zucman (2014). R6 rents: weighted average of eight capital cities, Australian Bureau of Statistics; dwellings 2016–2020 OECD 9B; market cap World Federation of Exchanges.

## (e) Interpolated / `_interp` / `_ipolated` variables

**Neither document mentions any variable named with `_interp` or `_ipolated`, nor does either define an "interpolated" variant of a return series.** The only interpolation/extrapolation statements in the documents are the following; which (if any) of them the dataset flags in those columns cannot be determined from these PDFs and should be checked against the JST codebook/main documentation:

Housing (price and rent indices):
- The rent-price approach itself is a forward/back extrapolation from one benchmark year (eq. (5), RORE p.5) and "cumulative errors from year-by-year extrapolation" are acknowledged.
- UK: 1915–1919 rent changes interpolated with Samy (2015) London index (p.45, Table 17); 1947–1955 rent-price ratio extrapolated with balance-sheet growth, benchmarked to 1946/1956 (p.25, fn 7); 1946–1954 rents linked using Holmans (2005) local-authority rents (p.45).

- Denmark: 1870–1926 and 1955–1964 Copenhagen series used as proxies for Denmark (p.33).

- Canada: house prices 2013–2016 from OECD, no returns.

Equity:
- Portugal 1975: exchange closed; no dividends assumed and "interpolated the stock prices of firms listed both before and after the closure" (p.59).

## (f) The R6 update note (July 2022)

**Main macro data changes (R6 §1, pp.2–3)** — four updates: (1) discontinued IMF series replaced using "the IMF's original data source" — affected: short-term interest rates, long-term interest rates, narrow money, broad money; (2) Ireland added (18 countries); (3) "major revision on the GDP and related series" — current accounts revised in many countries, feeding nominal GDP, investment-to-GDP, debt-to-GDP, real GDP per capita; consumption per capita minor revisions; (4) three new variables: unemployment rate and nominal wages index (1990 = base) from Gabriel (2021) (unemployment: % of unemployed in total labor force where possible; early data from trade-union/insured subsets, assuming their growth rates track national unemployment; recent data ILOSTAT CPS/EU-LFS; wages: index of average earnings of all employees where possible, earlier sector-specific; recent IMF wage index and OECD), and corporate debt from Jordà, Kornejew, Schularick, Taylor (2022) (business lending by banks and financial markets, incl. bond market debt and non-bank credit, non-corporate business liabilities where available; construction differs pre-/post-WW2; Online Appendix H).

**RORE extension 2016–2020 (R6 §2, pp.3–11)** — "we present the updated rates of returns based on the paper … During the update, there has been changes in the previous data on total bond returns of Australia, Finland, Italy, Japan, Netherlands, and Norway during 1988-2020 … Also, note that for some countries, not all data were available for the full 2016-2020 period."
