# Ray Dalio, *Principles* (2011 free PDF) — mechanism extraction

Source: `Principles_2011.pdf`, 199 pages (an unofficial Kindle/Calibre conversion of the 2011 bwater.com PDF; converter note on p2). Page numbers below are the PDF page index (1–199), not any printed numbering. All text is paraphrase in my words; the few quotations are at most 25 words and marked with page.

## (a) Document structure

- **Front matter (pp. 2–6).** Converter note; a reading guide; an introduction. Dalio says ~200 principles cover nearly all the problems he has seen (p5) and that every principle should be met with the question "Is it true?" (p6, p9).
- **Part 1: The Importance of Principles (pp. 7–9).** Five unnumbered questions (what principles are, why they matter, where they come from, which you hold, how well they work). No mechanism content beyond "ask yourself 'Is it true?'".
- **Part 2: My Most Fundamental Life Principles (pp. 10–56).** Memoir ("Where I'm coming from", pp. 10–17), then: Truth as the foundation (p19); hyperrealism and evolution (pp. 18–23); ego barriers (p24); five decision trees (pp. 26–31); "Your two yous and your machine" (pp. 32–35); the 5-Step Process (pp. 35–54); Bridgewater's core beliefs (pp. 55–56). Not numbered as principles; each sub-section ends with self-assessment questions.
- **Part 3: My Management Principles (pp. 57–184).** Introduction (pp. 57–62); a "Summary and Table of Principles" (pp. 63–80) which is the outline; then the full text ("What follows is the Meat", pp. 81–184).
  - **Numbering.** One running sequence, 1–210. Bold-caps section heads carry their own number (1, 8, 20, 37, 44, 52, 64, 90, 100, 117, 128, 133, 147, 163, 166, 188, 190, 196, 198, 205); the principles under each head continue the count ("... 2)", "... 3)"); sub-principles are lettered (24a–24f, 31a–31c, 33a–33l, etc.).
  - **Four groups:** "To get the culture right" (1–36); "To get the people right" (37–132); "To perceive, diagnose, and solve problems" (133–189); "To make decisions effectively" (190–210).
- **Endnotes [1]–[72] (pp. 185–196)**, then a duplicated table of contents (pp. 197–199).

## (b) Epistemic mechanisms — how truth is found

Format: **principle no. (if any)** — paraphrase — page.

### Foundations (Part 2, unnumbered)

- **"Is it true?"** — the test every belief and every principle is to be subjected to; the author asks readers to judge his principles by it rather than adopt them. pp. 5–6, 9, 28; restated as mgmt principle 22, p89.
- **Truth as foundation** — "an accurate understanding of reality" is "the essential foundation for producing good outcomes" (p19). Good/bad are defined as operating consistently/inconsistently with how reality works, judged by what gets rewarded and punished rather than by group opinion. pp. 18–20.
- **Principles are probabilistic claims that get stress-tested.** Nothing is certain; "the best we can hope for is highly probable"; publishing the principles and letting hundreds of skeptics test them raises the probability they are right (p5). The theory of the culture is treated as having been "tested" over 40 years with results that verify it (p61).
- **The five personal practices** (unnumbered, repeated three times): work for what you want; form the best independent opinions you can; stress-test them by having the smartest people you can find try to find where you are wrong, caring about their reasoning not their conclusions; stay wary of overconfidence and be "good at not knowing" (keep gathering information until confident, or remove your exposure to the risk); wrestle with reality, experience the consequences of decisions and reflect on them to improve. pp. 11–12, 14, 17; restated as what Bridgewater collectively does, pp. 59–60.
- **Opinions should have a cost.** Markets taught him that bad opinions are costly there while elsewhere people produce opinions at no cost, so he became cautious about his own (p11). Endnote 7: when he knows he doesn't know, he positions so the outcome doesn't affect him; he limits bets to the few things he is confident in (p186). Endnote 14: people who are quick with answers usually haven't considered the ways they could be wrong (p187).
- **Five decision trees** (pp. 26–31), each a fork between a losing and a winning habit:
  1. React to pain with fight/flight vs. reflect: **"Pain + Reflection = Progress"** (p27; repeated p47, p87).
  2. Confuse what you wish were true with what is true vs. ask "Is it true?" (p28).
  3. Worry about looking good (hide what you don't know) vs. worry about being good; the best decision-makers rarely believe they hold the best answer and seek believable people who disagree (pp. 28–30).
  4. Overweight first-order consequences vs. weigh second- and third-order consequences (p30).
  5. Blame others vs. hold yourself accountable for outcomes (p31). Endnote 38: luck is real but not an excuse; over a large sample of decisions (poker hands) luck cancels and skill dominates (p190).
- **Ego barrier** — defensive, emotional (amygdala) reactions that stop people from reflecting on weaknesses or letting others explore them; called the single biggest problem of mankind; because one's own weaknesses are hard to see, others' help is essential (p24). Diagnosis requires "the willingness to touch people's nerves" to get past ego barriers (p47). Endnote 52: ego stops people admitting what they don't know (p192).
- **Blind spot barrier** — two reasons one cannot see one's own weaknesses: ego barriers, and that a missing ability is like a missing sense, which you cannot perceive lacking; hence others must show you (pp. 52–53).
- **The "two yous"** — you(1) is the designer/overseer of the plan; you(2) is one participant/resource in it; you(1) must assess you(2) objectively, assign you(2) only what you(2) does well, and if not, "fire yourself(2)" and get a replacement; finding out you(2) is bad at something is good news for you(1) (pp. 32–35; endnote 40, p190).
- **The machine metaphor** — goals determine the machine you build; the machine (design + people) produces outcomes; compare outcomes to goals; when they diverge, change the design or the people; "higher level thinking" is looking down on the machine and yourself inside it (pp. 32–33). Organizations are machines too, with culture and people as the two parts (p58). Restated as principles 64–67 (pp. 115–116), 148 (p149), 167 (p164).
- **Feedback-loop rate determines evolution rate** — many high-quality loops give a steep improvement curve; few or poor loops give a flat or declining one; the main difference between great and bad organizations is how well they manage their feedback loops (pp. 58–59).
- **The 5-Step Process** — (1) set clear goals; (2) identify and don't tolerate problems; (3) diagnose them to root causes; (4) design plans; (5) do the tasks. Rules: keep steps distinct; each needs different abilities; stay unemotional; the process is iterative; you need not be good at every step if you know your weak step and get help (pp. 35–37). Goals should change slowly, designs faster, tasks fastest (p52). The process doubles as a diagnostic: find which step you fail at, list the qualities it needs, see which you lack (p53). Summarized as Values → Goals → Problems → Diagnoses → Designs → Tasks (p54). Management versions: 134 (p142), 152 (p152).
- **Self-assessment must cite evidence.** Each step closes with: how confident are you in your self-assessment, and why — e.g. a demonstrated track record, or many believable people having told you (pp. 42, 43, 45, 48, 50, 51). The same "-5 to +5, how confident?" pattern is used for thinking-style self-ratings (pp. 104–108).
- **Problems as signal** — problems are improvements "screaming at you"; be precise about which problem; don't confuse a problem with its cause ("I can't sleep" is a cause; "I perform badly because I'm tired" is the problem) (pp. 42–44). Tolerating a problem is as bad as not seeing it; endnote 47: people stop caring to solve problems when expected reward is below expected cost (p44, p191).
- **Root cause vs proximate cause** — proximate causes are actions (verbs: "didn't check the schedule"); root causes are reasons, usually traits (adjectives: "forgetful"); only removing root causes removes problems (p46). Restated in 151 (p151) and drilldown step 2 (p156).
- **Design before doing; written plan; external benchmarks** — a plan is a movie script of who does what when; write it down; set clear benchmarks and ideally have someone other than yourself measure whether the plan is being followed (pp. 48–51).
- **Track records are built from many decisions** — the volume of decisions guarantees chances to build a record (p38); start with modest goals and raise them as the track record gives you grounds for confidence (p40).

### Culture principles (1–36)

- **1–4** — Trust in truth; nothing to fear from it; everyone has the right to understand what makes sense and no one may hold a critical opinion without voicing it; be extremely open ("just don't filter"); views are judged on their merits, producing a "meritocracy of thought" (pp. 81). 
- **5, 5a, 5b** — Integrity: never say about someone what you wouldn't say to their face; don't "try" people in their absence; loyalty must not suppress truth, partly because it removes a subordinate's right of appeal (pp. 82–83).
- **6, 6a** — Radical transparency: give people direct exposure so they form their own views; a candid Q&A lets people probe your thinking; "Record almost all meetings and share them with all relevant people" (p83).
- **7, 7a** — Don't tolerate dishonesty; don't believe a caught liar's promise to reform (p83).
- **8–19** — Mistake culture: OK to make mistakes, unacceptable not to identify, analyze and learn from them (p84); patterns of mistakes reveal weaknesses, which are either learning deficits (fixable slowly) or ability deficits (nearly unfixable, design around) (11, p85); replace "blame/credit" with "accurate/inaccurate" (14, p86); **don't depersonalize mistakes — name the person**, because "we didn't handle this well" hides the diagnosis (15, p86); write down your own and others' weaknesses (16, p86); when in pain, reflect, and ask believable others to help find what is true (17, pp. 86–87); self-reflective vs "deflective" people (18, p87); bring mistakes into the open, penalize cover-ups, and add lessons to the procedures manual (19, p87); the issues log is the main tool and its use is mandatory (19a, p88).
- **21–24** — Constantly get in synch about what is true and what to do; ask "Is it true?" and "Does it make sense?"; fight for the right answer with the relevant parties; be assertive and open-minded at once — the goal is to find out what is true, not to win; being found wrong is more valuable than being right (p89). **24a**: ask whether you have earned the right to an opinion; **24b**: you always have the right to ask questions; **24c–f**: open-minded people ask, closed-minded people tell; avoid closed-minded inexperienced people; beware the intellectual commenting "from the stands"; beware people who find it embarrassing not to know (pp. 89–90).
- **25** — Responsible parties must explain their reasoning openly so it can be assessed; on disagreement an appeal goes to the manager's boss or an agreed knowledgeable group that generally includes people more believable and senior than the decision-maker; the arbiters must be objective or the meritocracy fails (p90).
- **26, 26a, 26b** — Conflict is how people find out whether their principles align; disagreement between thoughtful people is "giant untapped potential"; endnote 53: there is an overabundance of confident bad opinions and very little learning from disagreement (pp. 90–91, p192).
- **27, 27a–d** — Know when to stop debating; but if people disagree about whether something is worth debating, debate it, otherwise the boss gets a de facto veto; judge how a responsible party does the job by whether it is done well, not your way; evaluate a disputed decision within its broader context; distinguish idle complaints from improvement-seeking ones (pp. 91–92).
- **29, 29a–d** — Choose debate (among approximate equals), discussion (across levels), or teaching (between levels) according to goal and relative believability; say which you are having; involve the most relevant people (managers, reports, agreed experts) for best-answer communication, a wider set for cohesion; the Tiger Woods example: don't weight a novice's view equally with a champion's, but the novice should keep questioning until convinced, approaching it without overblown confidence (pp. 92–94).
- **30, 30a** — Don't treat all opinions as equal; views without a track record aren't equal to views with one; all views are still considered, but "placed in the proper context of experience and track record"; the test is "can you handle your responsibilities well?"; a hierarchy of merit is essential to a meritocracy of ideas, partly for time reasons (p94).
- **31** — **Believability** defined as "the probability that a person's view will be right," roughly assessed from the quality of their reasoning and their track record; ask "Why should I believe you?" and "Why should I believe myself?" (pp. 94–95).
  - **31a** — with a demonstrated track record you may have an opinion on how to do the thing; without one you may only have theories and questions (p95).
  - **31b** — the believability tiers: people who have repeatedly succeeded at the thing *and* give great explanations when probed are most believable; one of the two, somewhat; neither, least. Ideas are nonetheless judged on their merits so that newcomers can build their own track records; because of the openness, everyone can see how these assessments are made. State your confidence honestly: a suggestion is a suggestion, a conviction a conviction; newcomers should ask, highly believable confident people should assert (p95).
  - **31c** — before answering, ask whether you are the responsible party/right person to answer (p95).
- **33, 33a–l** — Meeting mechanics: a named responsible party for each meeting; pick participants by the value they add, never by whether they agree with you; a small group of 3–5 smart conceptual people beats a single decision-maker, which beats a large leaderless group; 1+1=3 with diminishing then negative returns from added people; run the conversation at two levels (the case, and the principle/machine it exemplifies), which also tests the principles; enforce logic; substance over style; reach completion by stating conclusions, working theories and assigned to-dos in writing; assign a note-taker; don't lose personal responsibility in group decisions (pp. 95–98).
- **35** — Getting in synch is two-way; assume transmission errors and consider multiple interpretations (p99).

### People principles (37–132) — the epistemic parts

- **56c, 56d, 56f, 56g** — Pay attention to track records; dig into why people did what they did; ask for past reviews from believable people and look for documented evidence; check references (p112). **60** — the clearest demonstration of excellence is outstanding performance within an outstanding peer group (p113).
- **65b** — An expert is a better and harsher critic than a novice (the Olympic skier judging the instructor) (p115).
- **66** — Compare outcomes to goals constantly so you have a large sample; a large sample distinguishes one-off errors from root causes (pp. 115–116).
- **69** — When a problem occurs hold two discussions: why the machine produced it, and what to do now (p116).
- **70b** — Explain the logic of rules; each person's job is to evaluate whether they agree and say why, and to hold each other to the principles (p117).
- **72, 72a** — Daily accountability builds the sample size that reveals problems early; distinguish a broken "contract" from a case where no expectation was ever set, which is a design fault (p118).
- **73a** — Test whether someone understands goals vs tasks by asking "how is goal X going?": a good answer synthesizes first, then supports with tasks (p118–119).
- **81a, 83** — Daily updates (five-minute notes of what was done, planned, problems, questions); managers scan the outputs of tools — issues log, metrics, daily updates, checklists — and spot-check (pp. 122–123).
- **82, 83** — Confidence in a person is learned, not presumed; vary probing with how suspicious an area looks (pp. 122–123).
- **88** — Escalation: if the boss simply does the failing person's job, the attribution of success and failure is lost; the aim is to learn what a person is like "without crashing the car" (pp. 123–124).
- **92–99** — Probe your reports and have them probe you; probing invites subterranean discontent to the surface and makes people players rather than spectators; probe two levels down and invite people two levels down to bring disagreements with their bosses to you; few people see themselves objectively; fight every battle because small badness is symptomatic; ask hard questions and "independently audit"; don't assume answers are correct — double-check, and trust in a person's statements is gained or lost through that process; **make probing transparent rather than private so others can judge its quality** (pp. 126–127).
- **101** — Accurate assessments take time and back-and-forth; judge by whether the job is done well, not your way; consult other believable, honest people; don't be overconfident (p128).
- **105** — Treat your view of a person as a stated theory: articulate it, share it, test it with training and assignments, revise it on observed performance (p129).
- **106d** — Reviews should hold few surprises; agreed patterns emerge from probing specific cases; everyone fails sometimes ("every batter strikes out a lot"), so the goal is the pattern; a person's track record is "the level and the steepness" of the trajectories they own, "rather than the wiggles"; performance problems are design problems or fit/ability problems, and ability problems are innate or training-related (pp. 130–131).
- **109, 109a, 109b** — The only reason to look at what people did is to learn what they are like; judge patterns, not single events; distinguish "you made a bad decision" from "you are a bad decision-maker", which only a pattern can show; some qualities need one observation, others five to ten; being bad at one thing doesn't mean bad at everything (p133).
- **110** — Poor performance is either inadequate learning (fixable) or inadequate ability (not) (p134).
- **111, 111a, 111b** — The two biggest assessment errors are overconfidence and failing to get in synch; evaluations should run both ways, not only manager-to-managee (pp. 134–135).
- **113** — When both sides genuinely agree on a weakness it is probably true; write the agreed conclusion on the person's baseball card (p135).
- **114** — Judgments of people need a mutually agreed "by-and-large" understanding with high confidence, not proof beyond doubt; perfect understanding wastes time (p136).
- **115–116** — Rough assessment after 6–12 months of close contact and tests, confident after ~18 months; keep re-assessing; manager-to-managee ratios should be at most 1:10, preferably 1:5, so there is enough contact (p136).
- **118** — At least two believable trainers per trainee, to triangulate (p137).
- **121** — Everything is a case study: ask what it is a case of and which principles apply (p138).
- **124** — Decide which mistakes are acceptable: let people "scratch or dent the car" but not total it (p139).
- **127** — Separate deciding "what's true" (months, large sample) from "what to do about it" (hours or days) (p140).

### Problem-solving principles (133–189)

- **136–137** — Problems are fuel; the key perception is whether something is above or below the bar, which requires synthesis (p142).
- **140, 140a–d** — Have as many eyes looking for problems as possible; keep communication flowing; hold people accountable for raising complaints and escalating if ignored; the leader must encourage disagreement and be impartial or open-minded; the people closest to a job know it best (pp. 142–143).
- **141** — Perceive problems by comparing "how the movie is unfolding relative to your script" — the expected range of outcomes vs actual; a flat trajectory against an expected rising band signals a root cause (p145).
- **142–143** — No anonymous "we" or "they"; name names; start with specific problems, then observe patterns (p145–146).
- **144** — Tools for catching problems: issues log, metrics, surveys, checklists, outside consultants, internal auditors (pp. 146–147). Details in (c).
- **145–146a** — The "frog in the boiling water" problem; problems are ranked by state: unidentified (worst), identified without a planned solution (worst for morale), identified with a plan, solved (pp. 147–148).
- **148–150** — Every problem is an output of the machine (design + people), so diagnose both; a scripted set of diagnostic questions: what sub-optimality was experienced; who is the responsible party for the machine as a whole; what was the responsible party's "mental map" of how it should have worked (a mental map must allow for imperfect people); what broke — design or behavior — by comparing map to what happened; proximate causes (verbs) vs root causes (reasons); is it consistent with prior patterns, and what systematic change follows; a diagnosis should take 10–20 minutes and be done constantly so no single case is a big deal (pp. 149–151).
- **152** — Locate chronic failure in a 5-Step step, each requiring different abilities: goal-setting (vision, values), perceiving problems (perception, synthesis, intolerance of badness), diagnosis (logic, assertiveness, open-mindedness), design (creativity, visualization), tasks (determination, discipline) (p152).
- **153–154** — Diagnosis is collaborative because there is a reasonably high probability you don't know the answer; diagnoses must produce outcomes — at least written theories of root causes, kept as "a collection of synthesized dots" for pattern-finding (pp. 152–153).
- **155–156** — Don't over-squeeze one "dot" (one outcome); collect many quickly, triangulate with others' dots, and build a "pointillist painting" of the person; maintain a continuously updated synthesis through time (p153).
- **159** — Avoid "Monday morning quarterbacking": judge a past decision by what could reasonably have been known at the time, asking what a quality person should have known and done (p154).
- **160–161** — Identify which principles were violated and whether they would have helped; same people doing the same things yields the same results (p154).
- **162** — The "drilldown": list problems (specific, not solutions, name names); identify root causes (separate session; keep asking why; remove the blame/credit mentality; use the issues log to observe patterns rather than judge quickly); create a plan; implement with monthly to-do lists, open monthly progress meetings, metrics on commitments, and a publicly posted plan; include the people under scrutiny so they can argue back (pp. 154–159).
- **164, 164a** — Go back before going forward: diagnose what went right and wrong, "tell the story" of how you got here; new employees listen to recorded tapes of the company's story (p160).
- **165** — "Above the line" conversation moves through main points to a synthesis; "below the line" dissects details for their own sake (pp. 160–163, with diagrams).
- **172–173** — Visualize alternative machines and their outcomes month by month before choosing; think about second- and third-order consequences (the "1.6 effect") (pp. 165–166).
- **184c** — "Double-do" rather than double-check: two people independently do the same task and the answers are compared, which catches more errors and reveals differences in people's performance (p172).

### Decision principles (190–210)

- **191** — Your probability of holding the best answer is small, and even if you have it you can't be confident unless other believable people test you (p175).
- **192, 192a, 192b** — Dealing with not knowing beats knowing; ask "What don't I know, and what should I do about it?"; ask believable people for their reasoning, not their conclusions; successful people objectively assess the probability that they have the answers (p175).
- **193** — The goal is the best answer, not the best answer you personally have (p175).
- **194** — "While everyone has the right to have questions and theories, only believable people have the right to have opinions" — you may still voice views if you flag that you are unsure (p175).
- **195, 195a, 195b** — Constantly worry about what you are missing; ask for criticism; **triangulate**: no important decision without asking at least three believable people, understanding and assessing their reasoning, and having them probe yours (p176).
- **196–197** — Make all decisions as expected-value calculations: weigh probabilities and payoffs; make the probability of ruin nil; the cost of a bad decision is at least the reward of a good one, so knowing what you don't know is as valuable as knowing; seek asymmetric bets with little downside even at low probability; **197c**: people voice opinions once they think they are just over 50% likely right and ignore the value of pushing that to 85% by investigating and checking with others; **197d**: don't bet too much on anything — 15 or more good, uncorrelated bets (p177).
- **199–200** — 80/20: typically fewer than five things matter in a decision; decide timing by marginal value of more information vs cost of delay (pp. 179–180).
- **202** — The best choices have more pros than cons, not zero cons; distrust people who argue against things merely by finding a flaw (p180).
- **203** — Possibilities raised without probabilities wreck prioritization; "almost anything is possible", so every possibility must carry a likelihood (p180).
- **204, 204a** — Use "by and large": a claim that is mostly true should be treated as true enough rather than derailed by exceptions; "it's not totally true" usually means true enough (pp. 180–181).
- **206–207** — Connect the dots over time: categorize each outcome by type and quality, and chart improvement vs deterioration by type; it takes about five dots of one type to find a root cause; the level, not the rate of change, is what matters, judged against an "acceptable bar" and an "excellent bar" (pp. 182–184).
- **208** — If your best solution isn't good enough, think harder or escalate that fact (p184).
- Endnote 30: ask believable people, especially those with opposing views who want the truth rather than to be proven right, about the causes of your pain (p189).

## (c) Scoring and incentive mechanisms

- **Track record as the primary credential.** The right to an opinion is conditional on a demonstrated track record (24a p89, 30 p94, 31a p95); "the proof is in the pudding" — can you handle the responsibility (30, p94). In hiring: look at track records, prior reviews from believable people, documented evidence, references (56c, 56f, 56g, p112), and outstanding performance within an outstanding peer group (60, p113). Over a large sample of decisions luck cancels and skill shows (endnote 38, p190).
- **Believability tiers (31b).** Two inputs — repeated demonstrated success at the thing in question, and great explanations when probed; both = most believable, one = somewhat, neither = least (p95). Believability is assessed per person and openly (p95); different people may rate it differently, which is accepted (p94).
- **Confidence labeling.** Everyone must state how confident they are; suggestions and convictions must be labeled as such; newcomers ask, proven people assert (31b, p95). Opinions are typically voiced at just over 50% confidence while raising it to 85% is where the value lies (197c, p177).
- **"Baseball cards" and "believability matrixes" (101b).** Keep a card per person showing performance statistics the way a baseball card shows batting average and errors; it is explicitly for seeing what people do well and poorly, calling on the right people for the right roles transparently, and simplifying decisions on compensation, incentives, promotion and cuts; inputs he uses: "ratings, forced rankings, metrics, results, and credentials"; cards are passed to prospective managers (p128). Agreed conclusions about weaknesses are written on the card (113, p135); when someone is sorted out of a role, the reasons are recorded on the card (129, p141).
- **Believability-weighted voting.** Endnote 54: he once "toyed with the possibility of developing a voting system based on a believability matrix"; it may be impractical, but it expresses the merit-based decision-making the process aims at; the probing is meant to assess the quality of work over time, not second-guess every decision (p192). In the body: open debate is not a referendum (28 p92, 34 p98); the decision hierarchy should be "ideally based on merit" (34, p99).
- **Evaluation instruments (101a, 144).** Performance surveys, metrics, formal reviews documenting all aspects of performance (101a, p128). Metrics measure individual, group and system performance; a single metric misleads, so look for patterns; metrics and 360 reviews help agreement on strengths and weaknesses; **grader bias is detected by comparing each grader's average grade** (lenient graders show up as high averages); **forced rankings** (best to worst, i.e. grading on a curve); metrics that allow independent grading across departments are especially valuable; metrics must not be gamed and should be used to ask questions rather than to manage mechanically (pp. 146–147).
- **Issues log as a scored, incentivized ledger (19a, 144).** Logging is mandatory (p88). Anything that went wrong goes in; the log must contain a frank assessment of individuals' contributions, strengths and weaknesses; counts and types of logged issues become performance metrics; usage is rewarded and non-use punished — if something goes wrong and is not in the log the responsible people are "in big trouble", but if it is there and diagnosed they are likely praised; personal accountability is non-negotiable (p146). Issues-log output feeds the living procedures manual so diagnosed mistakes aren't repeated (183, p171).
- **Radical transparency of records and reasoning.** Almost all meetings recorded and shared (6a, p83); probing is public so its quality can be judged by others (99, p127); assessments of believability are visible to all (31b, p95); candidates are shown the principles and the tapes (58, p113); drilldown plans are posted publicly and reviewed with the group (p159); logic behind rules is explained and feedback welcomed (70b, p117).
- **Attribution: name names.** Mistakes are tied to specific people (15, p86; 142, p145; 150, p150); the anonymous "we" is treated as hiding responsibility; "we should..." statements are replaced by named responsible parties (78b, p121).
- **Accuracy replaces blame and credit.** Feedback is framed as "accurate/inaccurate" for the future rather than punishment for the past (14, p86); the blame-and-credit mentality is removed as a precondition for honest diagnosis (p157); accurate criticism is treated as the most valuable thing a person can receive (104, p129).
- **Pain and consequences as the learning signal.** "Pain + Reflection = Progress" (p27, p47, p87); growing pains (p47); people learn principles by experiencing the rewards and punishments of their actions, and clearly designated responsibility sharpens that feedback (endnote 64, pp. 193–194); people don't fix problems when expected reward < expected cost, e.g. when working for others' goals without supervision, reward or punishment (endnote 47, p191); managers must force difficult things (75, p120) and hold people accountable daily (72, p118); "you will get what you deserve over time" (41, p101); acceptable mistakes are bounded by the damage they can do (124, p139); people are helped through the pain of exploring weaknesses, with calm follow-up (112, p135).
- **Status is earned incrementally and re-tested.** Confidence in a person is learned, not presumed (82, p122); involvement scales inversely with confidence (83, p122); careers aren't planned — roles evolve from discovered strengths and weaknesses through periodic mini-reviews and major reviews (108, p132); 6–12 months for a rough read, ~18 months for a confident one (115, p136); continue assessing throughout (116, p136).
- **Sorting and the bar.** Someone who doesn't "click" is moved out fast; reasons are logged (129, p141); keeping an unsuited person "erodes meritocracy" (130, p141); do not lower the bar (132, p142); no opt-outs from exploring one's weaknesses, because two sets of rules would undermine the system (209, p184).
- **Double-do (184c)** — independent duplicate work that both verifies results and exposes performance differences (p172).
- **Controls and audit (187, 187a, 187b).** Controls exist so that trust is never the question; auditors report outside the audited department and audit procedures are not disclosed to those audited; "no sense in having laws unless you have policemen" (pp. 172–173).
- **Level, not rate (207).** An improving but still-below-bar performer is not good enough; everything managed must be on a trajectory to cross the acceptable bar in acceptable time (pp. 183–184).
- **Daily updates, checklists, procedures manual** (81a p122; 184 p171; 183 p171) — low-cost logging instruments that make work auditable; checklists are not a substitute for responsibility (184a, p171).

## (d) Governance mechanisms

- **The responsible party (RP).** The most important decisions are who you choose as RP (37, p100); the inevitable RP is the person who bears the consequences and cannot delegate that away, so must pick and incentivize delegates well (40, p101); the most important RPs are those responsible for goals, outcomes and machines, i.e. higher in the pyramid (42, pp. 101–102); only people who understand goals vs tasks should run things (43, p102).
- **Authority maps to assessed ability, not to votes.** Responsibility and authority are assigned to individuals based on an assessment of their ability; they are held accountable for outcomes and given authority to achieve them; an RP may carry through a decision that knowledgeable others disagree with, after weighing the disagreement seriously; "an explicit decision-making hierarchy, ideally based on merit" (34, pp. 98–99). Open debate informs the decision-maker, it does not create rule by referendum (28, p92). Group oversight of a person can exist, but only when designed into the structure with overseers chosen for knowledge and judgment (28, p92).
- **Rights are tiered.** Everyone may ask questions and hold theories; only believable people may hold opinions (24b p89; 194 p175); the right to complain, advise and debate is distinct from the right to decide (34, p98); a hierarchy of merit is essential (30a, p94).
- **Appeal and escalation paths.** Disagreement with an RP is appealed to the RP's boss or an agreed knowledgeable group including people more believable and senior; arbiters must be objective or the meritocracy fails (25, p90). If you can't get in synch, agree on a believable third party (36, p99). Escalate when you can't handle a responsibility (88, p123) with an explicit "escalation button" tool so escalation isn't mistaken for complaining (88b, p124). People are accountable for raising complaints and for escalating if the response is inadequate (140b, p143). Cross-departmental issues go to the "point of the pyramid" above both parties (89, p124). Repeating problems that can't be resolved must be elevated (endnote 69, p194). Escalate when your best solution isn't good enough (208, p184).
- **Anti-veto rule.** When people disagree about whether an issue deserves debate, it gets debated, so the boss can't quietly veto (27a, p91).
- **Oversight structure.** Everyone is overseen by a believable person with high standards (175a, p170); manager ratios capped at 1:10, preferably 1:5 (175c, p170); clear single reporting lines with no crossing (176, 176a, p167); the "clover-leaf": two or three RPs with overlapping believabilities who challenge and check each other and escalate what they can't resolve (178, p168).
- **Leader obligations.** The leader must encourage disagreement and be impartial or open-minded (140c, p143); RPs must explain their reasoning openly (25, p90); don't try to be followed, try to be understood (70, p116); explain the logic of rules and each person is expected to judge whether they agree and say why (70b, p117); the manager's job is "to get at truth and excellence, not to make people happy" (p159); don't try to please everyone (210, p184).
- **Guards against bad incentives and gaming.** Metrics must not be gamed (144, p146); grader leniency detected via average grades and forced rankings (144, p147); independent auditors with undisclosed procedures (187a, p173); organize around goals so that merged functions don't produce conflicting incentives (174, p166); support departments don't set the vision ("department slip", 180, pp. 168–169); responsibilities by workflow and ability, not title (181, p169); don't believe a caught liar's reform (7a, p83); no talking behind backs (5a, p82); "loyalty" must not suppress truth because it eliminates the right of appeal (5b, p82); don't pick meeting participants for agreeing with you (33b, p96); don't lose personal responsibility in group decisions (33l, p98); distinguish broken contracts from missing contracts and fix the design when the latter (72a, p118); agreed goals and tasks are displayed to the whole department monthly or quarterly so it is clear who is keeping up their end (78a, p121).
- **The principles are themselves governed.** They are an evolving reference, to be stress-tested rather than obeyed, and future managers may replace them (p5, p9, endnotes 1–2 p185); every case is to be connected to the principle it exemplifies, which also tests the principle (68 p116; 121 p138; 33f p97); diagnoses identify which principles were violated (160, p154).

## (e) Mapping onto an open forecasting ledger — my mapping, not Dalio's

| Dalio mechanism | Open public scoring-system equivalent |
|---|---|
| Believability = probability a person's view is right, from track record + reasoning quality (31, p94) | Credibility score per forecaster computed from resolved forecasts (proper scoring rule) plus a legibility/rationale component |
| Believability tiers: repeated success AND good explanations when probed (31b, p95) | Two-axis reputation: calibration/resolution record, and rationale quality rated after resolution; both high = top tier |
| Baseball cards / believability matrix with ratings, forced rankings, metrics, results (101b, p128) | Public per-forecaster card: Brier/log score by domain, sample size, calibration curve, rank among peers |
| Believability-weighted voting (endnote 54, p192) | Aggregate forecast = believability-weighted mean of submissions by domain, rather than one-person-one-vote |
| Opinions should be costly, as in markets (p11, endnote 7) | Every submitted probability is scored on resolution; no unscored "opinions" |
| Everyone may ask questions and hold theories; only believable people hold opinions (24b, 194) | Anyone may submit scenarios/questions unscored; only scored forecasts earn credibility; display tier beside each forecast |
| State your confidence; suggestion vs conviction; the 51%→85% value (31b, 197c) | Forecasts must be numeric probabilities; interface discourages default 50–60% entries; calibration tracked |
| Many decisions build a track record; luck cancels with sample size (p38, endnote 38) | Show n per forecaster; require minimum resolved count before credibility is displayed; confidence intervals on scores |
| Level and steepness of trajectory, not the wiggles (106d); level matters more than rate (207) | Rank on current rolling score level with trend shown; don't reward recent improvement alone |
| Patterns not single events; 5–10 observations (109a, 155) | No promotions/demotions in tier on one resolved call; tier changes need multiple resolutions |
| Issues log: mandatory, rewarded when used, punished when skipped (19a, 144) | Mandatory post-mortem on every missed forecast; unresolved or unreviewed forecasts count against the forecaster |
| Blame/credit → accurate/inaccurate (14) | Scoring and review language is strictly accuracy; post-mortems are public and non-punitive but attributed |
| Name names, no anonymous "we" (15, 142) | Persistent attributed identities (pseudonymous allowed) on every forecast; no anonymous submissions in the scored pool |
| Record meetings and share; probing is transparent (6a, 99) | Immutable, timestamped public ledger of forecasts, rationales and all edits |
| Monday-morning quarterbacking (159) | Score against the information available at submission time; lock timestamps; no retroactive edits |
| Mental map vs what happened; diagnostic question script (150) | Structured scenario template (the "map") and structured post-mortem fields: proximate cause, root cause, which step failed |
| Possibilities must carry probabilities (203) | Scenario submissions are not accepted into the scored pool without a probability and resolution criterion |
| "By and large" true is true enough (204) | Resolution criteria define tolerance in advance; resolver applies by-and-large standard, logged |
| Triangulate with at least three believable people (195b) | Consensus displayed as weighted aggregate of top-tier forecasters, shown alongside the crowd |
| Debate is not a referendum; appeal to more-believable arbiters (28, 25, 36) | Resolution disputes go to a panel of high-believability forecasters, not a popular vote |
| Double-do: independent duplicates compared (184c) | Independent duplicate resolution by two resolvers; disagreement triggers review |
| Grader bias detected via average grades; forced ranking (144) | Monitor resolver and rationale-rater leniency; normalize ratings per rater |
| Auditors independent, procedures undisclosed (187a) | Resolvers independent of forecasters; resolution sampling/audit not gameable |
| Metrics must not be gamed (144) | Scoring rules that are proper and robust to question cherry-picking (e.g. coverage requirements, per-domain scores) |
| Expected value, risk of ruin, 15+ uncorrelated bets (197) | Portfolio view of a forecaster's calls; bounded scores so one blow-up doesn't dominate; diversity across domains |
| Everything is a case study; identify principles violated (121, 160) | Post-mortems tag which forecasting heuristic failed; a living "procedures manual" of lessons |
