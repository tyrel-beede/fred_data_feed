## (b) Definitions and the manageability test

| Term | Definition (paraphrase) | Page |
|---|---|---|
| Credit | The giving of buying power, granted in exchange for a promise to pay it back. | 9 |
| Debt | The promise to pay back that credit; it is one person's asset and another's liability, and a promise to pay in a specific currency. | 9, 15, 39 |
| Good vs bad credit growth | Whether fast credit/debt growth is good or bad depends on what the credit produces and how the debt is serviced; borrowed money must be used productively enough to generate the income that services it. Too little credit growth can be as costly as too much (foregone opportunities). | 9 |
| Debt cycle | Borrowing creates a cycle almost by definition: spending more than you make now requires spending less than you make later; a "logically-driven series of events that recur in patterns" (p.10). | 10 |
| Short-term debt cycle (business cycle) | Spending constrained only by lenders' and borrowers' willingness; credit availability controlled mainly by the central bank, which eases rates to restart the cycle. Produces bumps (mini-booms and recessions). | 13–14 |
| Long-term / big debt cycle | Many short-term cycles add up because each cyclical high and low has a higher debt-to-income ratio than the one before, until rate cuts can no longer sustain the expansion (the interest rate hits 0%); then deleveraging begins. More extreme than short-term cycles because debt burdens are higher and monetary policy is less effective. US went through one twice in the last century (1920s/1930s and 2000s/2008). | 13–14 |
| Debt crisis / why they happen | Debt and debt-service costs rise faster than the incomes needed to service them. Central banks can relieve typical crises by lowering real and nominal rates; severe ones (depressions) occur when that is no longer possible. | 13 |
| Depression (as used in the study) | A case where real GDP fell by more than 3% (the selection rule for the 48 cases). | 12, 13 |
| Deflationary depression | Policy makers cut rates; at ≈0% the lever stops working; restructuring and austerity dominate without enough stimulation (printing, currency depreciation); debt burdens rise because incomes fall faster than debt is restructured, paydowns shrink the debt stock and borrowers borrow to cover higher interest. Typical where the unsustainable debt was financed domestically in local currency — forced selling and defaults, not a currency/balance-of-payments problem. | 15 |
| Inflationary depression | Occurs in countries reliant on foreign capital with a lot of foreign-currency debt that cannot be monetized; when inflows slow, credit contracts, capital withdrawal dries up liquidity while currency declines produce inflation. Hardest to manage. | 15 |
| Bubble | Debts rise faster than incomes, producing self-reinforcing strong asset returns and growth, financed by leverage; unsustainable. | 16 |
| Top | When prices have been driven by leveraged buying and the market is fully long, leveraged and overpriced; usually triggered by central bank tightening. | 21 |
| Beautiful deleveraging | A reduction in debt/income ratios accompanied by acceptable inflation and growth: the four levers balanced so nominal growth exceeds the nominal interest rate while asset prices and activity improve. | 12, 32 |
| Ugly inflationary deleveraging | Abusive use of money printing/monetization and excessive devaluation (Weimar 1920s; Argentina and Brazil 1980s). | 32–33, 35 |
| Pushing on a string | Late in the long-term cycle, when lower rates and central-bank purchases of debt assets no longer convert into spending (phrase from the 1930s). | 35 |
| Solvency problem | Under accounting/regulatory rules the entity lacks enough equity capital to operate ("broke"). | 24 |
| Cash-flow problem | The entity lacks cash because its lenders are taking money away (a "run"); can occur with adequate capital if assets are illiquid. "the trigger and main issue of most debt crises" (p.24). | 24 |
| Shadow banking | Largely unregulated non-bank lending institutions that develop in the bubble. | 17 |
| Hyperinflation | Inflation so extreme that prices of goods and services at least double every year, accompanied by extreme wealth losses and severe hardship. | 58 |
| Classic warning sign of a coming bubble | An increasing amount of money is being borrowed to make debt-service payments. | 11 |
| Reversal condition | When the cost of debt service exceeds what can be borrowed to finance spending, the upward cycle reverses. | 11 |

**The manageability test (p.10).** Scale of a really bad debt loss for a whole economy: about 40% of a loan's value unrecoverable; such bad loans ≈20% of all loans; so losses ≈8% of total debt; total debt ≈200% of GDP; so the shortfall ≈16% of GDP. If "socialized" (borne via fiscal/monetary policy) and spread over 15 years it is ≈1% of GDP per year, which is tolerable; concentrated and not spread out, it is intolerable. Hence the downside of a large debt stock depends on policy makers' willingness and ability to spread the losses, which depends on two factors: (1) whether the debt is denominated in the currency they control, and (2) whether they have influence over how creditors and debtors behave with each other.

Related tests: (p.12) in almost every case where debts are in the country's own currency it is possible to manage the crisis well; the biggest risks are (a) policy makers failing to act (lack of knowledge/authority) and (b) political fallout of adjustments; a well-managed crisis is still very costly to some people. (p.12) Two long-term costs of a crisis: the losses from debt-service payments not being made (a 4% expected payment coming in at 2% or 0%; or a 50% loss if the debt is marked down), and the permanent reduction of lending and the spending it financed. (p.9) Subway example: a $1bn debt-financed system whose revenue is half of plan and whose debt is written down 50% is still worth building if it is worth $500m more than budgeted, i.e. about 2% a year more over a 25-year life. (p.21) No single metric — economy-wide debt/income or debt service/income — is an adequate crisis indicator; the debt-service capacity of individual entities and the concentration of debt (especially in key entities) matter more. (p.13) Aggregate debt data also omit pension and health-care liabilities, which are much larger.

Players and money (pp.14–15): money is both a medium of exchange and a store hold of wealth, serving "proletariat-workers" (sell time) and "capitalists-investors" (lend money for a debt instrument or equity); government sets the rules. When claims on money (debt) are too large relative to money, a deleveraging must occur; the central bank becomes buyer of last resort and the government a redistributor; the Year of Jubilee (debt wiped out every 50 years) is cited from the Old Testament (p.14).

## (c) THE DEFLATIONARY TEMPLATE (average of 21 cases; pp.16–38)

Template basis (p.13): 48 big debt cycles — all cases in large countries where real GDP fell >3%. Split into (1) little foreign-currency debt → deflationary depressions and (2) significant foreign-currency debt → inflationary depressions; about a 75% correlation between the share of foreign debt and the inflation experienced. Statistics average 21 deflationary and 27 inflationary cases from five years before the bottom of the depression to seven years after. Archetype charts are sensitive to outliers, so for each chart roughly the third of cases least related to the average were excluded (fn.3, p.16).

### Phase 1 — The Early Part of the Cycle (p.16; months −60…−48)
- Conditions: debt growth strong but not faster than incomes, because the debt finances activities that produce fast income growth (e.g., expanding a business, productivity); debt burdens low; balance sheets healthy; room for private sector, government and banks to lever up. Debt growth, growth and inflation "neither too hot nor too cold" — the "Goldilocks" period.
- Indicator readings (archetype charts): total debt ≈205–215% of GDP; debt service ≈49–52% of GDP (p.16, chart read); equities ≈55–62 indexed (p.18); nominal short rate ≈3.3% (p.20).
- Transition to the bubble: debts begin rising faster than incomes.

### Phase 2 — The Bubble (pp.16–21; months −48…−12)
- Defining conditions: debts rise faster than incomes and produce accelerating asset returns and growth; self-reinforcing because lenders size loans on (1) projected income/cash flows, (2) net worth/collateral, (3) their own capacity to lend — all of which rise together. Borrowers feel rich and spend more than they earn (p.16 example: $50,000 income, $50,000 net worth, $10,000/yr borrowing → $60,000/yr spending; when it reverses, spending must fall to $40,000 for as many years as $60,000 was spent, p.17).
- Up-wave mechanics (p.17): the up-wave can run for decades, with short-term debt cycles inside it; central banks progressively lower rates, which raises asset prices (present-value effect) and keeps debt-service burdens from rising — until debt service equals or exceeds what can be borrowed.
- Start of the bubble (p.17): over-extrapolation of a justified bull market (lower rates lift assets, profits, balance sheets); confidence and new buyers fuel it; implicit or explicit government guarantees encourage reckless lending; credit standards fall; banks lever up; shadow banking, new lending vehicles and financial engineering emerge. Asset-liability mismatches build: (a) borrow short to lend long, (b) liquid liabilities funding illiquid assets, (c) risky assets bought with borrowed money, (d) borrowing in one currency to lend in another. Debts rise fast and debt service rises even faster.
- Psychology (p.18): consensus is priced in and believed; humans move in crowds and overweight recent experience, so extrapolation occurs.
- Headline numbers: "the typical bubble sees leveraging up at an average rate of 20 to 25 percent of GDP over three years or so" (p.18). "total debt-to-income levels averaged around 300 percent of GDP" in the cases studied (p.19). Bubbles most likely at the tops of the business cycle, balance-of-payments cycle and/or long-term debt cycle (p.19).
- Table "Conditions During the Bubble" (p.19) — change during bubble (range): 1. Debt growing faster than incomes 40% (14% to 79%); Debt growing rapidly 32% (17% to 45%); Income growth high but slower than debt 13% (8% to 20%). 2. Equity markets extend rally 48% (22% to 68%). 3. Yield curve flattens (SR − LR) 1.4% (0.9% to 1.7%).
- Monetary policy (pp.19–20): monetary policy usually helps inflate the bubble rather than constrain it, especially when inflation and growth are good and returns great (Japan late 1980s; much of the world late 1920s and mid-2000s). Central banks target inflation (or inflation and growth), not debt growth; Dalio argues they should target debt growth at a sustainable level — one where income growth can service the debts regardless of what the credit buys. Central banks do tighten somewhat and short rates rise, but they fall behind the curve; borrowers are not yet squeezed; interest is increasingly paid by borrowing more (an unsustainable-trend signal). The Taylor Rule ignores the debt-service perspective (fn.4). Bubble assets vary: 2008 US = residential and commercial real estate, private equity, lower-grade credit and to a lesser extent listed equities; US Great Depression and Japan = stocks and real estate (fn.5, p.20).
- Market behavior: equities rise from ≈62 to ≈100 indexed over the bubble band (p.18, chart read); short rate drifts from ≈3.5% to ≈4.5% with "not much tightening until later in the bubble" (p.20); yield curve ratio rises from ≈0.7 toward 1.0 (p.22).
- Transition to the top: central bank tightening/rising rates (or an external liquidity tightening) begins to squeeze debt service.

**Bubble-identification checklist (p.20), paraphrased** — the seven measurable characteristics he lists:
1. valuation: prices sit high against the usual yardsticks (earnings, rents, incomes)
2. extrapolation: prices already assume further rapid gains from those high levels
3. sentiment: bullishness is broad, not confined to specialists
4. leverage: purchases are financed with a lot of borrowed money
5. forward commitments: buyers have locked in unusually far ahead - stockpiles, supply contracts, pre-orders - to speculate or to hedge against higher prices
6. new entrants: people who were never in the market before are now buying
7. policy: easy money is still feeding the bubble, and tightening would be what pops it

Method (p.20): look at all the big markets, see which are in bubbles, then look at what is connected to them that would be hit when they pop.

### Phase 3 — The Top (pp.21–23; months −12…0)
- Defining conditions: market fully long, leveraged and overpriced; "When things are so good that they can't get better—yet everyone believes that they will get better—tops of markets are being made" (p.21).
- Triggers (p.21): most often the central bank starts tightening and rates rise — sometimes because the bubble itself lifts growth/inflation against capacity constraints, sometimes externally driven (a country reliant on external creditors sees lending pulled; tightening in the currency the debts are denominated in, e.g., reserve-currency tightening or a foreign financial crisis; a rise in the debt's currency relative to the income currency). Unanticipated cash-flow shortfalls can also trigger it.
- Timing indicators: credit/debt problems emerge about half a year before the peak in the economy, first in the most vulnerable, frothy pockets; riskiest debtors miss payments, credit spreads tick up, risky lending slows, runs from risky to safe assets begin (p.21). The fastest rate of tightening comes about five months before the top of the stock market; short rates peak just a few months before the stock-market top (p.22). Early in the top, rising short rates narrow or eliminate the spread to long rates; a flat or inverted curve makes cash attractive just before the bubble pops (pp.21–22). Economy runs hot: demand against capacity, unemployment at cyclical lows, inflation rising (p.22).
- Mechanism: debt-service squeeze hurts asset prices (negative wealth effect, fn.6: less lending and spending from worry and less collateral); borrowers' new borrowing goes to debt service and/or isn't rolled; spending slows (p.21). Rising short rates raise the discount rate on assets and make credit purchases dearer (p.22).
- Severity rule (p.22): the more leverage and the higher the prices, the less tightening it takes to prick the bubble and the bigger the bust; the size of the downturn depends less on the size of the tightening than on each sector's sensitivity and how losses cascade — look sector by sector and at the big players, not economy-wide averages. Immediately post-bubble the wealth effect matters more than monetary policy; people underestimate it and misjudge the early decline as a buying opportunity because earnings have not yet fallen (pp.22–23).
- Market behavior: equities peak ≈108 indexed around month −6 (p.18/22 chart read); yield curve (SR−LR) peaks ≈+0.5% ("Tight") around month −8 (p.22); nominal short rate peaks ≈5% around month −6 (pp.20, 24).
- Transition to depression: rates are cut but the floor (≈0%) is hit; defaults begin.

### Phase 4 — The "Depression" (pp.23–32; months 0…36)
- Defining condition (p.23): in a normal recession, rate cuts fix the money/debt-service imbalance via (1) a positive wealth effect, (2) stimulating activity, (3) easing debt service; in a depression this cannot happen because rates are already at or near 0% (or, with heavy currency outflows, the floor is higher for credit/currency-risk reasons). Happened in the early stages of 1930–32 and 2008–09. In 2007–08 the Fed cut quickly then moved to alternative stimulus; in the 1930s it eased slowly and even tightened to defend the dollar's gold peg.
- Dynamics (pp.24–26): defaults and restructurings hit leveraged lenders (banks) "like an avalanche"; runs on institutions without government protection; rate cuts don't help because floors are hit and credit spreads rise; defaults and austerity (deflationary forces) dominate and are not balanced by monetization. Liquidity becomes the central concern (a $100,000 debt instrument assumed convertible to $100,000 of goods). The depression is driven by supply and demand for credit, money and goods — not primarily psychology (p.25): most of what people think is money is credit, which appears in good times and simply disappears in bad times. Self-reinforcing loop: debtors' incomes/borrowing insufficient → assets sold and spending cut → asset values and collateral fall → incomes fall → creditworthiness (judged by assets/collateral vs debts and incomes vs debt service) falls faster than debts → lenders pull back (p.25). Mark-to-market accounting made 2008 banks "insolvent" where 1980s loan-book banks were not (p.25). Pre-existing protections (deposit insurance, lender of last resort, capital injections/nationalization) are helpful but rarely adequate because lending happened in shadow banking or new instruments (p.25).
- Write-down arithmetic (p.26): a write-down must be large enough for the debtor to service the restructured loan; a 30% write-down cuts a creditor's assets by 30%, a creditor levered 2:1 loses 60% of net worth (fn.7: $100 assets/$50 debt → $70 assets → $20 net worth); banks are typically levered about 12:1 or 15:1, so it is devastating. Debt burdens rise even as debts are written down because spending and incomes fall; household debt/net worth spikes (chart p.26).
- Typical readings: equity declines "typically around 50 percent" (p.26); capitalists-investors face falling portfolios, falling incomes and higher taxes, become defensive, move money abroad (weakening the currency), dodge taxes and seek liquid safe assets (government bonds, gold, cash) (p.26). Nominal short rate falls from ≈5% to ≈1.2% ("Easy") by month ≈18 (p.24); yield curve to ≈−3% ("Easy") (p.22); equities trough ≈50 indexed near month 30 (p.23); total debt/GDP keeps rising from ≈265% to ≈300% through the band (p.16, chart read); real FX vs trade partners falls from ≈+1% to ≈0% (p.33); gold in local currency rises from ≈75 to ≈150 indexed (p.34).
- Policy response — the four levers in the depression (pp.26–32): (1) austerity, (2) debt defaults/restructurings, (3) debt monetization/money printing, (4) wealth transfers from haves to have-nots. Key is to get the mix right so deflationary forces are balanced by inflationary ones (p.27). Policy makers typically get the mix wrong initially (taxpayer anger, moral hazard), are slow, and the longer they wait the uglier the deleveraging; once they guarantee, print and monetize enough, the depression is short (US 2008) rather than prolonged (1930s Great Depression; Japan's "lost decade") (p.27).
  - Austerity (p.27): the obvious first try; even deep austerity does not rebalance debt and income because cutting spending cuts incomes; revenues fall and demands on government rise so deficits widen; governments raise taxes. "Both moves are big mistakes" (p.27).
  - "Printing money" to stop the bleeding (pp.27–29): runs force the government/central bank to decide which depositors/lenders and institutions to protect; guarantees and nationalizations; money comes from the budget and from printing; central banks inevitably choose to print rather than compete with the private sector for scarce money. Moves come in progressively larger doses; early inadequate moves produce bear-market rallies — the Great Depression had six big rallies of 16% to 48% inside a bear market that fell 89% in total, each triggered by government action (p.28). When risks of systemic failure emerge, act immediately to: curtail panic and guarantee liabilities (deposit/debt guarantees, injections, occasionally deposit freezes); provide liquidity (lend against a widening range of collateral and to a wider range of institutions); support solvency of systemically important institutions (mergers, regulatory pushes to raise capital, accounting adjustments); recapitalize/nationalize/cover losses of systemically important institutions (the "shipping port" analogy) (pp.28–29).
  - Debt defaults/restructurings (pp.29–31): cleansing bad debts is critical. Best-managed cases: (a) swiftly recognize the magnitude; (b) don't save every expendable institution, balancing failure risks against knock-on effects; (c) create or restore robust credit pipes; (d) keep growth and inflation acceptable while bad debts are worked out. Policy makers usually enact insufficient one-off policies and act decisively only after "a couple of years" of needless pain; speed and aggressiveness of response are among the most important determinants of severity and length (p.29). Nonsystemic institutions absorb losses or fail — merged with healthy institutions in about 80% of cases, otherwise liquidated or moved to an asset-management company (AMC) (p.29). Creditor hierarchy: small depositors protected in nearly every case (deposit insurance expanded); depositors took losses in around 30% of cases, often on foreign-exchange deposits converted at below-market rates; equity, subordinated debt and large depositors absorb losses in most cases regardless of systemic importance; senior/sub-debt protection and dilutive recapitalizations mainly in the developed world; domestic creditors sometimes prioritized over foreign private creditors, but IMF/BIS loans are prioritized (pp.29–30). Bad assets go to an AMC in about 40% of cases and stay on the original lender's balance sheet in about 60%; disposal levers: restructuring (extended terms), debt-for-equity swaps and seizures, direct sales, securitizations; AMCs are publicly owned, mandated to sell within a targeted period (e.g., 10 years), financed by government or pseudo-government debt (p.30). Systemically important borrowers are kept intact via restructuring (debt-for-equity swaps, debt reduction, lower rates, terming out, new lending); nonsystemic borrowers restructure privately or go bankrupt; governments and AMCs help household debtors (pp.30–31). Regulatory reforms follow (deposit guarantees 1930s; Dodd-Frank and Volcker rule 2010; labor-market, credit-standard, competition, capital-requirement changes), for better or worse depending on politics (p.30).
  - Redistributing wealth (pp.31–32): wealth gaps widen in bubbles and become galling in hard times; rich and poor sharing a budget in a downturn produces conflict and populism on left and right; net worth of the top 0.1% ≈ bottom 90% combined both in the 1930s and today (p.31). Taxing the rich (especially finance) becomes attractive; central-bank asset purchases benefit the rich; leftward shifts hasten redistribution; the rich move money, causing "hollowing out" of high-tax areas. Taxation rises mainly via income, property and consumption taxes; wealth/inheritance taxes raise little (illiquid wealth; unconstitutional in the US, fn.9). Transfers rarely contribute meaningfully to a deleveraging unless there are "revolutions" (p.32).
- Table "Frequency of Levers Used to Manage Debt Problems (% of Cases)" (p.31; 48 cases): Liquidity support — Emergency Lending/Liquidity 88%; Bank Liability Guarantee 58%; Bank Holiday/Deposit Freeze 21%. Address insolvent lenders — Bank Restructuring/Mergers 81%; Recapitalization 73%; Nationalizations 60%; Losses Imposed on Depositors 29%. Dispose of bad debts — Through Asset Purchases and Transfers 44%; Through Centralized Asset Management Co's 38%. Sovereign Default/Restructuring 35%. IMF Program 52%.
- Transition to beautiful deleveraging: large-scale printing/monetization, guarantees and currency devaluation that outweigh the deflationary forces (typically two to three years in — see Phase 5).

### Phase 5 — The "Beautiful Deleveraging" (pp.32–35; months 36…≈78)
- Definition (p.32): the four levers moved in balance to reduce intolerable shocks and produce positive growth with falling debt burdens and acceptable inflation — enough printing/monetization and devaluation to offset austerity/defaults and lift nominal growth above the nominal interest rate, but not so much that inflation accelerates, the currency is devalued and a new bubble forms.
- Mechanism (p.32): when spending can't be financed with debt, it must come from more money; the central bank lends against a wider range (lower quality, longer maturity) of collateral and buys (monetizes) lower-quality/longer-term debt. Right amounts: (a) neutralize what would otherwise be a deflationary credit-market collapse, (b) get nominal growth marginally above the nominal rate to spread the deleveraging tolerably. Arithmetic: debt/income 100%, rate 2%, income growth 1% → burden goes from 100/100 to 102/101; nominal income growth must exceed nominal rates. Printing is not inflationary if it merely offsets credit destruction ("it's been repeatedly proven out in history", p.32) — money growing fast while credit and activity contract, which traditional economists read as falling velocity. Abuse produces an ugly inflationary deleveraging (Weimar 1920s; Argentina and Brazil 1980s) — "The key is to avoid printing too much money" (p.33); harder with foreign-currency debt held by foreigners.
- Inevitability (pp.33–34): printing/monetization and guarantees are inevitable where rate cuts don't work, though of little value where printing is constrained; all deleveragings studied eventually involved big waves of money creation, fiscal deficits and devaluations (against gold, commodities and stocks). Money printing comes in two classic waves: liquidity to stressed institutions, then large-scale asset purchases (p.33). Gold/commodity/FX-pegged systems are forced to run tighter policy until the pain makes them break the link (Great Depression: suspending dollar-gold convertibility created the bottoms; gold rose overnight when Roosevelt broke the peg; 2008 was easier) (p.34). "In the end, policy makers always print" (p.34): austerity causes more pain than benefit, big restructurings wipe out too much wealth too fast, transfers need revolutions. Those who print quickly and well (US 2008–09) do much better than those who do it late (US 1930–33).
- Typical magnitudes (p.34): money printing around 4% of GDP per year; a large initial currency devaluation of around 50% against gold; deficits widen to about 6% of GDP; the aggressive stimulation comes two to three years into the depression, after stocks have fallen more than 50%, economic activity has fallen about 10%, and unemployment has risen to around 10–15% — with a lot of variation; faster and smarter rollouts beat these averages.
- Table "Policy Responses" (p.34) — Avg (Range): 1. Length of contraction (months) 55 (22 to 79); 2. Size of FX decline vs. Gold −44% (−58% to −37%); 3. Peak Money Creation (%GDP, ann) 4% (1% to 9%); 4. Peak Fiscal Deficit −6% (−14% to −1%).
- Market behavior: equities recover from ≈55 to ≈70 indexed over the band (p.18); nominal short rate drifts ≈1% → 0.5% (p.20); real FX falls to ≈−4% vs trade partners (p.33); gold peaks ≈195 indexed near month 54 then eases to ≈160 (p.34); money (%PGDP) rises to ≈5% by month ≈55 (p.33); change in debt/GDP turns negative after month ≈60 (p.19).
- Transition: as MP1 and MP2 lose traction the cycle enters "pushing on a string"; recovery proceeds to normalization.

**Well-managed vs poorly managed, by phase (table p.35, paraphrased):**
- Bubble — well: central banks consider debt growth and its asset-market effects; use macroprudential policy to restrain debt growth where bubbles form and allow it elsewhere; fiscal policy tightened. Poorly: speculators and lenders over-extrapolate; central banks focus only on inflation/growth, ignore investment-asset bubbles, keep credit cheap too long.
- Top — well: constrict the bubble with broad monetary policy or well-chosen macroprudential policy, then ease selectively. Poorly: keep tightening well after the bubble has burst.
- Depression — well: ample liquidity, rates cut quickly to 0%, then aggressive monetization with targeted macroprudential policy; aggressive, sustained fiscal stimulus easing past the turn; systemically important institutions protected. Poorly: slower cuts, limited liquidity, early tightening, delayed monetization; austerity without easing; systemically important institutions left damaged or failed.
- Beautiful deleveraging — well: reflation begins with aggressive monetization (asset purchases or big currency declines) sufficient to lift nominal growth above nominal rates; stimulative macroprudential policy protects systemic entities and stimulates high-quality credit; nonsystemic institutions allowed to fail in an orderly way; defaults/austerity balanced against monetization, currency decline and fiscal stimulus. Poorly: monetization stutters; purchases muted and cash-like (no wealth effect); fiscal austerity undermines the central bank; over-indebted nonsystemic entities protected (zombie banks, malaise); faith in the currency allowed to collapse with too much printing → ugly inflationary depression.

### Phase 6 — "Pushing on a String" (pp.35–38)
- Condition: late in the long-term debt cycle, lower rates and central-bank debt purchases have diminished effect; low growth and low asset returns; central banks must get money and credit more directly to spenders. Risk: too much printing/devaluation relative to deflationary alternatives → ugly inflationary deleveraging (p.35). Occurs in the later years of the long-term cycle (e.g., 1937–38 and "now" in the US) when rates and risk premiums are both low — little "gas in the tank" (p.37).
- The three monetary-policy styles:
  - **Monetary Policy 1 (MP1)** — interest-rate driven; most effective and broadest: (a) positive wealth effect via present values, (b) cheaper credit purchases (durables, housing), (c) lower debt-service burdens. First approach in a debt crisis; stops working when short rates hit ≈0% (p.36).
  - **Monetary Policy 2 (MP2)** — "quantitative easing": printing money to buy financial assets, typically debt. Works on investors/savers (they re-deploy cash into other assets), not borrowers/spenders; benefits asset owners and widens the wealth gap. Most effective when risk and liquidity premiums are large (pushes them down, lifts risky assets, wealth effect); loses effectiveness as premiums are compressed and prices rise — cash can start to look appealing versus overpriced assets; if private credit growth doesn't pick up, policy makers are pushing on a string. Larger monetizations then risk doubts about the currency as a store hold of value and a shift to alternatives like gold; monetization swaps one IOU (debt) for another (printed money) — a Ponzi-like worry if goods and services can't back all the IOUs (p.36).
  - **Monetary Policy 3 (MP3)** — puts money more directly into spenders' hands and incentivizes spending; more productive when the wealth gap is large and the economy weak (p.37). A continuum from coordinated fiscal-monetary action to central-bank "helicopter money", with macroprudential policy as a parallel lever. Forms, with historical references (p.37): (1) debt-financed fiscal spending, sometimes paired with QE buying most of the issuance (Japan 1930s; US WWII; US and UK 2000s); (2) debt-financed fiscal spending where the Treasury isn't on the hook — central bank prints to cover debt payments (Germany 1930s) or lends to non-government entities for stimulus (development banks in China 2008); (3) no debt issuance — newly printed money given directly to government to spend, via printing fiat currency (Imperial China, American Revolution, US Civil War, Germany 1930s, UK WWI) or debasing hard currency (Ancient Rome, Imperial China, 16th-century England); (4) printing plus direct cash transfers to households ("helicopter money"; US veterans' bonuses in the Great Depression; Imperial China) — variants: equal or targeted amounts; one-off or ongoing (universal basic income); use-it-or-lose-it within a year; directed to retirement/education/small-business accounts; or distributing QE returns to households; (5) a big debt write-down with big money creation (the "year of Jubilee": Ancient Rome, Great Depression, Iceland). Dalio's view (p.38): the most effective approaches involve fiscal/monetary coordination so that both provision and spending of money occur; helicopter money alone is less adequate than money with spending incentives; categories overlap (a tax break is probably not helicopter money depending on financing; government can spend directly without central-bank financing). Macroprudential policies (regulatory control of credit cost/availability in targeted parts of the system) are valuable in all seven stages; covered in the Appendix (p.38).

### Phase 7 — Normalization (p.38)
- Recovery is slow even in a beautiful deleveraging: "It typically takes roughly 5 to 10 years (hence the term 'lost decade') for real economic activity to reach its former peak level" (p.38); stock prices take longer, around a decade, because equity risk premiums stay high.
- Table "Recovery Conditions" (p.38) — Avg (Range): 1. Length of equity drawdown (months) 119 (60 to 249); 2. Length of GDP drawdown (months) 72 (25 to 106); 3. Change in debt-to-GDP post-stimulation −54% (−70% to −29%).
- Market behavior: equities ≈80 indexed at month 84 (still below the ≈108 peak) (p.18); short rate ≈0.3% (p.20); total debt/GDP ≈270% at month 84 vs ≈305% peak (p.16, chart read); debt-to-GDP ratio falling ≈10%/yr by month 84 (p.19).

### The four levers of deleveraging and the balance that makes it "beautiful" (pp.12, 26–27, 32–35)
1. Austerity (spending less) — deflationary.
2. Debt defaults/restructurings — deflationary.
3. Central bank "printing money" and buying assets (or providing guarantees)/debt monetization — inflationary, stimulative.
4. Transfers of money and credit from those who have more than they need to those who have less (wealth redistribution).
The levers shift who bears the burden and over what time; the balance is "beautiful" when deflationary forces (1, 2) are offset by (3) and currency devaluation just enough to put nominal income growth above the nominal interest rate and let debt/income ratios fall while activity and asset prices improve — without triggering a run on debt assets, accelerating inflation or a new bubble.

## (d) THE INFLATIONARY TEMPLATE (average of the 27 worst inflationary cases; pp.39–60)

### Framing (pp.39–40)
Ten reminders (p.39): currency and debt serve as mediums of exchange and store holds of wealth; debt is one person's asset and another's liability; debt is a promise to pay a specific currency; debt holders care about inflation vs the interest rate they earn; central banks can only produce the money/credit they control; over time central banks and free-market borrowers/lenders create ever-bigger piles of debt assets and liabilities; the bigger the pile the harder to balance between deflationary and inflationary depressions; policy makers can usually balance because they have power to redistribute burdens; central banks relieve crises by printing the currency the debt is in, which cheapens it; and the currency rule — if a currency falls faster than its interest rate, holders of debt in it lose money, and expected continued weakness without compensating rates creates a dangerous currency dynamic.

Currency dynamic (pp.39–40): that dynamic produces inflationary depressions: holders of weak-currency debt sell and move to another currency or a non-currency store (gold); in a debt crisis the central bank cannot raise rates enough to compensate; money leaves; the central bank must let credit markets tighten or print (which produces a lot of currency weakness). Inflation/growth trade-offs are easier with inflows and harder with outflows; when money is flowing out, real interest rates need to rise less if real exchange rates fall more (and vice versa). Capital outflows weaken the currency a lot; those who borrowed the strong currency to fund weak-currency activities see debt costs soar. Countries with the worst debt problems, a lot of foreign-currency debt and high dependence on foreign capital have significant currency weakness; "The currency weakness is what causes inflation when there is a depression" (p.40). The squeeze ends when (a) debts are defaulted on and/or enough money is created, (b) debt-service requirements are reduced (forbearance), and/or (c) the currency depreciates much more than inflation rises so assets and exports become competitive and the balance of payments improves; politics can throw productivity into a long downward spiral (p.40).

**Conditions that make a country prone to severe inflationary deleveraging/hyperinflation (p.40):** far more likely where the country (1) does not have a reserve currency (no global bias to hold it as a store hold of wealth); (2) has low foreign-exchange reserves (small cushion against outflows); (3) has a large foreign debt (cost rises with rates or currency moves; shortage of dollar credit); (4) has a large and increasing budget and/or current-account deficit (must borrow or print); (5) has negative real interest rates (lenders not compensated); (6) has a history of high inflation and negative total returns in the currency (lack of trust). The more of these, the worse. Weimar is the iconic case. Reserve-currency countries without significant foreign-currency debt can still have inflationary depressions, but they emerge more slowly and later, after sustained and repeated overuse of stimulation to reverse a deflationary deleveraging; permitting much higher inflation to keep growth up by printing erodes reserve status.

Structure (p.41): inflationary deleveragings follow five stages that mirror the deflationary ones and transpire much the same up to the fourth stage (the depression); archetype = average of 27 inflationary deleveragings with a lot of foreign-currency debt, then compared with four hyperinflationary cases.

### Phase 1 — The Early Part of the Cycle (pp.41–42; months −60…−48)
- Conditions: favorable capital flows from good fundamentals (competitiveness, productive investment opportunities); low debt, healthy balance sheets; exports attract foreign capital that funds productive investment; debt and incomes rise at comparable rates; debt and equity markets strong; private sector, government and banks borrow sensibly because incomes rise fast. Capital flows (within and among countries) are "the most important flows to watch because they are the most volatile" (p.41).
- Currency: a cheap currency plus favorable balance of payments means demand for the currency exceeds supply, so the central bank gets more growth per unit of inflation and can appreciate the currency, lower rates and/or accumulate reserves. Central banks often sell their currency for the inflows and park it in foreign bonds ("foreign-exchange reserves" — savings that cushion currency swings or buy strategic assets); accumulating reserves is stimulative (limits appreciation, adds domestic money). Currency total return attractive (either foreigners buy it, or the central bank sells it and lifts local-currency asset prices).
- Indicator readings: total debt ≈125% of GDP; current account ≈−3% of GDP; GDP gap ≈+1%; real FX ≈0% vs TWI; capital inflows ≈4–6% of GDP; short rate ≈25–27%; reserves ≈65–75 indexed (pp.43–48, chart read).

### Phase 2 — The Bubble (pp.42–45; months −48…−12)
- Conditions: self-reinforcing virtuous cycle of strong inflows, good returns and strong conditions; currency and asset prices bid up and increasingly debt-financed; debts rise rapidly relative to incomes; strong exchange rate, rising reserves, booming economy; foreigners' total return (local asset return + currency) attractive; the country becomes "the hot place to invest"; overbought; investors who were never involved rush in; market becomes fully long, leveraged and overpriced (p.42). Entities build structurally long currency positions (foreign subsidiaries hold local-currency deposits unhedged; locals borrow in the cheaper foreign currency because foreign bankers are eager) (p.42). Productive investments become scarce as more capital chases them; the rising currency erodes competitiveness; growth increasingly debt- rather than productivity-financed; heavy reliance on foreign financing shows up as rising foreign-currency debt (because the local financial system is undeveloped, local-currency lending is distrusted and domestic savings are small) (p.43).
- Typical developments (p.42): foreign capital inflows high (around 10% of GDP on average); central bank accumulating FX reserves; real FX bid up to around 15% overvalued on a PPP basis; stocks rally by over 20% a year on average for several years into their peak; foreign capital finances a consumption boom; imports rise faster than exports and the current account worsens; high export (commodity) prices lift income and investment.
- Typical developments (p.43): debt burdens rise fast — debt/GDP rises at an annual rate of about 10% over three years; foreign-currency debt rises to around 35% of total debt and around 45% of GDP; the GDP gap is very strong, around +4%, with tight capacity. Over the bubble, debt/GDP rises from around 125% to about 150% and the current account deteriorates by about 2% of GDP (p.43). The income–spending gap widens; growth depends on ever-larger inflows; the country becomes fragile and a minor event can trigger reversal (p.44).

- Market/indicator behavior: real FX rises to ≈+17% (p.45); capital inflows to ≈11% of GDP (p.46); current account to ≈−6% (p.44); GDP gap to ≈+5% (p.44); equities ≈75 → ≈120 indexed (p.52); reserves ≈85 → ≈112 indexed (p.48); short rate drifts down ≈22% → ≈15% (p.48).
- Transition to the top: inflows slow (naturally or via a shock) and the currency/asset-price/debt-growth combination becomes unsustainable.

### Phase 3 — The Top and Currency Defense (pp.45–48; months −12…0, with the defense phase lasting ≈6 months around the top)
- Defining condition: the flows that drove the bubble, the high currency, high asset prices and high debt growth become unsustainable; a mirror-image spiral begins — weaker inflows, weaker asset prices, worse conditions, weaker flows — "a balance of payments crisis and an inflationary depression" (p.45). Because optimism is priced in, even a minor event can trigger a slowing of inflows and a rise in domestic outflows; adverse capital-flow shifts matter more than trade balances (p.45).
- Causes of the top-reversal (p.46): (1) income from selling to foreigners drops (currency too strong; commodity price fall); (2) import costs or borrowing costs rise; (3) inflows decline because (a) the unsustainable pace slows, (b) worries about economic/political conditions, (c) monetary tightening in the local currency and/or the debt currency (or tightening abroad pulling capital home); (4) citizens/companies want to get their money out. Weakening inflows are "often the first shoe to drop"; they cut the investment and consumption they financed, borrowers look less creditworthy, foreigners lend less — self-reinforcing.
- Typical developments (p.46): growth slows relative to potential as inflows slow; domestic outflows pick up a bit; export earnings fall (prices or quantities) — exports typically flat.
- Currency-defense dynamics (pp.47–48): asset/currency holders fear capital restrictions and rush out; policy makers fear a currency collapse; the central bank's trade-off worsens (less growth per unit of inflation). Central banks typically defend by (a) spending reserves and/or (b) raising rates; these defenses and managed declines typically don't work because they give sellers a better exit without moving the currency/rates to sustainable levels. Interest-parity math: an expected 5% annual decline requires 5% higher rates; a 5% expected monthly decline requires about 80% annualized (fn.10: 80% rather than 60% because of compounding) — intolerable in a weak economy; even a 5–10%/yr expected depreciation implies an intolerable 5–10% rate premium. A managed decline with falling reserves makes the market expect further depreciation, raising domestic rates (a tightening in a weak economy), encouraging withdrawals and speculation, widening the BoP gap and forcing more reserve sales or abandonment of the planned path; a reserve-based defense must stop because no sensible policy maker runs out of "savings". Policy makers (especially peg defenders) make boldly confident vows just before the next stage — letting the currency go (pp.47–48). The forward price falls ahead of spot (the interest differential props up spot; "whip-like" effect); after the currency is let go the spot catches up and the forward rallies relative to spot as the differential narrows (p.48). Capital controls are a third, often last-ditch lever that seldom works: investors find ways around them and the attempt to trap capital makes people want to escape (bank-run analogy); at best a temporary fix (p.48).
- Duration/magnitude: the defense phase is usually brief — in the vicinity of six months — with reserves drawn down about 10 to 20 percent before the defense is abandoned (p.48).
- Market behavior: short rate turns up from ≈15% to ≈20% at month 0 on its way to ≈35% (p.48); reserves fall from ≈112 to ≈105 indexed (p.48); real FX ≈+15% at month 0 (p.45); equities ≈120 → ≈110 (p.52); capital inflows roll over ≈11% → ≈9% (p.46).
- Transition: the currency is let go.

### Phase 4 — The Depression (Often When the Currency Is Let Go) (pp.49–53; months 0…36)
- Mechanism (p.49): unlike a family, a country can change the amount and value of its currency; a 30% devaluation is a 30% pay cut only relative to the rest of the world, so currency declines offer price cuts to the world without domestic deflation. After unsustainable support (reserves, tightening, strong no-devaluation assurances, sometimes FX controls), policy makers stop fighting and let the currency fall (usually trying to smooth it).
- After the currency is let go (p.49): a big initial depreciation, on average around 30% in real terms; the decline is not offset by tighter short rates, so losses from holding the currency are around 30% in the first year; policy makers try to smooth the fall and keep spending reserves (on average another 10% for a year into the bust).
- Devaluation advice (pp.50–51): central banks should not defend to the point of dangerously low reserves or dangerously high rates; devaluations are stimulative (assets rise in the weakened currency, exports and margins improve, imports fall, BoP adjusts). But they are double-edged: imported-goods prices rise sharply → inflation; a gradual, persistent decline breeds expectations of more decline, capital withdrawal, speculation and persistent inflation psychology. Better: a large one-off devaluation to a level with a two-way market (no broad expectation of further weakness), ideally a surprise so reserves/rates need not be spent beforehand — which is why policy makers say they will defend right up to the moment they stop (p.51). After the move, people push to exit; asset-liability mismatches that were profitable become suddenly unprofitable, making the reversal self-sustaining; when capital is gone, spending stops, growth halts, lenders (especially domestic banks) hit debt problems, foreigners lend even less (p.51).
- Typical readings — capital (p.51): inflows dry up, falling fast — by more than 5% of GDP in less than 12 months; outflows continue at a pace of −3 to −5% of GDP. Printing is limited because it risks enabling more exit (p.52).
- Typical readings — rates, printing, equities (p.52): nominal short rates rise by about 20 percentage points and the yield curve inverts; printing is limited (1 to 2% of GDP on average); equities fall in local-currency terms by around 50% on average, and worse in foreign-currency terms.
- Typical readings — debt (p.52): foreign-currency debt is the key mismatch; debtors sell local currency, hedge and move savings abroad, adding downward pressure. Debt service rises further (by more than 5% of GDP on average) because incomes fall and FX debt service rises in local terms; FX debt burdens rise — debt/GDP rises by about 20% on average from the fall in incomes and the currency.
- Typical readings — inflation (p.53): inflation rises (typically by 15%, peaking around 30%) and stays elevated for about two years from the top.
- Bottom/catharsis (p.53): everything looks terrible; debt, economic, political and currency problems reinforce each other; hidden fraud and corruption surface; foreigners stay away and domestics flee. "Hitting bottom" is the mirror of the bubble — aggressive exit, shortage of buyers; the most severe and painful part, self-reinforcing and rapid; the pain forces a radical change in pricing and policies ("catharsis"). The cheap currency finally cuts imports enough to restore the BoP, often with IMF/BIS/multilateral aid and big political shifts to sounder policies.
- Typical readings — activity (p.53): the GDP gap falls a lot (by about 8% on average); unemployment rises; the bottom in activity comes after about one year with the GDP-gap trough near −4%.
- Market behavior (charts): real FX trough ≈−18% around month 15 (p.49); foreign FX return trough ≈62 indexed around month 14 (p.50); short rate peak ≈35% around month 12–14 (p.48); reserves trough ≈82 indexed around month 10 (p.48/50); equities trough ≈50 indexed around month 22 (p.52); FX debt/GDP peak ≈72% around month 18 (p.52); core inflation peak ≈35% around month 18 (p.53); growth vs potential trough ≈−9% around month 14 (p.47/54); imports trough ≈−8% around month 14–16 (p.56); exports trough ≈−3.5% around month 18 (p.57); total debt/GDP peak ≈183% around month 12 (p.43); capital outflows trough ≈−5% around month 6–8 (p.51).
- Transition to normalization: supply and demand for the currency rebalance — mainly via capital flows (the central bank makes the currency desirable to hold), secondarily via lower spending and imports.

### Phase 5 — Normalization (pp.54–57; months 36/48…84)
- Condition (p.54): balance between supply and demand for the currency relative to others; mostly a capital-flow phenomenon. Policy makers keep capital in by producing a positive total return on the currency at an acceptable (not-too-high) interest rate; contrary to the instinct to defend, the currency level that is good for trade, yields a positive total return and allows an appropriate domestic rate is a low one.
- Policy rules (p.55): let the currency depreciate sharply and quickly so that total return (spot appreciation + rate differential) is likely positive at a low real rate; BoP fundamentals and the central bank's willingness to limit printing and depreciation determine whether that total return is positive; "Devaluing currencies is like using cocaine" (p.55) — short-term stimulus, ruinous when abused; watch what central banks do before going long; if investors are burned too long while the currency keeps falling, that is often the break-point into an inflationary spiral. Central-bank objective: a currency cheap enough to stimulate the economy and BoP, with policy tight enough to make owning the currency attractive. Returns to foreign holders start negative then rally about a year after the devaluation. Systemically important institutions still need targeted recapitalization/liquidity (typically by printing), carefully balanced because it is inflationary. Fn.11: domestic savers weigh inflation vs rates; foreign lenders weigh currency change vs rates, so policy makers stabilizing the BoP prioritize a positive expected return in the currency over inflation.
- Typical readings at the bottom (p.56): the import collapse improves the current account a lot (by about 8% of GDP on average); inflows stop declining and stabilize; capital flight abates; the country frequently turns to the IMF or other international entities; short rates start down after about a year (long rates stay elevated) and return to pre-crisis levels in around two years; the forward currency rallies vs spot as rates fall; inflation comes down as the currency stabilizes — nearly two years after the bottom to reach pre-crisis levels. Through the crisis, imports contract by around 10% and equities fall by over 50%; the current account goes from a deficit of −6% of GDP to a surplus of 2% about 18 months into the crisis; exports play a smaller role (they contract during the worst, as other countries also slow) and rebound in later years (p.56).
- Recovery readings (p.57): recovery takes a few years — burned investors are slow to return but cheap goods and labor attract FDI; incomes and spending pick up after about one to two years; it takes about three years from the bottom for activity to return to average; the real FX is undervalued by around 10% on a PPP basis at the start of stabilization and stays cheap; exports pick up by 1 to 2% of GDP; capital inflows return after about four to five years and equities take about as long to recover in foreign-currency terms. The country then re-enters the early part of a new cycle.
- Well-managed vs poorly managed (table p.57, paraphrased): Managing the currency — well: policy makers bluff that they will never let the currency weaken, then devalue as a surprise, by enough that no further weakening is expected (a two-way market); poorly: weakness widely expected (more pressure, higher rates), initial devaluation too small with more to follow, raising rates and inflation expectations. Closing external imbalances — well: tight money contracts domestic demand in line with the fall in incomes; incentives (rates compensating for depreciation risk) keep investors in the currency; poorly: policy favors domestic conditions, money too loose, pain postponed and inflation stoked; capital controls or other restrictions attempted. Smoothing the downturn — well: reserves used judiciously to smooth foreign-capital withdrawal while closing imbalances; poorly: reserve sales used to sustain higher spending. Managing bad debts/defaults — well: over-indebted entities' debts worked through with credit supplied elsewhere; poorly: disorderly defaults, uncertainty and capital flight.

### The spiral from a transitory inflationary depression to hyperinflation (pp.58–60)
- Many cases recover with incomes/spending up and inflation back to normal (transitory BoP crises); a subset spiral into hyperinflation (inflation so extreme that prices at least double every year, with extreme wealth losses and hardship) — more common than one might think (p.58).
- Defining characteristic (p.58, paraphrase of the bolded rule): policy makers fail to close the gap between external income, external spending and debt service, and fund external spending for sustained periods by printing lots of money. Weimar had a crushing external debt (reparations) that mostly could not be defaulted on, so large inflation was all but destined; in other cases policy makers choose to keep printing to prop up growth rather than align spending with income, and if repeated over years on a large scale, an avoidable hyperinflation follows. Stopping printing mid-crisis causes extreme illiquidity and deep contraction, and it gets harder the longer the crisis runs: by late October 1923 Germany's entire 1913 money stock would have bought a one-kilo loaf of rye bread, so stopping would have halted commerce (p.58).
- How the spiral plays out (pp.58–60): inflationary psychology sets in; currency declines → more capital flight → more depreciation, inflation and printing; the growth linkages weaken. Each round of printing sends more money into real or foreign assets rather than goods and services (those who shorted cash and bought real/foreign assets did better), so stocks stop providing a wealth effect and devaluation stops stimulating (p.58). Persistent inflation feeds a wage-cost spiral (wage demands, price rises, wage indexing) (p.59). Savers get quicker to short cash and buy foreign and physical assets; depositors shorten maturities and investors shorten or stop lending — "During inflationary deleveragings, average debt maturities always fall" (p.59). Shorting cash is cheap (printing pushes real rates down), causing illiquidity; banks can't meet cash demand; the central bank chooses printing again to support banks and lend to businesses, which funds more foreign-asset buying and hedging (p.59). FX-denominated debt burdens rise as the currency falls; foreigners demand higher rates for default risk; debt service rises (p.59). Governments raise income and wealth taxes; the wealthy evade and move capital abroad (p.60). Domestic credit creation doesn't produce growth because it is spent on foreign assets or stores of value (gold, factories, imports — "even rocks" in Weimar) (p.60). Eventually money loses all three roles: store of value (people hold a few days' reserves), unit of account (too many zeros) and medium of exchange (producers demand foreign currency or barter); illiquidity peaks because foreign exchange is short and cannot be relieved by printing; stores close, unemployment rises, the economy contracts rapidly (p.60). Hyperinflation wipes out financial wealth, inflates away lenders' wealth and debtors' liabilities, and brings strikes (even police), disorder, crime, looting, violence; Weimar declared a "state of siege" (p.60).
- Investing rule in a hyperinflation (p.60): get short the currency, get money out of the country, buy commodities and commodity industries (gold, coal, metals); equities are a mixed bag — a losing proposition as inflation turns to hyperinflation (share prices diverge from the exchange rate); gold is the preferred asset, shares a disaster in real terms even though they rise in local currency, bonds wiped out.
- End state (p.60): a currency that has spiraled into hyperinflation never recovers its status as a store hold of wealth; the classic exit is a new currency with very hard backing while the old one is phased out.
- Chart (p.59) "Domestic Currency Bond Return (USD, Indexed)": typical inflationary depressions fall from ≈100 at month 0 to ≈68 around month 12 and recover to ≈105 by month 84; hyperinflations fall from ≈100 to ≈10 by month 24 and ≈0 from month ≈48 on.

### War economies (pp.61–64)
- War economies differ totally (armament GDP that is destroyed, lower unemployment from conscription, resource reallocation, different capital flows) so the statistics require a different orientation (p.61).
- Cycle rule (p.61, bolded in original): war risk rises sharply when two conditions coincide — (1) internal conflict between the wealthy/capitalist right and the poor/proletarian left that brings populist, autocratic, nationalistic and militaristic leaders to power, and (2) external rivalry between economic and military powers of comparable strength; economics and politics then become tightly entangled and disruptive conflicts become far more likely than normal. Periods of war are followed by periods of peace in which the dominant power sets the rules until a rival emerges; a ~250-year frame is needed to see the rise and fall of empires and reserve currencies (p.61).
- Two types of relationships (p.61): (a) cooperative-competitive (win-win; tough but respectful negotiation) and (b) mutually threatening (lose-lose; interaction through "war" rather than "negotiation"); either side can force the second path, both are needed for the first. Relative power should be in the back of both parties' minds; war makes dominance clear and is followed by extended peace under the winner's rules (p.62).
- Economic-policy priority in war (p.62): maintain access to the financial and non-financial resources needed for the war effort; since no country can fund a war and tolerable civilian spending from current income, it needs borrowing capacity (creditworthiness, deep local-currency debt markets) and/or very large FX reserves. After the war, in the paying-back period, the debts and the outcome (won or lost) have enormous market consequences; the worst thing a country can do is run up a lot of debt and lose a war — "ABOVE ALL ELSE, DON'T DO THAT" (p.62); cf. Germany after WWI and Germany/Japan after WWII.
- Magnitudes (p.62): averaged across war cases, both military spending and soldiers as a share of population rise by around five times; in WWII 20% of the US workforce shifted to the military.
- After a major war (p.63): winners and losers are both saddled with debt; the contraction of military spending usually causes a postwar recession (retooling, re-employment); countries deleverage with the same dynamics as other deleveragings, but losers fare much worse — deeper depression, more money printing, meaningful drawdown of savings/reserves, much higher inflation (sometimes hyperinflation). Post-WWII losers (Germany, Japan, others) lack charts because their statistics were unreliable or unavailable (p.64).

### In Summary (pp.64–65)
Headline (p.64): managing debt crises is about spreading out the pain of the bad debts; it can almost always be done well when the debts are in the country's own currency; the biggest risks are policy makers' failure to act from lack of knowledge and/or authority; with foreign-currency debt the choices are harder and the consequences more painful. Policy makers' understanding and authority vary a lot and they tend not to react forcefully until the crisis is extreme; strong checks-and-balances systems can block required moves; rules can't anticipate everything; politics is horrendous in debt crises. Crises are devastating over three to ten years but fade in importance relative to productivity in the long run; their political consequences (e.g., populism) can be more consequential than the crises themselves. The eight real-GDP-per-capita charts (p.65) show contractions of more than 3% shaded; the biggest bumps came from wars more than from the worst depressions (though those wars may have been caused by political fallout from the depressions).

## (g) Principles stated as rules (short paraphrases, with page)

1. Judge credit growth by what it produces and whether the debt can be serviced; too little credit can be as damaging as too much (p.9).
2. The downside of a big debt stock depends on policy makers' willingness and ability to spread the losses — which requires own-currency debt and influence over creditor/debtor behavior (p.10).
3. Debt crises recur because lending is never done perfectly and policy makers err toward easy credit for near-term rewards (p.10).
4. A classic bubble warning: a rising share of borrowing is used to pay debt service (p.11). The cycle reverses when debt-service costs exceed what can be borrowed (p.11).
5. In a crisis, handle the lending institutions first; the most leveraged, most concentrated lenders are the systemic risk (p.11).
6. Nearly all own-currency debt crises can be managed well; the biggest risks are policy makers' ignorance and lack of authority and the politics of who bears the pain (p.12, p.27, p.64).
7. The key to handling a crisis: know the levers, have the authority, and decide the rate per year at which burdens are spread and who benefits and suffers (p.12).
8. Four levers — austerity, defaults/restructurings, printing/monetization, wealth transfers — must be balanced to achieve a beautiful deleveraging (p.12, p.27, p.32).
9. Central banks should target debt growth at a sustainable level (income growth able to service it), not just inflation and growth; ignoring bubbles is imprudent (p.19).
10. No single aggregate metric predicts a crisis; examine individual entities' debt-service capacity and concentration (p.21).
11. Tops are made when things can't get better yet everyone believes they will (p.21).
12. The downturn's size depends on sector-level sensitivity to tightening and loss cascades more than on the size of the tightening; analyze big sectors and big players, not averages (p.22).
13. Right after a bubble bursts, the wealth effect dominates monetary policy; don't mistake the early decline for a buying opportunity (pp.22–23).
14. Solvency problems need equity capital or rule changes; cash-flow problems need cash or guarantees; both can be fixed by fiscal/monetary policy if debt is in the home currency (p.24).
15. Depressions are driven by the supply/demand of credit, money and goods, not primarily by psychology (p.25).
16. Policy makers initially get the mix wrong (austerity, moral-hazard reluctance); the longer stimulus is delayed the uglier the deleveraging (p.27).
17. Austerity and tax increases in a depression are big mistakes (p.27).
18. When systemic failure looms, act immediately: curtail panic and guarantee liabilities, provide liquidity, support solvency, recapitalize/nationalize systemic institutions (pp.28–29).
19. Best-managed cases: recognize the magnitude swiftly, don't save every expendable institution, restore credit pipes, keep growth/inflation acceptable during the workout; speed and aggressiveness determine severity and length (p.29).
20. Protect small depositors; let equity, subordinated debt and large depositors absorb losses; prioritize IMF/BIS obligations (pp.29–30).
21. Rich and poor sharing a budget in a downturn produces conflict and populism; how well it is handled determines how the society weathers the period (p.31).
22. Nominal income growth must exceed nominal interest rates for debt burdens to fall; printing that merely offsets credit contraction is not inflationary (p.32); avoid printing too much (p.33).
23. In the end policy makers always print; doing it quickly and well beats doing it late (p.34).
24. Right amount of stimulus: neutralize the deflationary credit collapse and get nominal growth above nominal rates — without a run on debt assets (p.35).
25. MP1 (rates) first; MP2 (QE) works while risk premiums are high and fades as they compress; MP3 (money to spenders) when both are exhausted; fiscal-monetary coordination with spending incentives is most effective (pp.36–38).
26. Expect 5–10 years for activity and about a decade for equities to regain prior peaks after a big deflationary deleveraging (p.38).
27. Currency rule: if a currency is expected to fall faster than its interest rate compensates, holders exit; when money flows out, real rates must rise less if the real exchange rate falls more (p.39).
28. Currency weakness is what makes a depression inflationary; the squeeze ends via defaults/money creation, debt-service relief, or depreciation beyond inflation (p.40).
29. Vulnerability screen: no reserve currency, low FX reserves, large foreign debt, large and rising budget/current-account deficits, negative real rates, history of high inflation and negative currency returns (p.40).
30. Watch capital flows above all — they are the most volatile flows (p.41).
31. Reserve defenses, managed declines and capital controls typically fail; defend only until reserves or rates become dangerous (pp.47–48, 50).
32. Prefer a large, surprise, one-off devaluation that creates a two-way market over gradual decline; bluff until you move (pp.51, 57).
33. The right currency level is a low one: good for trade, positive total return, and an interest rate appropriate for domestic conditions (p.54); depreciate sharply and quickly (p.55).
34. Devaluation is like cocaine — stimulative short term, ruinous when abused; watch the central bank before going long the currency (p.55).
35. Hyperinflation follows when policy makers keep funding external imbalances by printing for sustained periods; stopping printing mid-crisis is extremely hard and gets harder with time (p.58).
36. In a hyperinflation: short the currency, move money out, hold commodities and commodity industries (gold); shares and bonds are losers (p.60). A hyperinflated currency never regains store-of-wealth status; the exit is a new hard-backed currency (p.60).
37. War economics: maintain access to financial and non-financial resources; above all, never run up big debts and lose a war (p.62).
38. Headline: managing debt crises is about spreading out the pain; policy failure, not debt, is the main risk; foreign-currency debt makes everything harder (p.64).
39. Long run: productivity dominates; a crisis's political consequences (populism) can outlast and outweigh the crisis (p.64).
