## (b) Dated timeline (with the numbers Dalio cites)

### Phase 0 — WWI financing, Jul 1914–Nov 1918
- Pre-1914: Germany on gold standard; all paper money and government debt convertible to gold at fixed rate; but by 1914 the central bank lacked enough gold to back money in circulation at the fixed price (PDF 75).
- Jul 29–30, 1914 (news): Berlin nervous, big banks keeping gold in vaults; Berlin Bourse on cash basis; runs on savings banks (PDF 75).
- Aug 1914: war breaks out; citizens rush to convert paper to bullion; run on banking system; within weeks Reichsbank and Treasury paid out 195 million marks of gold to the public, ≈10% of total gold reserves (PDF 75). Aug 2 news "German Bank Rate Up"; Aug 3 news: Reichsbank raised bank rate to 5% and Lombard rate to 6% (PDF 75).
- Jul 31, 1914: conversion of money to gold suspended; Reichsbank authorized to buy short-term Treasury bills and use them (with commercial bills) as collateral for printing; by end-Aug 1914 Reichsbank notes in circulation up ≈30% (PDF 76).
- 1914–1917: government spending/GDP rose 2.5x (PDF 76). Huge resistance to taxation and lockout from international lending → war financed with domestic debt (PDF 76–77).
- 1914: government debt insignificant; 1918: local-currency debt stock 100 billion marks ≈130% of GDP (PDF 77). Deficit averaged ≈40% of GDP 1914–1918; money supply up almost 300% over the war (PDF 78).
- Up to 2H 1916: public financed the entire deficit; war bonds regularly oversubscribed (PDF 77). War bonds paid fixed 5% while inflation passed 30% by early 1915 → real rates deeply negative (PDF 77). Mark fell ≈25% vs USD by 1916 (PDF 77). Subscription ratios (chart PDF 78): 2H14 ≈170%, 1H15 ≈125%, 2H15 ≈122%, 1H16 ≈100%, 2H16 ≈85%, 1H17 ≈65%, 2H17 ≈45%, 1H18 ≈38%, 2H18 ≈20%.
- News (PDF 76): Sep 22, 1915 rush for third war loan; Mar 12, 1916 food crisis; Oct 9, 1916 fifth loan 10,590,000,000 marks; Feb 24, 1917 Reichstag votes 15,000,000,000 marks, total credits 67,000,000,000 marks ($16,750,000,000); Jul 9, 1917 sixth loan 13,120,000,000 marks; Nov 18, 1917 seventh loan 15,000,000,000 marks ($3,570,000,000 at normal exchange) "an uphill fight".
- After 1917: money creation accelerated as citizens refused new debt; marks in circulation almost doubled mid-1917 to mid-1918 but the mark rallied on expectations of victory after Russia's withdrawal; it fell only in 2H 1918 as defeat loomed (PDF 78).
- Last two years of war: government begins borrowing in foreign currency; by 1918 Reichsbank and private firms each owed ≈2.5 billion gold marks in FX (≈5 billion total; one gold mark = one paper mark in 1914); hard-currency debt <10% of total debt stock but larger than the Reich's entire public gold reserves (PDF 78).
- Havenstein (Reichsbank president): covering FX debts "will be extraordinarily difficult if we do not get a large war indemnity" (PDF 79). Jaffé: unless England paid a third to half of German war costs, "currency collapse" (PDF 79).
- By start of 1918: mark down ≈25% vs USD, prices tripled (PDF 79). German wartime inflation peaked ≈60% y/y (chart) vs USA ≈20%, GBR ≈30% — not out of line with other belligerents (PDF 79).
- News (PDF 77): Feb 18, 1918 budget 7,332,000,000 marks vs ≈5,000,000,000 prior year; Mar 13, 1918 war debt 109,000,000,000 marks; Jun 13, 1918 loan 15,001,425,000 marks; Oct 27, 1918 "Debts Now Exceed Assets"; Nov 7, 1918 debt exceeding $35,000,000,000, two-fifths of national wealth mortgaged; Nov 11, 1918 Armistice, Kaiser flees.

### Phase 1 — Versailles and the first inflation, Nov 1918–Mar 1920
- Nov 1918: surrender triggers capital flight; over next few months mark −≈30% vs USD, stock market loses almost half its real value, local-currency government debt +≈30% (almost all monetized), money supply +≈50%, inflation to 30% (PDF 80). News Nov 23, 1918: steady run on banks nationwide, cities printing Notgeld (PDF 80).
- Jun 28, 1919: Treaty signed. Terms: lose 12% of territory, 10% of population, 43% of pig-iron capacity, 38% of steel capacity; compensate Allied citizens for wealth seized; honor all prewar debts to Allied creditors; reparations commission to set the bill in 1921; interim payment equivalent to 20 billion marks in gold, commodities, ships, securities, real assets (PDF 80–81).
- Jul 1919–Jan 1920: mark −90% vs USD; inflation 140% by end-1919 (PDF 81). Driver: citizens getting capital out, debtors with external liabilities buying FX to repay (PDF 81). Aug 10, 1919 news: mark at 35 centimes in Switzerland vs peace price 125 (PDF 81).
- Jul 1919–Mar 1920: devaluation and negative real rates stimulate: nominal equities ≈0.3 → ≈0.7 (chart), commodity index ≈25 → ≈225, union unemployment ≈6.5% → ≈3%, exports ≈80 → ≈240 m goldmarks (PDF 82).
- Dec 1919: Erzberger Financial Reform passed — top income rate approaching 60%, wealth 65%; direct taxes to become 75% of Reich income (≈15% in 1914); intended to cover all spending except reparations by 1922 (PDF 83). Dec 17, 1919 news: premium bond loan raised only 3,800,000,000 of expected 5,000,000,000 marks (PDF 82).
- Inflation ≈200% by end-1919; real domestic war debt cut to ≈25% of its 1918 value; real local-currency debt ≈57 bn (1913 marks) Jan 1919 → ≈15 bn Jan 1920 (PDF 83).
- Mar 1920 (news Mar 14/28): Kapp Putsch; collapses within days after general strike (PDF 83–84; the body text misdates it "March 1921").
- Jan 31, 1920 news: 500 paper marks paid for 20-mark gold pieces; May 16, 1920: prices up 650% since 1914, necessities +17% in first two months of 1920 (PDF 83).

### Phase 2 — Relative stabilization, Mar 1920–May 1921
- Fourteen months in which the mark halted its slide, prices stabilized, and Germany outperformed a contracting world; Keynes lost ≈£13,000 shorting the mark (PDF 84).
- Global backdrop: 1920–21 industrial production −20% US, −18.6% UK; unemployment 22% US, 11.8% UK (PDF 84); US/UK short rates ≈7% in 1920 (chart PDF 85).
- Reichsbank discount rate held at 5% until 1922; spring 1921 commercial-bill purchases raised from 3.1% to 9% of bills outstanding; deficit narrowed but still ≈10% of GDP, financed by floating debt; real ex-reparations spending rose in 1920 and 1921 (PDF 85).
- 1919–1921: industrial production +75% (still far below 1913: IP ≈40% of 1913 in 1919 → ≈70% in 1921; real GDP ≈62% → ≈80%) (PDF 85–86).
- Mar 1920–May 1921: import prices from US/UK −≈50%; capital inflows; mark rallies from ≈100 to ≈38/USD (mid-1920) then ≈60–65; inflation ≈200% → ≈0% by early 1921 — prices stop rising for first time since 1914 (PDF 86). Jul 2, 1920 news: German debt 265 billion marks; Sep 17, 1920: mark 210/£, −40% in six weeks (PDF 85).
- By 1921 almost a third of deposits at the seven largest German banks foreign-owned (PDF 86); Keynes calls the speculative inflow "the greatest ever known" (PDF 86).
- Mar–Jun 1920: economic ministry intervenes to depress the mark after the early-1920 appreciation tripled union unemployment (≈2% → ≈6%); mark falls ≈38 → ≈78/USD by Sep 1920; unemployment back to ≈3.7% by Mar 1921 (PDF 87).
- May 27, 1921 news: Berlin to pay Allies $200,000,000; May 31 reparation payment via Treasury bills in dollars (PDF 87).

### Phase 3 — London Ultimatum, May 1921
- Allies threaten to occupy the Ruhr within six days. Reparations fixed at 132 billion gold marks (≈330% of GDP); 50 bn payable in quarterly installments of 3 bn gold marks/yr = ≈10% of GDP or 80% of export earnings; remaining 70 bn to start whenever the Allies judged Germany able — debt service could triple at a moment's notice (PDF 88).
- German FX debt 330% of GDP vs other pre-inflationary-depression cases (chart PDF 88): PER 1987 ≈185%, BGL 1995 ≈80%, BRZ 1980 ≈65%, HUN 2008 ≈57%, ECD 1998 ≈47%, PHP 1983 ≈45%, TLD 1996 ≈40%, IDR 1997 ≈37%, ARG 2001 ≈33%, TUR 2000 ≈33%, CHI 1981 ≈27%, RUS 1997 ≈25%.
- Mark sells off immediately: −75% by end-1921; prices almost double (PDF 88).
- Why the four BoP levers were closed (PDF 89–90): ≈50% of revenues would go to reparations so non-reparation cuts would have to be drastic and were "politically impossible"; summer 1920 state of emergency after looting, Mar 1921 Communist seizures of shipyards, Erzberger assassinated (text says Oct 1921; news dates it Aug 28, 1921); taxes already raised by Erzberger reform; prewar foreign holdings seized under the treaty; gold reserves below even the first interest payment; Bank of England "politely refused" a 500 m gold-mark loan for the second installment; default impossible under invasion threat.

### Phase 4 — Emerging spiral, Jun–Dec 1921
- Central bank depletes much of its gold for the first reparation payments (PDF 91). Jun 25, 1921 news: 800,000,000 marks paid in European currencies; Jun 30: second installment 44,000,000 gold marks (PDF 91).
- Mark −75% over the period; inflation approaching 100% p.a.; sharpest drop Oct 1921 after the League cedes Upper Silesia to Poland (PDF 91). Nov 8, 1921 news: 3 marks for 1 cent, record low (PDF 93).
- Flight into goods: "general liquidation", auto sales at all-time highs, textile bookings months ahead, full capacity and overtime (PDF 91–92).
- Market rates anchored at 5% with inflation ≈10x that; real investment at prewar highs; monthly bankruptcies −75%; "flight from the mark to the machine" (PDF 92). Real wages −≈30% (PDF 92). Union unemployment ≈4% → ≈1.2% by end-1921 (chart PDF 92).
- 2H 1921 "orgy of speculation": stocks nearly triple in real terms; Aug 1921 Berlin exchange shuts three times a week; Nov 1921 open one day a week, banks refuse share orders after 10 a.m. (PDF 92–93). Nominal equity index ≈1.5 → ≈3.8; real ≈1.0 → ≈2.5 peak (chart PDF 93).
- End-1921: inflation nearing 100%; external deficit ≈10% of GDP funded by foreigners still buying marks (PDF 93). NYC mark purchases (goldmark mln): 2H19 ≈4.5, 1H20 ≈17, 2H20 ≈11, 1H21 ≈13, 2H21 ≈14 (PDF 93).
- News: Aug 6, 1921 debt +8,339,040,000 marks in June to 135,031,060,000; Dec 14, 1921 note issue +1,846,000,000 in first week of Dec (PDF 92–93).

### Phase 5 — Moratorium negotiations, Jan–May 1922
- Jan 1922 Cannes conference: Reparations Commission cuts the year's debt-service bill by 75% conditional on new taxes (incl. forced loan of 1 bn gold marks on the wealthy), lower spending and printing, and formal Reichsbank independence; Havenstein welcomes independence as freedom to print (PDF 94).
- End-Jan 1922: mark +30% from 1921 lows; inflation ≈140% p.a. but no longer accelerating (PDF 94).
- Mar 29, 1922: Rathenau Reichstag speech blaming BoP not printing (PDF 94–95).
- Feb–May 1922: mark swings 10–20% on negotiation news; −≈40% vs USD by end-May (PDF 95). Indexed chart (PDF 95): Cannes opens ≈−5%, mid-Jan peak ≈+10%, French tougher stance ≈0%, Genoa postponed ≈−20%, Genoa deadlock ≈−55%, early-Apr trough ≈−78%, Genoa progress ≈−60%, Rapallo ≈−55%, bankers' gold-loan optimism rally to ≈−20% (early May), Genoa ends ≈−45%, late-May ≈−60%, early-Jun ≈−40%.
- Jun 1, 1922 news: Allies grant partial moratorium on this year's scheduled payments (PDF 95).

### Phase 6 — Hyperinflation begins, Jun–Dec 1922
- Jun 1922: France rejects the commission's capacity-to-pay findings and threatens to seize the Ruhr coal mines; J.P. Morgan Jr.'s international loan committee concludes a gold loan is impossible; Jun 24 Rathenau assassinated (PDF 96).
- Foreigners withdraw: of the ≈one-third of German bank deposits that were foreign-owned, ≈two-thirds leave within months; domestic capital flight ahead of the agreed confiscatory taxes (PDF 96).
- Jul 1922: acute liquidity crisis and bank runs; banks on three-day weeks, unable to honor deposits or weekly payrolls; some print their own (illegal) marks (PDF 96). Aug 20 news: circulation +6,811,000,000 marks in second week of Aug, +14,900,000,000 since July (PDF 96). Aug 30 news: Reichsbank paying only 40% of cash demanded (PDF 97).
- Aug 1922: brink of collapse; Reichsbank accelerates printing; Treasury bills held by Reichsbank ≈40–50% → ≈80% by end-1922; marks in circulation ≈100 bn (1921) → ≈1,300 bn (end-1922) (chart PDF 97). Commercial-bill purchases 0 → ≈700 bn marks and loan-bureau credit ≈25 → ≈380 bn marks Jan 1922–Jan 1923 (chart PDF 97). By end-1922 the Reichsbank held ≈one-third of all commercial bills and had raised credits to the banking system by 1,900%; money supply up ten-fold (PDF 97).
- Aug 1922: prices rising >50% per month; policy makers recognize the spiral but keep printing (PDF 98). Keynes in Hamburg: Reichsbank advised banks to cash no checks over 10,000 marks (PDF 98).
- Sep 1922: trapped in the spiral; FX weakness leads prices, which lead money growth (log chart PDF 99: by Dec 1922 mark/USD ≈40–50x Jan level, CPI ≈40x, money supply ≈12x).
- Oct 1922: Frankfurter Zeitung — dollars replacing marks as unit of account (PDF 100). Oct 12, 1922: capital controls restricting citizens' FX purchases — "rarely successful" (PDF 100).
- Stocks: −50% real since June, rally 2H Oct 1922 while real profit margins collapse (PDF 100). Real equity index ≈0.5 by late 1922 vs ≈5.5 in 1914; food ≈95% of household spending (chart PDF 100).
- News: Sep 7, 1922 record issue 22,978,000,000 marks in last week of Aug; Sep 11 prices doubled in August; Oct 28 issue 35,466,969,000 marks in third week of Oct; Dec 17 debt +123,000,000,000 marks in first ten days of Dec; Dec 23 deficit nearing one trillion marks, ordinary spending more than double tax receipts; Dec 25 Reichsbank discounts +172 billions in a week, currency +123 billions; Dec 27 Allied commission declares Germany in willful default (wood deliveries) by 3 to 1 (PDF 97, 99). Dec 4, 1922: private banks charging 20% through fees (PDF 99). Mark ≈7,000/USD and inflation ≈2,500% y/y at end-1922 (charts PDF 98).

### Phase 7 — Ruhr occupation and terminal inflation, Jan–Aug 1923
- Jan 1923: Germany misses a timber delivery; French-Belgian force occupies the Ruhr; Berlin declares and subsidizes "passive resistance"; ≈half the coal supply must now be imported; spending, deficit, printing all rise (PDF 101). Jan 11 news: French enter Essen 4:45 a.m. (PDF 101).
- Jan–Jun 1923: Reichsbank issues dollar-denominated debt at a steep discount, sells ≈400 million gold marks of borrowed FX and reserves to defend a dollar peg (monthly net reserve sales, goldmark mln: Jan ≈42, Feb ≈42, Mar ≈55, Apr ≈125, May ≈42; chart PDF 102); rate raised to 18% while inflation ≈10,000% ("mostly a symbolic move") (PDF 101). News: Jan 19 bank rate 10% → 12% ("was 5 per cent in July"); Apr 30 rate 12% → 18%; Jan 28 mark 28,500/USD; Jan 31 paper marks +216 billions in a week; Feb 12 prices +248½% in January, 7,159x prewar (PDF 101–102).
- Intervention lifts the mark ≈50% for three months, brief deflation hurts shorts; by May reserves are clearly insufficient; peg abandoned six months after adoption; inflation reaches 36,000,000,000% by Nov 1923 (PDF 101).
- Summer 1923: sporadic FX interventions fail; finance minister: "the complete collapse of the mark is already underway" (PDF 102). Jul 1922–Nov 1923: mark −99.99999997% vs USD (dollar cost +1,570 billion %), prices +387 billion %; the entire 1913 money stock (6 bn marks) buys one kilo loaf of rye bread by late Oct 1923 (PDF 102).
- News: Feb 22, 1923 Germany says 45,600,000,000 (gold) marks paid, 56,500,000,000 incl. treaty losses; Apr 9 US claims $1,187,736,867; Apr 16 fiscal deficit 6¼ trillion marks; May 15 suicides 80,000/yr vs 1,200 prewar; May 21 exchange open three days a week; Jun 25 prices +41% in ten days, old investments obliterated 100%; Aug 1 mark printers strike (PDF 102).

### Phase 8 — Ending the hyperinflation, Aug 1923–Oct 1924
- Late 1923: inflation >1,000,000%, real tax revenue collapsing, famine, barter; Wissell: inflation "is murdering the Republic" (PDF 103). Aug 29 news: hunger deaths from toadstools (PDF 104).
- Aug 1923: small-denomination dollar-indexed "Gold Loan" bills issued; hoarded, vanish from circulation; nearly 2,000 kinds of Notgeld circulating by fall 1923 (PDF 106). Aug 2 news: Cuno cabinet "near gold" loan plan; Aug 20: Hilferding refuses to spend last gold buying paper marks; Berlin streetcar fare 100,000 marks (PDF 103).
- Aug 18, 1923: Reichsbank privately tells government it will discount no more government debt from 1924 (PDF 108).
- Aug 1923: some taxes indexed to inflation plus emergency taxes; Oct 1923 all taxes indexed; 25% of government employees dismissed, remaining salaries −30%; passive-resistance subsidies ended (PDF 108).
- Sep 1923: Ruhr industrialists agree to resume coal transfers; mid-Oct government ends financial support of passive resistance (PDF 104). Sep 26 news: all states accept Ruhr peace; Oct 10 first Ruhr mine agreements (PDF 104).
- Oct 15, 1923: Rentenbank and rentenmark announced; cap of 1.2 bn rentenmarks on government credit; Reichsbank monetization of government debt forbidden after Nov 15 (PDF 106, 108). Nov 15: rentenmark enters circulation, pegged 1 rentenmark = 1 trillion paper marks and 4.2/USD (prewar gold parity) (PDF 106). Nov 19 news: 142,000,000 rentenmarks delivered to government (PDF 105).
- Nov 1923: Allies suspend reparations and reopen negotiations; end-Nov Dawes committee created; no hard-currency payment for the next 10 months (PDF 103–104).
- Dec 1923: rentenmark and newly pegged paper mark at par abroad; inflation at sustainable levels (PDF 106); government, having spent the 1.2 bn immediately, requests 400 m more — Rentenbank refuses (PDF 108). Real tax receipts ≈15 m gold marks (Oct 1923) → >300 m (Dec 1923) (PDF 109). Dec 3 news: real wages 44–60⅞% of prewar; Dec 10 some prices −50%; Dec 17 emergency issues one-fifth of Reichsbank circulation (PDF 105).
- Jan 1924: budget in surplus (PDF 109). Nov 1923–Jan 1924: Reichsbank FX holdings ≈20 m → ≈300 m gold marks as hoarded FX is converted (PDF 110). Feb 1, 1924 news: internal debt wiped out, private bonds/mortgages restored at 10% of gold value; Feb 18: mortgages valorized at 15%; Feb 18: living costs −34½% vs Nov; Mar 10: supported unemployed 1,301,270 (Feb 15) vs 1,582,852 (Jan 15), partly unemployed 635,839 → 257,840 (PDF 107–108).
- Feb 1924: government "revalues" some private debts (mortgages, deposits, debentures) — acts as a tightening (PDF 109). Apr 7, 1924: Reichsbank caps total private-sector credit; inflation slightly negative May 1924 (PDF 109). Apr 28 news: surplus 19,280,800 marks; Apr 21 loans doubled in a month (PDF 109).
- Apr 1924: Dawes report (minimum normal payments 2,500,000,000 marks/yr per Apr 10 news); accepted by France/Britain Apr 16, adopted Apr 18 (PDF 109). May 4 elections: 35 million voters; Dawes coalition 230 seats vs 192 (PDF 110). Aug 1924: Dawes Plan in force; Aug 17 agreement, French to quit Ruhr within a year (PDF 104, 110).
- Aug 1924: reparations service cut to ≈1% of GNP in 1924 and 1925 (>90% below 1923), payments rescheduled; full 130 billion gold marks still owed (PDF 104). Oct 1924: Dawes loan 800 m gold marks in dollars, pounds, francs (PDF 110). Oct 11, 1924: reichsmark introduced 1:1 for rentenmarks, convertible to gold at prewar parity; paper marks withdrawn by Jun 5, 1925; rentenmark phased out over the next decade (PDF 107). Stabilization regime Nov 1923–Oct 1924 under constant speculative attack until reserves rebuilt (PDF 110). Crisis "largely over" by 1924 (PDF 110).

## (c) Policy responses in order and Dalio's assessment

1. **Jul 31, 1914 gold suspension + monetization of Treasury bills** — classic and near-universal in wartime; prevents a liquidity crisis; he treats it as the lesser evil vs a credit contraction (PDF 76).
2. **Deficit finance via domestic war bonds at fixed 5%** — worked while the public bought; became a self-reinforcing monetization spiral once real rates went deeply negative and subscriptions fell below issuance (PDF 77–78). Mistake embedded: "naive lenders" assumed a return to old gold parity (PDF 77).
3. **Foreign-currency borrowing late in the war** — "a bad sign"; hard-currency debt cannot be printed away and exceeded gold reserves (PDF 78).
4. **Allowing the mark to fall / not defending with tight money after Versailles (1919)** — stimulative for exports, profits, employment, equities; policy makers consciously preferred it to "confiscatory taxation" and a deflationary recession; inflation cut the real domestic debt to ≈25% of its value — he presents this as the classic have-nots-over-creditors redistribution (PDF 82–84).
5. **Erzberger tax reform, Dec 1919** — raised direct-tax share to 75% and was meant to cover all non-reparation spending; later a constraint because taxes were already very high and the reform had been watered down (PDF 83, 89).
6. **Deliberate depreciation Mar–Jun 1920** to restore competitiveness after the early-1920 rally: "It worked" — exports and employment recovered (PDF 87).
7. **Easy Reichsbank policy 1920–22 (5% discount rate, bill purchases, 10%-of-GDP deficits financed by floating debt)** — allowed Germany to escape the 1920–21 global contraction (+75% industrial production 1919–21) but left it dependent on hot money (PDF 85–87).
8. **Response to the London Ultimatum (1921): rely exclusively on printing** — he says policy makers "wagered" printing was the least terrible choice; "In my opinion, they made a mistake in not trying to achieve a better balance between deflationary forces and inflationary ones" (PDF 90).
9. **Anchoring rates at 5% while inflation ran ≈10x higher (1921)** — produced a borrowing and speculation surge, unproductive investment, and reinforced the spiral (PDF 92).
10. **Cannes compromise, Jan 1922** (75% cut in the year's service; new taxes, forced loan, Reichsbank independence) — "mostly symbolic"; taxes too small, independence used to print more; but renewed optimism halted the slide for a few months (PDF 94).
11. **Massive liquidity provision to banks, 2H 1922** (commercial bills, loan bureaus; credits +1,900%) — prevented collapse of the financial system but caused a ten-fold money-supply increase and shifted psychology from "semi-managed" to "out of control" (PDF 97). His verdict: printing was "less the cause of the hyperinflation than what was required" to avoid deflationary defaults; stopping would have collapsed all commerce; French invasion threat made stopping impossible (PDF 98–99).
12. **Capital controls, Oct 12, 1922** — classic, "rarely successful": easy to evade and they intensify the desire to escape (PDF 100).
13. **Dollar-peg defense Jan–May 1923** (borrowed-FX sales ≈400 m gold marks, rate to 18%) — worked for three months, then failed for lack of reserves; abandoned after six months, hyperinflation returned stronger (PDF 101).
14. **Gold Loan bills, Aug 1923** — disorganized, reactive; hoarded rather than circulated (PDF 106).
15. **Stabilization package, Aug 1923–Oct 1924** — succeeded because comprehensive: (i) reparations restructured (Dawes; service to 1% of GNP) — "critical" precondition, without relief no currency could have held (PDF 104); (ii) rentenmark with hard backing and a 2.4 bn total credit cap, mortgages on 5% of agricultural/industrial property, plus implicit gold backing since the shrunken real money stock could be fully covered by reserves (PDF 106–107); (iii) end of monetization (Aug 18 and Oct 15, 1923 announcements; Rentenbank refusing the extra 400 m) — signaled central-bank independence (PDF 108); (iv) deficit closed via indexed taxes, 25% staff cuts, 30% pay cuts, end of Ruhr subsidies; stabilization itself raised real tax yields (surplus by Jan 1924) (PDF 108–109); (v) credit tightened (Feb 1924 revaluation, Apr 7, 1924 credit cap) — painful but inflation went slightly negative (PDF 109); (vi) FX reserves rebuilt (repatriation; Dawes loan 800 m gold marks) — ended the speculative attacks (PDF 110). Earlier one-offs (peg, capital controls) had failed because they were not comprehensive (PDF 103).

## (d) Market behavior by phase (numbers as cited)

- **WWI**: gold price in marks ≈1.0 → ≈1.7 (1914–17); mark/USD ≈4.2 → ≈7.3 (late 1917), rally to ≈5 (1918), ≈6.7 (end-1918); inflation peaks ≈60% y/y; war-bond subscriptions 170% → 20% of issuance (PDF 76–79).
- **Nov 1918–Mar 1920**: mark −30% then −90% (Jul 1919–Jan 1920) to ≈100/USD; stocks −≈50% real at surrender, then nominal equities ≈0.3 → ≈0.7 and commodities ≈25 → ≈225 (Jul 1919–Mar 1920); inflation 30% → 140% → ≈200% (PDF 80–83).
- **Mar 1920–May 1921**: mark ≈100 → ≈38 → ≈60–65/USD; inflation to ≈0%; equities/industry booming on foreign inflows; Jan 7, 1921 news "Stocks in Germany Have Climbed Fast"; May 15, 1921 "German Shares Boom" (PDF 86–87).

## (e) Lessons and principles (paraphrased, with page)

- When money in circulation far exceeds gold backing and holders rush to convert, the central bank must either tighten (defaults) or end convertibility and print; it almost always prints because that is less painful (PDF 76).
- Debt monetization is inflationary; it lowers real rates, discourages lending to government, and so forces more monetization — a self-reinforcing spiral (PDF 78).
- Hard-currency (FX/gold) debt cannot be printed away; borrowing in foreign currency is a bad sign (PDF 78).
- Inflationary depressions are most likely in countries that lack a reserve currency, have low FX reserves, large foreign debt, large and rising budget/current-account deficits, negative real rates, and a history of inflation and negative currency returns; Germany met all six by 1918 (PDF 79).
- Early on, currency weakness causes the printing, not the reverse: capital flight drives the currency down, which drives inflation (PDF 81).
- Devaluations hurt importers and FX debtors but are stimulative for the economy and assets during weakness (PDF 82).
- In crises, redistribution from haves to have-nots is the likelier policy because have-nots' conditions become intolerable and they outnumber the haves (PDF 84).
- Economic pain breeds populist and reactionary leaders on both left and right (Kapp Putsch) (PDF 84).
- A bubble sign: many market participants are new buyers with no prior experience (PDF 86).
- Capital inflows lower inflation and lift growth; outflows do the opposite and make the central bank's job harder; dependence on hot money makes a recovery fragile (PDF 87).
- A balance-of-payments crisis is like any payment problem: spend less, earn more, borrow/dissave, or default/relief; a country has an extra lever — changing the amount and value of its currency (PDF 89–90).
- The defining feature of cases that become hyperinflations: policy makers never close the income/spending gap and keep funding it by printing; too much reflationary printing and too deep a devaluation relative to austerity and default produce the worst inflationary depressions (PDF 90).
- An inflationary spiral: FX obligations → currency decline → inflation and liquidity crisis → printing → more capital flight (PDF 91).
- Rising inflation causes a surge in buying that is flight from money, not real growth; real assets, consumer durables, machines, and stocks become inflation hedges (PDF 91–93).
- In inflationary depressions each round of printing sends more money out of the currency rather than into activity; once at the hyperinflationary stage it is extremely hard to stop printing because money is scarce even as it loses value; stopping would collapse the payment system (PDF 98).
- Hyperinflations are not simply caused by reckless central banks that could just stop; spirals push policy makers into printing as the least-bad option (PDF 98).
- As the spiral accelerates velocity rises; currency weakness leads inflation leads money growth, not the other way around (PDF 99).
- Money's two roles — store of wealth and medium of exchange — are lost in that order (PDF 99–100).
- Capital controls are a classic but rarely successful lever: easy to evade, and the trap makes people want out even more (PDF 100).
- Ending it: restructure the hard-currency debt, replace the currency with hard backing, cap printing and monetization, close the deficit, tighten credit, rebuild FX reserves — comprehensively, not piecemeal; and none of it works unless the FX debt is actually reduced (PDF 103–104).
- Currency credibility depends on the issuing institution; a currency with few promises-to-deliver against it is not forced into the monetize-or-default dilemma, and a quantity limited to its backing can be kept stable (PDF 107).
- To build confidence, debt monetization must stop; central banks must be independent of politics (PDF 108).
- When the central bank stops monetizing, the budget must be balanced because new creditors are unavailable in an inflationary deleveraging (PDF 108).
- Stabilization raises real tax receipts, creating a virtuous cycle (PDF 109).
- Debt revaluations tighten credit, support the currency and lower inflation — the mirror of debt reductions (PDF 109).
- Without meaningful FX reserves, speculative attacks remain a threat even after reform (PDF 110).

## (c) Policy responses in order and Dalio's assessment

1. **1927 discount-rate cut 4% → 3.5%** to help France/Germany/UK stem gold losses — focused on growth/inflation not debt growth; "inadvertently" financed the bubble (PDF 118).
2. **Macroprudential restraint 1929** (acceptance rate, supervision, "direct pressure" letter) instead of rate hikes — "largely ineffective" (PDF 122).
3. **Tightening Feb 1928–Aug 1929 (to 6%)** — "It was the tightening that popped the bubble"; worst bubbles come with asset inflation not CPI inflation, so central banks accommodate too long (PDF 121–122).
4. **Private market supports Oct 1929** (Mitchell's $25 m in March; Bankers' Pool $125 m; brokers' ad; Rockefeller bid) — first of "many failed attempts to bolster the market" (PDF 125, 128).
5. **NY Fed $100 m injection, Oct 29, 1929, outside Washington approval** — eased credit, prevented some failures, but couldn't stop the collapse; normal-time checks and balances are "poorly suited to crisis scenarios" (PDF 126).
6. **Rate cuts Oct 1929–Mar 1930 (6% → 3.5%)**, Hoover's Nov 1929 tax cut and $175 m construction, pledge conferences, Mar 1930 appropriations (≈1% of GDP) — "broadly praised", produced a meaningful rally; but he says early-stage investors and policy makers classically underestimate the real-economy weakening so initial responses "aren't enough" (PDF 128–129).
7. **Smoot-Hawley (Jun 1930) and the Sep 1930 immigration ban** — classic protectionist responses; triggered retaliation (Canada) and accelerated the trade collapse (PDF 130–131).
8. **Letting the Bank of United States fail, Dec 1930** — bankers judged it insolvent; it was systemically important (Broderick) and became "The First Domino"; Fed's gold-standard and member-bank limits (only 35% of banks) left it unable to act as lender of last resort (PDF 132–133).
9. **Hoover's "indirect relief" and budget balancing (1931)** — opposed direct federal relief; Mellon/Hoover austerity is "the classic rookie mistake"; austerity cuts incomes along with spending so debt/income barely improves (PDF 134–135, 149).
10. **Hoover moratorium, Jun 20–Jul 6, 1931** — big relief rally (+12% in two days), but a 35%-type rally is small against a ≈90% decline; disappointment followed because it wasn't enough (PDF 138–139).
11. **Standstill agreement and refusal of new German loan, Jul 1931** — Hoover's "banker-made crisis" instinct to make banks bear the cost is "classic but misguided"; left Germany's collapse unchecked, run on the mark (PDF 139–140).
12. **Bank of England reserve sales, rate rises, foreign loans ($250 m; $200 m + $200 m), Aug 1931** — classic defense moves that merely funded the flight; Hoover: loans "mostly served to create more fear" (PDF 140).
13. **UK suspends gold, Sep 20, 1931** — sterling −30%, but debt in own currency so no default risk; bonds dip then recover, rates cut 1 pt, equities +11%: devaluation plus printing "kicked off a beautiful deleveraging" (PDF 141–142).
14. **Fed rate hikes Oct 9/16, 1931 (1.5 → 3.5%) to defend gold** — "not a path to good things"; BoP-crisis rate rises big enough to compensate currency risk are too big for the domestic economy, "so they don't work"; bank runs and deposit contraction followed (PDF 143).
15. **National Credit Association (Oct 1931), Home Loan Discount Banks, Federal Land Banks** — well received, +35% rally, but no change in total money/credit so the fundamental imbalance was unresolved; proposals "too small" (PDF 144–145).
16. **RFC, Jan 1932 ($500 m + $3 bn)** — classic widening of collateral and borrowers; $1.3 bn to 5,520 institutions by Aug; limited by "good collateral" rule so missed the neediest; later neutered by the disclosure requirement (PDF 145–146, 148).
17. **Banking Act Feb 27, 1932 (Fed buys government bonds — "quantitative easing") and Section 13(3)** — a weakening of gold-standard principles passed on urgency; bill yields −2 pts, 10y 4.3% → <3.5%, Dow +19.5%; later critical for 2008 (PDF 146).
18. **Mark-to-market suspension, Jan 1932** — "another classic move"; relieved bank pressure (PDF 147).
19. **Jawboning banks to lend (Hoover/Mills committees)** — little success (PDF 147).
20. **Fed halts open-market purchases, Jul 1932** — political (regional Feds; "weaken the dollar"; low long rates hurt bank profits) (PDF 149).
21. **Revenue Act Jun 6, 1932 (tax increases)** — contractionary; deficit grew relative to GDP as economy shrank faster — "rookie move" (PDF 149).
22. **FDR's "no devaluation" pledge during campaign** — expedient promises inconsistent with fundamentals "should never be believed" (PDF 149).
23. **Nevada/Michigan/Maryland bank holidays, Nov 1932–Mar 1933; RFC refusal to rescue Guardian** — classic response to runs, but failing to rescue a systemically important institution let the ripple spread system-wide (PDF 149–151).
24. **Hoover's War Powers capital-controls idea, Feb 1933** — classic but ineffective; blocked anyway (PDF 152).
25. **FDR Mar 5–9, 1933: bank holiday, gold embargo, Emergency Banking Act, guarantees, triage of banks, fireside chat** — "by far the most impactful"; redeposits of >$1 bn show liquidity rather than withholding it fixes liquidity-driven runs; systemically important banks always supported, small ones allowed to fail (PDF 152–155).
26. **Leaving gold / devaluation / printing (Mar–Apr 1933; gold to $35)** — rates fall, money +1.5% in three months, Dow +100% in four months; "the turning point"; averages overstate the recovery for the poor (PDF 154).
27. **Regulatory overhaul (Securities Act, Banking Act of 1933/FDIC/Reg Q, gold-clause ban, HOLC)** — mapped to template levers: restructuring debts, deposit insurance, increased regulation — "typical at the end of big debt crises" (PDF 155–156).
28. **Fiscal stimulus (CCC, FERA, TVA, PWA; +$2.7 bn = 5% of GDP by 1934)** — deflation turned "acceptable"; stimulation balanced deflationary forces so nominal growth exceeded nominal rates — the definition of a beautiful deleveraging (PDF 156).
29. **1935 Fed deliberations** — correctly held pat while capacity was slack; mistake was the later reading of the stock boom as a bubble to be leaned against (PDF 158).
30. **Reserve-requirement doubling Aug 1936–May 1937 + gold sterilization Dec 1936–Jul 1937 + fiscal tightening (outlays −10% twice, Revenue Act 1937, Social Security tax)** — first step hurt nothing ("typically the case"), the combination caused the 1937–38 sell-off (stocks −≈60%), credit contraction, unemployment to 15% (PDF 159–161).
31. **Reversal late 1937–1938 (twist, desterilization, reserve cut, deficit to ≈3% of GDP)** — stocks and credit recover in 1938–39, helped by war entry (PDF 162).

## (e) Lessons and principles (paraphrased, with page)

- Debt growth is healthy when it finances activity that produces fast income growth (1923–26) (PDF 117).
- Bubbles root in real productivity/technology gains plus leveraged bets that they continue (PDF 118).
- Central banks easing to help other countries' gold problems can inadvertently finance a domestic bubble (PDF 118).
- Seven bubble characteristics: high prices vs traditional measures; prices discounting further rapid gains; broad bullish sentiment; purchases financed by high leverage; extended forward purchases/inventories; new buyers entering; stimulative policy inflating it and tight policy popping it (PDF 118–119).
- New, fast-growing lending markets outside regulated banking (call loans, investment trusts) are symptomatic of bubbles; untested "innovations" lead to the next crisis; lenders and speculators profit symbiotically (PDF 119).
- Call loans created asset/liability mismatches; squeezing mismatched lenders and borrowers is a classic crisis ingredient (PDF 119).
- Higher prices draw more supply of the asset and looser credit standards, the reverse of what logic suggests (PDF 121).
- Bank earnings and balance sheets look healthy in good times; trouble appears when deposits run and asset values fall (PDF 121).
- The worst debt bubbles come with asset inflation financed by debt, not CPI inflation; central banks err by targeting inflation/growth rather than debt growth and whether debts will produce the income to service them (PDF 121).
- Rising short rates flatten/invert the curve, raise the return on cash, pull money out of assets, create a negative wealth effect — the bubble reverses into a bust (PDF 122).
- No specific shock is needed to burst a bubble; unsustainable leverage-driven price momentum and max positions suffice (PDF 123).
- Normal-time checks and balances are poorly suited to crises needing immediate aggressive action; in 1929 there were few established paths (PDF 126).
- Sharp bear-market rallies "classically occur repeatedly" during depression phases (PDF 127).
- Equities and credit-risk bonds fall while Treasuries and low-credit-risk assets rise — typical of this phase (PDF 128).
- Early in deleveragings, investors and policy makers underestimate how much the real economy will weaken; small rallies reverse and initial responses are too small (PDF 129).
- Even with rate cuts, widening spreads raise the rates most borrowers actually face (PDF 130).
- Protectionism and anti-immigrant sentiment classically rise in severe downturns (PDF 130–131).
- Runs on non-guaranteed financial institutions are classic and can kill them in days (PDF 131–132).
- A gold standard is akin to foreign-currency debt; policy makers have a limited toolkit until they break the link (PDF 132).
- Banks are structurally vulnerable to runs via the liquidity mismatch; legal limits (member-only lending) and gold constrained the Fed as lender of last resort (PDF 132–133).
- In depressions politics matters by blocking sensible policy or producing extreme policy (PDF 134).
- Deficit worries and austerity are classic depression-phase responses; one person's spending is another's income, so cuts barely dent debt/income ratios (PDF 135).
- A reserve currency widely lent by foreign institutions gets squeezed when credit creation in it collapses; falling imports deepen the shortage (dollar 1931 ≈ 2008) (PDF 135).
- Carry trades in good times set up worse bad times; wealth disparities feed populist extremists of left and right (PDF 136).
- When systemically important institutions risk failing, policy makers must keep them running; concentrated lender systems especially (PDF 137).
- Big policy announcements repeatedly produce optimism and rallies amid a ≈90% decline; moves that look small from afar feel huge in real time (PDF 139).
- Making banks bear the cost is a classic but misguided response — politically necessary, financially disastrous (PDF 140).
- Classic currency defense (reserve sales, rate hikes, foreign loans) tends to fund the flight (PDF 140).
- Devaluation and printing by a country with own-currency debt start a beautiful deleveraging; bonds dip then rally (PDF 142).
- In a BoP crisis, rate rises big enough to compensate currency risk are intolerable domestically and don't work (PDF 143).
- Rallies on liquidity programs fade when total money and credit don't change (PDF 144–145).
- Policy makers toy with deflationary levers for a couple of years before realizing debt reduction and austerity are too painful and inadequate, then act more aggressively (PDF 145).
- Lending against widening collateral and to wider borrowers is a classic liquidity lever, by central banks or governments (PDF 145).
- Abandoning mark-to-market is a classic bank-support move (PDF 147).
- "Main Street vs Wall Street" outrage over bailouts makes necessary rescues politically hard (PDF 149).
- Austerity to balance the budget in a depression is a rookie move (PDF 149).
- Deleveraging debates become antagonistically political and populist (PDF 149).
- Expedient political promises inconsistent with fundamentals should never be believed (PDF 149).
- Reliance on deflationary levers → "ugly deleveraging": nominal rates above nominal growth so debt/income rises despite defaults (PDF 150).
- Failing to rescue systemically important institutions spreads ripples system-wide (PDF 151).
- Capital controls are a classic, ineffective BoP response (PDF 152).
- Providing liquidity rather than withholding it cures liquidity-driven runs; guarantee the banks you keep open; support systemic banks, let small ones fail (PDF 153).
- Breaking the gold link is required to print; the dollar then falls against currencies and gold (1933 ≈ 1971) — the reason to study history for timeless principles (PDF 153).
- Leaving gold was the turning point at which all markets and statistics bottomed; averages mask the uneven recovery (PDF 154).
- Changing laws to prevent the last crisis is typical at the end of big debt crises (PDF 155).
- Beautiful deleveraging requires enough stimulation to offset deflation and lift nominal growth above nominal rates (PDF 156).
- The first tightening typically doesn't hurt stocks or the economy (PDF 159).
- Competitive devaluations leave everyone in the same place vs each other, all devalued vs gold (PDF 159).
- Fear of repeating the 1920s bubble led policy makers to tighten into a fragile recovery; combined monetary and fiscal tightening caused the 1937 sell-off and recession (PDF 158–161).
- Uneven recoveries that favor elites breed pressure for redistribution and strikes (PDF 161).
- Economic conflict between rising and incumbent powers (Thucydides's Trap) precedes military conflict; the 1930s wars really started ≈10 years before 1939/1941 (PDF 163).
