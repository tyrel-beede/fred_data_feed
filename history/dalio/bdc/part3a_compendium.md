## (b) Glossary of Key Economic Terms (PDF 263–265, printed 5–7) — paraphrased

The glossary opens by saying the explanations are simplified for brevity and apply to the whole book.

- **balance of payments** — the net of all transactions (goods, services, financial assets, other payments) between a country/currency and the rest of the world; worsens when inflows fall relative to outflows, like a household whose income and borrowing shrink relative to spending.
- **balance-of-payments crisis** — a crisis in which the balance of payments deteriorates until a country's entities lack buying power abroad; effectively running out of cash and credit.
- **bubble** — the debt-cycle stage of self-reinforcing, unsustainable rises in debt, asset prices and growth; "unsustainable" is the key word, so the boom is followed by a bust.
- **capital inflows/outflows** — cross-border purchases/sales of a country's assets; foreigners trading a country's assets are inflows, domestic players trading foreign assets are outflows.
- **core inflation** — inflation excluding especially volatile goods such as commodities.
- **currency peg** — a policy of holding the currency at a fixed value against another currency, a basket, or gold.
- **current account balance** — exports minus imports plus net income receipts; a deficit means spending exceeds income and must be financed through the capital account (borrowing or selling equity).
- **debt service** — the cost of carrying debt over a period: interest plus principal.
- **deleveraging** — reducing debt burdens.
- **deleveraging attribution** — Bridgewater's decomposition of what drove changes in debt burdens. The black dot is the annualized change in debt as a % of GDP over the period (positive = debt levels rose). Bars above zero are factors that increased debt burdens, below zero factors that decreased them; they include factors moving GDP (inflation, real growth) and factors moving debt (interest payments financed with new debt, other new borrowing). The methodology differs across countries, mostly because of data availability.
- **depression** — a severe downturn at the debt-crisis stage with self-reinforcing declines in asset prices and growth; classically when the central bank is constrained in easing.
- **easing** — central-bank moves that make money and credit more available (lower rates, printing, regulatory changes) or fiscal moves on spending, taxes or regulation.
- **fiscal balance** — whether the government spends more than it collects in taxes (deficit, financed by borrowing or drawing savings) or less (surplus).
- **foreign FX returns** — what an investor earns in a foreign currency: the exchange-rate change plus the interest differential versus home.
- **FX** — foreign exchange rate.
- **FX debt** — debt denominated in a currency other than the investor's home currency.
- **GDP** — gross domestic product, the total value (price × quantity) of final goods and services; the usual scale for expressing other quantities as a share of the economy.
- **GDP gap** — an imprecise measure of operating above or below sustainable capacity ("potential"); negative gap = slack, positive gap = little slack; also called the output gap.
- **liquidity** — whether money and credit are scarce or plentiful; when low, even good borrowers pay more; when high, they borrow easily and cheaply.
- **long rate** — the interest rate on longer-term debt; the book's nominal long rate is typically the 10-year government yield.
- **money 0 (M0)** — the amount of money printed in a currency: physical currency in circulation plus reserves at the central bank.
- **nominal growth** — the change in the value (price × quantity) of output, so it includes inflation, unlike real growth.
- **potential** — an imprecise estimate of the output an economy can sustain near capacity; the GDP gap measures current output against it.
- **real** — adjusted to remove inflation; the book stresses that such measures are imprecise (a country's true real FX is unknowable).
- **real FX** — an imprecise gauge of whether a currency is cheap or expensive from relative currency levels and price levels versus history; positive = more expensive, negative = cheaper; usually measured against trading partners (trade-weighted index, TWI).
- **real GDP** — an imprecise measure of the quantity of goods and services produced, as opposed to their value.
- **real growth** — an imprecise measure of the change in the quantity produced, as opposed to nominal growth.
- **real interest rates** — interest rates net of inflation; negative when inflation exceeds the interest earned, so lenders lose buying power.
- **reflation** — periods when monetary policy is easy/stimulative and helps produce recovery.
- **reserves** — a country's holdings of foreign currency and/or gold; government savings in foreign currency usable to make purchases and influence the supply, demand and price of its own currency.
- **short rate** — the interest rate on very short-term lending, usually 3 months or less.
- **stimulation** — see easing.
- **tightening** — policy moves that reduce the availability of money and credit and slow growth: raising rates, letting money supply shrink, cutting government spending, restricting bank lending.
- **yield curve** — the gap between short-term and long-term rates; inverted when short rates exceed long rates (short rates priced to fall), otherwise short rates priced to rise.

## (c) Introduction to the compendium: "48 Debt Crises" (PDF 267, printed 9)

**How the cases were chosen.** The authors systematically screened for deleveraging periods across major
countries over the last century, focusing on cases with a real GDP decline of more than 3%, then
triangulated that list against the work of the IMF and prominent academics. The list is explicitly a
sample, not a census, of the past century's debt crises; it was chosen to show the key similarities
(Part 1) and the differences. Post-WWII Germany, Italy and Japan were excluded for lack of data (TOC footnote).

**How the statistics are presented/computed.** Each case consists of a "simple computer-generated text
analysis" of what happened plus charts of the basic stats. The "auto-text" comments are observations of
the basic stats and "present a very simplified version of our algorithmic analysis" (p. 9), viewed through
the even-more-simplified template explained in Part 1 (the "Archetypal Big Debt Crisis"). The point of
showing cases at this level is that principle-level commonalities and differences "pop" compared with the
detail of Part 2. No formulas are given in this range; the only methodological statements are:

- Each case page: "The gauges below are composed from a compendium of stats shown in the chart deck that
  follows. Note these are meant to be rough measures." (e.g., PDF 269)
- Footnote on each case page: the first two charts are gauges of bubble/depression conditions and of
  tightness/easiness of money and credit; for each gauge the distance from zero conveys the extent of the
  bubble and the crossing of zero marks shifting into or out of the bubble (PDF 269 footnote, repeated on
  every case page).
- Glossary entry "deleveraging attribution" (above) defines the attribution charts.

**Archetype chart template.** Every case has the same page structure:

Page 1 — "[Country] [years] Case Auto-Summary": opening line stating the cycle type ("classic deflationary
deleveraging cycle", "classic wartime deflationary deleveraging cycle", "classic wartime hyperinflationary
deleveraging cycle"); sections "The Bubble Phase" (or "The War Phase") and "The Depression Phase" (or "The
Post-War Phase"). Right column, four charts:
1. *Bubble & Depression Gauge* (index, annotated "Bubble", "Depression", "Reflation"; war cases: "War Boom", "Post-War ReTooling" or "Post-War Depression", "Reflation"), with two vertical lines marking the phase breaks.
2. *Tightening & Easing Gauge* (index, annotated "Peak Tightening", "Peak Easing").
3. *Core Inflation (Y/Y)*.
4. *Deleveraging Attribution: Depression Period* — stacked bars with a dot/diamond labeled with the annualized change in total debt (% GDP, ann.), x-axis labeled with the depression dates.

Page 2 — "Case Auto-Summary (cont.)": section "The Reflation Phase", optional political paragraph
("The crisis had a notable impact on the politics of…helped set the stage for [name], whom many people
consider a populist leader, to take power"), and chart 5, *Deleveraging Attribution: Reflation Period*
(x-axis labeled with the reflation dates).

Pages 3–4 — "Chart Deck Appendix" and "(cont.)", 24 small charts in five blocks:
- *Indebtedness*: Total Debt (%GDP) with Debt Service (%GDP) [dual axis]; FX Debt (%GDP); Chg in Debt-GDP Ratio (Ann) with Debt Growth (%GDP, Ann).
- *Monetary and Fiscal Policy*: Nominal Short Rate; Money0 Level (%GDP); Fiscal Balance (%GDP).
- *Economic Conditions*: Real GDP (Indexed); Real Growth (Y/Y); GDP Gap; Core Inflation (Y/Y); Nominal Long Rate with Nominal Growth; Real Short Rate.
- *Markets*: Equity Price USD (Indexed); Nominal Long Rate with Nominal Short Rate; Yield Curve (SR-LR); Real FX vs TWI; FX Return for Foreign Investors (Indexed); Gold Price (Local FX, Indexed).
- *External Position*: Gold Reserves (Troy Oz Bln/Mln) for pre-war cases or Reserves (USD, Indexed) for modern cases (Japan 1987–2017 uses Reserves %GDP); Capital Inflows (%GDP); Imports (%GDP); Current Account (%GDP); Capital Outflows (%GDP); Exports (%GDP).
- Extra page 5, war cases only (UK 1941–67, US 1943–51, Germany 1918–25): *Government and Military*: Military Personnel (% Total Pop); Military Spending (%GDP); Govt Debt (%GDP).

Every chart carries the same two vertical phase-break lines as the gauges. Charts with no data print
"No Data Available". Attribution-chart legends come in four variants (recorded per case in the CSV
column `attrib_chart_legend_type`):
- detailed: Monetization / Interest / Real Growth / Inflation / Defaults / Net New Debt / Debt Change (%GDP, Ann)
- simple: Real Growth / Inflation / Chg in Dom. Debt / Debt %GDP (ann.)
- FX: simple + FX Debt Chg due to FX Move / FX Debt Chg due to Other (Finland, Hungary)
- reparations: simple + FX Debt Chg due to FX Move / Reparations (Germany 1918)

**The auto-text template (this is the "statistics table").** The numbers are embedded in standardized
sentences. The sentence patterns, with the CSV column each feeds, are listed in section (e).

## (d) Case notes (printed page / PDF page)

Abbreviations: D/GDP = total debt as % of GDP; "levers" = classic bad-debt policy levers pulled out of 9;
"ann." = annualized. Years-to-recover figures are "before real GDP reached its prior peak" and "equity
prices in USD terms recovered within". "Takeaway" lines are my synthesis, not the book's wording; the book
gives no per-case lesson line.

### 1. United States 1926–1936 (printed 11–14, PDF 269–272) — domestic currency, deflationary
- Phases: bubble 1926–29; depression 1929–33; reflation 1933–36.
- Bubble: rising debt, equities 31% ann., growth 3%, GDP gap 13%; D/GDP peak 125%; moderate tightening (+250 bp).
- Depression: shock = 1929 stock crash; GDP −26%, stocks −84%, home prices −24%, unemployment +23; D/GDP +98% (26% ann.) from falling incomes, deflation and interest financed with new debt.
- Reflation: "slightly longer than average" bust; broke gold peg; M0 +6% GDP; rates to 0%; real FX −5%; 8 of 9 levers (liquidity, asset purchases); nominal growth 8% vs long rates to 3%; unemployment −14; D/GDP −70% (21% ann.), via real incomes then inflation; GDP peak regained in 7 yrs, USD equities in 25 yrs.
- Politics: "helped set the stage for FDR" as a populist leader (PDF 270).
- Takeaway: the archetype — a monetary-sovereign depression ends when the peg goes, money is printed and the full policy toolkit is used; equities take a generation.
- Data gaps: FX debt, capital inflows/outflows.

### 2. United Kingdom 1927–1936 (printed 15–18, PDF 273–276) — domestic, deflationary
- Phases: no broad-based domestic bubble (tied to bubbly partners); depression 1929–31; reflation 1931–36.
- Pre-crisis D/GDP 210%, domestic currency, domestically owned.
- Depression: shock = ripples from US crash / early Depression; GDP −10%, stocks −61%, unemployment +7; D/GDP +13% (6% ann.), mainly interest financed with new debt, offset by paydown.
- Reflation: "slightly shorter than average" bust; broke gold peg; M0 +2% GDP; rates 0%; real FX −8%; only 1 of 9 levers ("not aggressive"); growth 4% vs long rates 3%; unemployment −8; D/GDP −29% (5% ann.) mainly by paying down debt; GDP peak 5 yrs, equities 8 yrs.
- Takeaway: early exit from gold plus a cheap currency produced a beautiful deleveraging with almost no bank clean-up.
- Data gaps: FX debt, capital inflows/outflows.

### 3. Japan 1925–1936 (printed 19–22, PDF 277–280) — domestic, deflationary
- Phases: no broad-based bubble; depression 1927–31; reflation 1931–36.
- Pre-crisis D/GDP 65%; domestic currency, domestically owned.
- Depression: shock = 1929 global crash; GDP −4%, stocks −47%; D/GDP +36% (8% ann.); drivers not stated.
- Reflation: "slightly longer than average" bust; broke gold peg; rates to 2%; real FX −26%; 3 of 9 levers ("somewhat aggressive"); growth 4.7% vs long rates 3.9%; D/GDP −18% (4% ann.), via real growth. No M0, unemployment or recovery-year figures.
- Politics: set the stage for Hideki Tojo (PDF 278).
- Takeaway: low debt and a very large real depreciation made this the mildest 1930s case in GDP terms.
- Data gaps: FX debt, M0, fiscal balance, GDP gap, all external-position flows and trade (only gold reserves shown).

### 4. France 1926–1938 (printed 23–26, PDF 281–284) — domestic, deflationary
- Phases: bubble 1926–29; depression 1929–36; reflation 1936–38.
- Bubble: strong growth (3%), equities 45% ann., GDP gap 9%; D/GDP fell 13% during the bubble to 205%.
- Depression: shock = US crash ripples; GDP −17%, stocks −57%; D/GDP roughly flat (chart: 1% ann.).
- Reflation: "relatively long" bust; broke gold peg; rates 2%; real FX −4%; inflation allowed to average 10%; 3 of 9 levers; growth 15% vs long rates 4%; D/GDP −37% (15% ann.) via inflation; GDP peak 21 yrs.
- Politics: set the stage for Leon Blum, 1936 (PDF 282).
- Takeaway: staying on gold stretched the depression to seven years; once the peg broke, inflation did the deleveraging fast.
- Data gaps: FX debt, M0, capital inflows/outflows.

### 5. United Kingdom 1941–1967 (printed 27–31, PDF 285–290; 290 blank) — domestic, wartime deflationary (war winner)
- Phases: war phase (WW2); post-war recession 1943–47; reflation 1947–67.
- War: big deficits financed by borrowing; growth 6%, GDP gap 10%, equities 16% ann.; debts "rose sharply" (no peak figure).
- Post-war: GDP −15%; D/GDP +102% (25% ann.) as incomes fell and war costs continued; peak fiscal deficit 31% of GDP.
- Reflation: "slightly longer than average" bust; devalued vs gold by 30%; M0 −8% GDP; rates 1%; real FX −10%; inflation averaged 4%; growth 7% vs long rates 2%; unemployment flat; D/GDP −139% (7% ann.) via inflation; GDP peak 10 yrs. No levers count.
- Takeaway: the slow-burn financial-repression template — two decades of nominal growth above pinned rates.
- Extra: Government and Military page. Data gaps: FX debt, capital inflows/outflows.

### 6. United States 1943–1951 (printed 33–37, PDF 291–296; 296 blank) — domestic, wartime deflationary (war winner)
- Phases: war phase (WW2); post-war recession 1945–50; reflation 1950–51.
- War: debts rose to 150% GDP; growth 13%, GDP gap 19%, equities 15% ann.
- Post-war: GDP −13%, unemployment +5; D/GDP roughly flat (chart: 2% ann.).
- Reflation: "relatively long" bust; M0 −0.9% GDP; rates 1%; Fed stayed easy as activity surged; growth 11% vs long rates averaging 2%; unemployment −2; D/GDP −22% (14% ann.) via real growth; GDP peak 6 yrs. No levers count, no real FX figure.
- Takeaway: the fast version of wartime deleveraging — growth outruns pinned rates.
- Extra: Government and Military page. Data gaps: FX debt, capital inflows/outflows.

### 7. Norway 1984–1996 (printed 39–42, PDF 297–300) — domestic, deflationary
- Phases: bubble 1984–87; depression 1987–92; reflation 1992–96.
- Bubble: growth, equities and housing (house prices 19% ann.); D/GDP peak 211%; inflows 4% GDP, current account −2% GDP; growth 3%, GDP gap 5%; large tightening (+700 bp).
- Depression: debt service peak 58% GDP; shock = commodity-price ripples; GDP −4%, home prices −38%; D/GDP roughly flat (chart 0%).
- Reflation: "relatively long" bust; M0 +2% GDP; rates to 4%; real FX +3%; 4 of 9 levers (bank nationalization, liquidity, labor-market reforms); growth 6.1% only "close to" long rates 5.5%; unemployment −4; D/GDP −35% (7% ann.); GDP peak 5 yrs.
- Takeaway: the one domestic case where nominal growth barely cleared rates, yet bank nationalization and a firm currency still deleveraged.
- Data gaps: none.

### 8. Finland 1987–2001 (printed 43–46, PDF 301–304) — domestic currency but heavily foreign-owned, deflationary
- Phases: bubble 1987–89; depression 1989–93; reflation 1993–2001.
- Bubble: growth 5%, equities 18% ann.; D/GDP peak 272%; inflows 3% GDP, current account −3% GDP; large tightening (+700 bp); real FX peaked +24%.
- Depression: shock = asset-price declines hitting bank solvency; GDP −12%, stocks −36%, home prices −32%, unemployment +13; D/GDP +32% (9% ann.) as incomes fell.
- Reflation: "slightly longer than average" bust; M0 +7% GDP; rates 3%; real FX −10%; 7 of 9 levers (nationalization, liquidity, asset purchases); growth 6% vs long rates 4%; unemployment −6; D/GDP −72% (8% ann.) via real growth; GDP peak 7 yrs, equities 5 yrs.
- Takeaway: overvalued currency + foreign funding = deep bust; devaluation + full bank clean-up = strong recovery. Attribution chart includes FX-debt revaluation terms.
- Data gaps: GDP gap, reserves.

### 9. Sweden 1987–2000 (printed 47–50, PDF 305–308) — domestic, deflationary
- Phases: bubble 1987–90; depression 1990–93; reflation 1993–2000.
- Bubble: debt +15% GDP to 239%; housing-driven; inflows 2% GDP, current account −3%; growth 2%, GDP gap 4%; large tightening (+500 bp); real FX peaked +15%.
- Depression: debt service peak 65% GDP; shock = housing-price declines hitting bank solvency; GDP −6%, stocks −34%, home prices −7%, unemployment +9; D/GDP +40% (12% ann.); peak fiscal deficit 10% GDP.
- Reflation: "relatively short" bust; M0 +5% GDP; rates 3%; real FX −5%; 7 of 9 levers (nationalization, liquidity, asset purchases, labor reforms); growth 5% vs long rates 4%; unemployment −1; D/GDP −28% (4% ann.); GDP peak 3 yrs, equities 4 yrs.
- Takeaway: the fastest clean recovery in the set — short bust, aggressive bank resolution, currency float.
- Data gaps: none.

### 10. Japan 1987–2017 (printed 51–54, PDF 309–312) — domestic, deflationary, net creditor
- Phases: bubble 1987–89; depression 1989–2013; reflation 2013–17.
- Bubble: debt +24% GDP to 307%; equities 28% ann.; growth 5%, GDP gap 4%; inflows 1% GDP; large tightening (+450 bp); net creditor kept the yen strong.
- Depression: debt service peak 78% GDP; shock = real estate and stock busts; stocks −67%, home prices −43%, unemployment +3; D/GDP +59% (3% ann.) mainly interest financed with new debt. No GDP-decline figure.
- Reflation: very slow resolution (policy never pushed nominal growth above rates for a long time); from 2013 M0 +58% GDP, rates 0%, long rates 0%, real FX −10%; 7 of 9 levers; growth 2%; unemployment flat; D/GDP −43% (9% ann.) mainly via monetization. No recovery-year figures.
- Takeaway: the "ugly" phase can last 24 years when easing is insufficient; monetization eventually does the work.
- Data note: several chart series labeled "%PGDP"; Reserves shown as %GDP.

### 11. United States 2004–2014 (printed 55–58, PDF 313–316) — domestic, deflationary
- Phases: bubble 2004–07; depression 2007–09; reflation 2009–14.
- Bubble: debt +38% GDP to 349%; inflows 8% GDP, current account −6%; growth 3%, GDP gap 3%; equities 14% ann.; large tightening (+400 bp).
- Depression: debt service peak 68% GDP; shock = housing bust; GDP −4%, stocks −50%, home prices −28%, unemployment +5; D/GDP +23% (15% ann.).
- Reflation: "slightly shorter than average" bust; M0 +16% GDP; rates 0%; real FX −10%; 6 of 9 levers (recapitalization, liquidity, asset purchases); growth 3% vs long rates 2%; unemployment −3; D/GDP −59% (11% ann.) via real incomes, inflation and paydown; GDP peak 4 yrs, equities 5 yrs.
- Takeaway: the modern beautiful deleveraging — short bust because printing and bank recapitalization came within two years.
- Data gaps: none.

### 12. Austria 2005–2017 (printed 59–62, PDF 317–320) — euro (not controlled), deflationary
- Phases: bubble 2005–08; depression 2008–09; reflation 2009–17.
- Bubble: mainly equities (20% ann.); debt +19% GDP to 279%; high foreign ownership; growth 3%; moderate tightening (+200 bp).
- Depression: debt service peak 69% GDP; shock = 2008 GFC; GDP −4%, stocks −66%; D/GDP +39% (32% ann.) as incomes fell.
- Reflation: "relatively short" bust, ECB-led; M0 +19% GDP; rates 0%, long rates 0%; real FX −2%; 5 of 9 levers (recapitalization, liquidity); growth 3%; unemployment flat; D/GDP −74% (9% ann.) via inflation; GDP peak 3 yrs; USD equities not yet recovered.
- Takeaway: a core euro member with foreign-owned debt got a short bust but a long, inflation-driven deleveraging.
- Data gaps: GDP gap, reserves.

### 13. Germany 2006–2017 (printed 63–66, PDF 321–324) — euro (not controlled), deflationary
- Phases: no domestic bubble; depression 2008–09; reflation 2009–17.
- Pre-crisis D/GDP 261%, mostly domestically owned; debt service peak 51% GDP.
- Depression: shock = 2008 GFC; GDP −7%, stocks −53%; D/GDP +26% (18% ann.) as incomes fell.
- Reflation: "slightly shorter than average" bust, ECB-led; M0 +14% GDP; policy rate −1%, long rates 0%; real FX −4%; 6 of 9 levers (nationalization, liquidity, asset purchases); growth 3%; unemployment −4; D/GDP −56% (7% ann.) via real growth; GDP peak 3 yrs; USD equities not yet recovered.
- Takeaway: without a domestic bubble the bust was sharp but brief, and growth rather than inflation did the deleveraging.
- Data gaps: reserves.

### 14. Greece 2005–2018 (printed 67–70, PDF 325–328) — euro (not controlled), deflationary; no beautiful phase
- Phases: bubble 2005–08; depression 2008–17; reflation chart labeled "2017-" only.
- Bubble: debt +40% GDP to 206%; inflows 22% GDP, current account −13%; growth 3%, GDP gap 10%; equities 19% ann.; moderate tightening (+200 bp); high foreign ownership.
- Depression: debt service peak 42% GDP; shock = 2008 GFC; capital inflows fell 34% of GDP; "short rates increased by 86%" (PDF 325); GDP −27%, stocks −91%, home prices −42%, unemployment +15; D/GDP +101% (11% ann.), mainly falling real incomes.
- Reflation: the book says Greece "hasn't transitioned into much of a 'beautiful' phase" because nominal growth was never pushed above nominal rates (PDF 326); chart shows −19% ann. for "2017-". No policy figures given.
- Politics: set the stage for Alexis Tsipras.
- Takeaway: the control case for what happens in a currency you cannot print: the deepest and longest depression in this half.
- Data gaps: none.

### 15. Hungary 2005–2017 (printed 71–74, PDF 329–332) — domestic currency, high foreign ownership, deflationary
- Phases: bubble 2005–08; depression 2008–13; reflation 2013–17.
- Bubble: debt +45% GDP to 214%; inflows 6% GDP, current account −8%; growth 3%, GDP gap 5%; equities 14% ann.; no bubble-phase tightening figure.
- Depression: debt service peak 31% GDP; shock = 2008 GFC; portfolio inflows fell 12% of GDP; short rates hiked by 5%; GDP −7%, stocks −73%, home prices −16%, unemployment +3; D/GDP +37% (7% ann.); peak fiscal deficit 5% GDP.
- Reflation: "relatively long" bust; M0 +4% GDP; rates 0%; real FX −6%; 4 of 9 levers; IMF program; growth 5% vs long rates 2%; unemployment −7; D/GDP −51% (12% ann.) via real growth; GDP peak 6 yrs; USD equities not yet recovered.
- Politics: set the stage for Viktor Orban.
- Takeaway: an own-currency country with foreign creditors had to hike into the bust (IMF-backed) before easing; FX-debt revaluation is a visible attribution component.
- Data gaps: none.

### 16. Ireland 2005–2017 (printed 75–78, PDF 333–336) — euro (not controlled), deflationary
- Phases: bubble 2005–08; depression 2008–13; reflation 2013–17.
- Bubble: debt +94% GDP to 271%; inflows 95% GDP, current account −5%; growth 5%, GDP gap 8%; equities only 5% ann.; moderate tightening (+220 bp); real FX peaked +17%.
- Depression: debt service peak 77% GDP; shock = European debt crisis; GDP −9%, stocks −73%, home prices −53%, unemployment +9; D/GDP +35% (7% ann.), interest financed with new debt and deflation, partially offset by defaults.
