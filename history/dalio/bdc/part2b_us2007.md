The sequence he scores as producing a 'beautiful deleveraging': liquidity first (Fed, Aug 2007–Mar 2008), then guarantees and nationalization of the GSEs (July–Sept 2008), then — after the Lehman mistake — blanket guarantees (money funds, bank debt, deposits), capital injections (CPP/TARP), printing (QE1 Nov 25, 2008; zero rates Dec 2008; QE expansion Mar 18, 2009), fiscal stimulus (ARRA Feb 2009), orderly handling of remaining insolvents (AIG, autos, stress tests), then regulatory re-tightening (2009–10) and a second dose of printing when the first wore off (QE2, Nov 2010). His verdict: the US did this faster than any prior case (what differed from 1930–33 'was the speed'), recapitalized quickly, unwound quickly and made money on the rescue; it would have been less painful had policy makers acted even earlier and had they not let Lehman fail for lack of authority.

## (f) Lessons and principles (short paraphrases)

- (p173; printed 105) The particulars (pooled/securitized mortgages, levered investment banks, OTC derivatives) are new ways of creating leverage outside regulated channels; seen that way the case is classic.
- (p174; printed 106) Bubbles emerge when central banks focus on inflation and growth and do not worry enough about debt-financed purchases of investment assets.
- (p174; printed 106) Debt bubbles form in one or a couple of markets, hidden beneath averages; only pro forma stress tests of the significant areas reveal them and their knock-on effects.
- (p174; printed 106) Because houses are bought with borrowed money, price gains have magnified effects on equity, which pulls in more borrowing, buyers and lenders.
- (p175; printed 107) The higher prices went, the more credit standards were lowered -- the opposite of what logic would dictate; classic bubble behavior.
- (p175; printed 107) Seven defining characteristics of a bubble: high prices vs traditional measures; prices discount further rapid appreciation; broad bullish sentiment; high-leverage financing; extended forward purchases; new buyers entering; stimulative monetary policy inflating it and tight policy popping it.
- (p176; printed 108) In bubbles people lever up to bet on continued price rises just as supply is increasing; nonsensical but typical.
- (p177; printed 109) The worst debt bubbles (US 1929, Japan 1989) are not accompanied by goods inflation but by debt-financed asset inflation; central banks err by accommodating debt growth while watching CPI.
- (p179; printed 111) High debt growth used to fund consumption rather than investment is a red flag because consumption produces no income to service the debt.
- (p180; printed 112) Shadow-banking growth is a common bubble feature: new channels avoid regulation, look safe through packaging and guarantees, and have not been stress-tested by a crisis; these innovations often lead to the crisis.
- (p181; printed 113) A regulatory system that lacks visibility into and authority over shadow channels, and worries about competitiveness, lets banks become under-capitalized and over-leveraged.
- (p182; printed 114) Three classic asset/liability mismatches: borrow short/lend long; lend to risky borrowers for the spread; borrow in one currency and lend in another.
- (p185; printed 117) Rising rates raise debt service, cut spending and incomes; a flattening curve raises the return on cash, pulls money out of long-duration and low-grade assets, and negative wealth effects feed back -- the tightening popped the bubble.
- (p186; printed 118) Debt crises are self-reinforcing because losses reduce capital, forcing asset sales or less lending, which lowers prices and produces more losses; mark-to-market and leverage amplify this.
- (p190; printed 122) Past volatility and correlations are not reliable forecasts of risk; low volatility and benign VAR encourage leverage (ratios near 100:1); leverage is a better indicator of future volatility than VAR.
- (p191; printed 123) Knowing which crisis actions the law permits, and how to get them approved, is a classic challenge in democracies with rigid rules and checks and balances.
- (p191; printed 123) How the moral-hazard question is handled is one of the biggest determinants of outcomes; above all keep systemically important parts alive, and err toward providing too much liquidity rather than too little.
- (p191; printed 123) Letting everything burn means the government ends up taking all the risk through nationalization at far greater cost (Geithner's view, which he shares).
- (p192; printed 124) Easing cannot solve the fundamental problem of institutions holding more debt assets and liabilities than can be serviced.
- (p192; printed 124) Short-term wholesale funding is like an uninsured deposit -- the same run dynamic as 1930-33 before FDIC insurance.
- (p193; printed 125) The securitization machine was data-mining history rather than sound logic; people levered up betting that something that had never happened would not happen.
- (p196; printed 128) Policy makers are reactive rather than proactive; political systems do not act until there is broad consensus on an intolerable problem, so they rarely act decisively until a crisis is on top of them.
- (p199; printed 131) A 'D' (deleveraging) is not a severe 'R' (recession): an R is a GDP contraction from tight policy that ends when the central bank eases; a D is a self-reinforcing financial deleveraging that causes the financial crisis and the economic crisis.
- (p200; printed 132) Early signs of stress in an institution funded short can turn into a run that causes failure within days -- lenders rationally withdraw.
- (p200; printed 132) If the debt is in one's own currency and policy makers have the knowledge and the authority, they can manage these situations with limited spill-over, though some pain is inevitable.
- (p201; printed 133) The biggest constraint on Geithner, Paulson and Bernanke was lacking the legal authority to make necessary moves; systems designed for normal stability are poorly suited to crises that require immediate aggressive action.
- (p202; printed 134) A private-sector solution (a healthier institution acquiring the failing one) is the best option when authority to inject capital is lacking.
- (p204; printed 136) Interest-rate and liquidity policies that affect the whole economy are very inefficient for sector-specific debt problems; macroprudential policies are more appropriate and should have been used in 2007.
- (p209; printed 141) Government guarantees, implicit or explicit, make risky assets look safer than they are, encouraging leverage and bad debt growth.
- (p209; printed 141) Nationalizing too-big-to-fail institutions on the brink is a classic, usually well-received move; with own-currency debt the government can eliminate default risk.
- (p210; printed 142) Rules and checks and balances have advantages but during crises what needs to be done may not be done swiftly enough; 2008 was full of close calls that required getting around the rules.
- (p213; printed 145) In investing it is as important to know when not to be confident and not to make a bet as it is to have an opinion -- hindsight makes bottoms look obvious when they were not.
- (p214; printed 146) A solvency problem can only be dealt with by providing more equity capital or changing accounting/regulatory rules.
- (p215; printed 147) Lehman was especially scary because of large and poorly understood interconnectedness.
- (p216; printed 148) Blanket protections are needed because the uncertainty itself makes everyone run from any entity that could go under.
- (p217; printed 149) Policy makers are constrained from explaining candidly during a crisis because candor can intensify the panic (Paulson).
- (p219; printed 151) Mark-to-market rules force fire-sale losses that strain capital and liquidity; accounting relief helps but hiding losses does not restore confidence either.
- (p220; printed 152) Guarantees work better than backstops -- when you say something is guaranteed, it is far more reassuring to investors (Paulson).
- (p220; printed 152) Quick thinking and creativity (e.g., using the ESF) are required to navigate regulatory and political minefields.
- (p221; printed 153) Policy debate in deleveragings classically takes on populist overtones; arguing over who should bear the costs is counterproductive -- save the patient first, decide who pays later.
- (p222; printed 154) Legislatures act only at the edge of the precipice; regulators therefore need broad emergency authorities rather than relying on Congress.
- (p223; printed 155) Among the most important aspects of managing a crisis is having wise, knowledgeable decision-makers with authority to do whatever it takes.
- (p223; printed 155) Doing the unpopular but right thing (recapitalizing banks) is heroic and underappreciated; the capital programs prevented a catastrophe and returned a profit.
- (p224; printed 156) Political systems typically make the right decisions only after heated fighting and just hours before disaster.
- (p226; printed 158) Policy makers must sometimes act at or beyond the edge of statutory authority (CPFF); fear of unintended consequences must yield to preventing a meltdown.
- (p229; printed 161) Price = spending / quantity sold; in crises prices move for positioning reasons (squeezes, rebalancing) unrelated to fundamentals, so volatility stays extreme.
- (p229; printed 161) A debt is a short cash position; when dollar liquidity tightens there is a dollar squeeze that supports the dollar until debtors default or get liquidity.
- (p231; printed 163) Central bankers in crises must choose between printing money to replace contracting private credit and allowing a big tightening; they inevitably choose to print, and that is when things change -- the speed of this step is what distinguished 2008 from 1930-33.
- (p233; printed 165) Investors lost money in 2008 because they held beta rather than alpha, beta concentrated in assets that do badly in bad times, risk and liquidity premiums rose, and 'alphas' had systematic pro-cyclical biases (hedge funds ~70% correlated with stocks).
- (p234; printed 166) Both in the 1930s and 2008 rates hit zero and printing followed; in both cases markets and the economy bottomed right after. QE is like a shot of adrenalin to a patient in cardiac arrest.
- (p240; printed 172) Investors in bear markets fall into three groups: those clobbered who cut risk at the bottom; those clobbered who held or added on blind faith; and the few who understood and sold high/bought low.
- (p240; printed 172) When market pricing already discounts terrible outcomes and the range of outcomes is enormous, it is prudent not to take much of a bet.
- (p241; printed 173) D-processes come along infrequently; the antidote (money printing and stimulus) had never been administered in this dosage.
- (p241; printed 173) As economic pain rises, populist calls to punish bankers are the norm and make saving the system harder; judging policy makers in this way is unfair -- it is battlefield medicine (Summers).
- (p242; printed 174) Tops are made when the rate of buying is unsustainable (people think prices will rise); bottoms when the rate of selling is unsustainable (most people bearish).
- (p242; printed 174) Had things gone worse or longer, the capitalist and democratic system would have been at risk of breaking.
- (p245; printed 177) Reflations do not necessarily cause inflation because they can simply negate deflations; 'inflation' is an average of many things that behave differently (labor and productive assets vs commodities/gold).
- (p246; printed 178) Tighter laws are typical at the end of big debt crises; over ~25 years the hangover fades, euphoria returns, the laws are flouted and new forms of leverage create the next crisis.
- (p247; printed 179) Merely slowing or ending QE is a tightening; the flow of purchases matters more than the stock of money.
- (p248; printed 180) Europe followed the same sequence: policy makers did not believe a crisis would come until it did, then made the rookie mistakes of leaning on deflationary levers like austerity, not printing, and not protecting systemically important entities.
- (p250; printed 182) All deleveragings are due to declines in private credit growth that require more central-bank money creation and larger deficits; austerity has never worked when the problems were big; every deleveraging studied led to waves of money creation, deficits and devaluations.
- (p250; printed 182) The first two years of a recovery from a deleveraging look like a cyclical 'sweet spot' (slack plus low inflation) but depend on printing and fiscal stimulus.
- (p251; printed 183) The US rescue stands out for fast recapitalization, fast unwinding of emergency programs, and good financial returns; the crisis ended (real GDP regained) in 2Q 2011 with slack still present.
