**Summary of the criteria for a believable person (as the book defines them):** (i) ≥3 successful repetitions of the specific thing in question; (ii) can lay out the causal reasoning when probed; (iii) scored per attribute/domain, not globally; (iv) evidence is a cumulative, openly inspectable track record (plus test results and peer ratings), not self-report; (v) confidence must be declared.

### B2. Believability-weighted decision making — the procedure

Assembled from Part II 5.10 (pp. 181–182), Part III introduction (pp. 223–227, 233), ch. 5 (pp. 261–268), ch. 6 (pp. 269–273), and the Appendix (pp. 370–371):

1. **Prerequisite formula** (p. 226): Idea Meritocracy = Radical Truth + Radical Transparency + Believability-Weighted Decision Making. Three behavioral requirements (p. 233): put honest thoughts on the table; have thoughtful disagreement; abide by agreed ways of getting past disagreement.
2. **Everyone may give input** (p. 267), but input is weighted by evidence: track records, test results, and other data.
3. **Pick the relevant qualities** for the decision (e.g., subject-matter expertise and ability to synthesize in the 2012 ECB example, p. 262); weights are attribute-specific.
4. **Poll** through the Dot Collector: yes/no or numeric rating (p. 371). The system shows **both the equal-weighted and the believability-weighted result** (pp. 261, 371) — not just majorities but "which way the people whose views have the most merit voted".
5. **If the two results agree, proceed** (p. 261). If they diverge, revisit the disagreement; the tool flags "nubby questions" (p. 371) and alerts you automatically when you disagree with the believability-weighted majority, with guidance on steps to resolve it in an evidence-based way.
6. **Resolution hierarchy** (pp. 261, 267): if still at odds, the believability-weighted vote supersedes the Responsible Party's opinion in some kinds of decisions and not in others; quote: "Responsible Parties can overrule believability-weighted voting but only at their peril" (p. 267). An RP who bets against believable consensus is making a bold statement that the results will prove right or wrong — i.e., the override itself becomes a scored call.
7. **Common perils** to avoid (Part II 5.10, p. 182): over-valuing your own believability; failing to distinguish who is more or less credible.
8. **When you disagree with believable people** (p. 182): explore the merits of their reasoning; if you agree on the principles, apply them; if you disagree on the principles, work through that disagreement.
9. **Choose your believable people** (5.5 c, p. 267): about three believable people who care about the outcome and will disagree openly; compare the crowd's believability-weighted view with your own and ask why you differ (5.5 d, p. 267); if you cannot resolve it, suspend judgment and go with the vote.
10. **Empirical claim** (p. 262): the believability-weighted answer is "more likely to be right than either the boss's answer or an equal-weighted referendum" (p. 262).
11. **Efficiency constraints** (5.5–5.6, pp. 266–268): random polling of everyone is an unproductive waste; decision-seeking communications involve the most relevant (believable) people; educational communications can be broader; you need not form judgments about everything.
12. **Legitimacy** (5.7, p. 268; 6.1, p. 269): the fairness and consistency of the system outranks any individual's wish to win; principles cannot be set aside by mutual agreement.

### B3. The 5-Step Process (Part II ch. 2, pp. 124–130)

Goals → identify problems → diagnose root causes → design a plan → push through. Mechanism points: do one step at a time; be hyperrealistic in each; the loop is iterative; the plan should be written so others can measure progress against it (2.4 e, p. 129); establish clear metrics to check you are following the plan (2.5 c, p. 129); identify at which step a chronic failure occurs to see which ability is lacking (2.6 a, p. 130; also Part III 12.1 b, p. 333). Diagnoses should distinguish proximate causes (verbs, actions) from root causes (adjectives, qualities of people/design) (2.3 b, p. 128; Part III 12.1 g, p. 334).

### B4. Pain + Reflection = Progress (1.7, p. 109; Part III 3.4, p. 251; Appendix, p. 372)

Pain signals a learning opportunity; reflect when it passes if not during; design the habit of going toward pain (1.7 a, p. 110). Organizationally: a culture must penalize covering up mistakes, so mistake logging is mandatory (3.4 c, p. 252). The Pain Button app operationalizes it (see C). Boxed on p. 252.

### B5. The machine metaphor and diagnosing the machine

- You are a designer/manager of a machine (design + people) that turns goals into outcomes; compare outcomes to goals and modify the machine (1.10 a–b, p. 112; diagram p. 113). The biggest mistake is not seeing yourself and others objectively (1.10 d, p. 115).
- Organization = machine with two parts, culture and people (p. 215); compare outcomes to goals constantly (10.1 a, p. 311); the manager as "organizational engineer" who builds process-flow diagrams and metrics (10.1 b, p. 311).
- Every case has two purposes: move toward the goal and train/test the machine; discuss at the machine level and the case level separately (10.2 a–b, p. 312).
- Diagnostic questions (12.1, pp. 331–334): Is the outcome good or bad? Who is responsible (the Responsible Party)? If bad, is the RP incapable and/or is the design bad? How should the machine have worked? Did it? What broke? Why (keep asking "why" until you reach a quality of a person or design)? Is the root cause a pattern? How should people/machines evolve as a result? Then: who should do what differently (12.1 a, p. 333); at which 5-step stage did it fail (12.1 b); which principles were violated (12.1 c); avoid Monday-morning quarterbacking — judge the decision on what was knowable then (12.1 d); distinguish quality of circumstances from quality of approach (12.1 e); capacity vs. capability (12.1 h, p. 334).
- Drill-down (12.4, pp. 335–337): Step 1 list problems specifically, naming people; Step 2 identify root causes by repeated "why"; Step 3 create a plan with owners, metrics, timelines; Step 4 execute and report planned vs. actual progress at least monthly, holding people publicly accountable.
- Perceiving problems: assign people whose job is to perceive problems, with independent reporting lines so they can report without fear of recrimination (11.2 a, p. 326); compare outcomes to the expected trajectory band (11.2 d, p. 326); "taste the soup" (11.2 e, p. 327); beware groupthink — nobody worrying is not evidence (11.2 c, p. 326).

### B6. Thoughtful-disagreement protocol

- Goal is to find which view is right, not to win (3.3, p. 137); open the exchange by agreeing on its mode — quote: "Would you rather I be open with my thoughts and questions or keep them to myself?" (fn. 26, p. 147).
- Two-minute rule: each party gets an uninterrupted two minutes to explain (p. 138; 4.4 i, p. 258).
- Ask whether you are arguing or seeking to understand, and decide by relative believability: the less believable party starts as student, the more believable as teacher; peers debate (3.2 g, p. 137; Part III 5.3, pp. 264–265). The student must try to understand the teacher before the teacher owes the student an explanation (5.3 a, p. 265).
- Decision making is two-step: take in information, then decide (3.2 b, p. 136; 5.1, p. 167).
- Surface areas of out-of-syncness in priority order; distinguish idle complaints from complaints meant to improve things; every story has another side (4.2 a–c, p. 255).
- Be open-minded and assertive at once; those in charge must explain their thinking openly and transparently; appeals go to a mutually agreed believable person or group (4.3 d, p. 256).
- Meeting mechanics (4.4, pp. 257–259): clear who runs it and whom it serves; be precise; decide what type of communication (debate vs. instruct); navigate levels; watch "topic slip"; enforce logic; conclude with to-dos, conclusions, or further work; leverage communications (FAQs, recordings).
- Escalation: if a one-on-one dispute stalls, escalate to a mutually agreed believable person higher in the chain; if a group stalls, the meeting owner takes a believability-weighted vote (6.3 b, p. 271). Default arbiter is the person you report to or a mutually agreed arbiter (fn. 38, p. 268).
- Everyone must raise their difference rather than hold it privately; the Dispute Resolver makes this explicit (pp. 255, 373).
- Timebox: small triangle of believable people; do not debate indefinitely (5.5 a, p. 266).

### B7. The ego barrier and the blind-spot barrier (3.1, pp. 133–135; 3.6, pp. 141–143)

- Ego barrier: subliminal need to be right/capable fights the higher-level logical self; the "two yous" (3.1 a–b, pp. 133–134).
- Blind-spot barrier: differences in wiring make each person systematically blind to some things; closed-mindedness is costly because you never see what you miss (3.1 c, p. 134).
- Remedies as mechanisms: use pain as a cue for reflection (3.6 a, p. 142); keep a written list of your blind-spot areas and do not decide in them without consulting others (3.6 c, p. 142); **if several believable people say you are wrong and you alone disagree, assume you are biased** — switch to question mode, compare believabilities, bring in a neutral party (3.6 d, p. 142); be evidence-based (3.6 f, p. 143); use evidence-based decision-making tools — a computer with a better track record than you (3.6 h, p. 143); know when to stop fighting and trust the process (3.6 i, p. 143).
- Organizational mirror: people will not recognize and compensate for their own blind spots, so design guardrails (13.7 a, p. 346).

### B8. Expected value and probability in decisions (5.6–5.7, pp. 178–180)

- Decision = probability × reward minus probability × penalty; worked example $100 reward at 60% vs $100 penalty at 40% → +$20 (p. 179). It is not always right to bet on the most probable outcome: a 20% chance of $1,000 against a $100 loss has EV +$120 (p. 179), provided you can cover the loss.
- Raising your probability of being right is valuable at every level: moving from 51% to 85% is worth seventeen times moving from 49% to 51% (5.6 a, p. 179).
- Knowing when not to bet is as important as knowing which bets to make; selective betting improves the track record (5.6 b, p. 180).
- Best choices have more pros than cons, not zero cons (5.6 c, p. 180).
- Weigh the value of additional information against the cost of not deciding (5.7, p. 180); do not mistake possibilities for probabilities (5.7 c, p. 180).
- Expected-value framing is reused for transparency exceptions (1.4 c, p. 240) and for how hierarchy allocates attention (5.3 b, p. 265).
- Leadership corollary: the leader should assess his own probability of being right logically rather than deferring to the average opinion or ignoring it (10.10 b, p. 320).

### B9. Open-mindedness tests — observable signs (3.5, pp. 140–141)

Seven behavioral cues of closed- vs open-minded people: (1) do not want their ideas challenged vs. curious about why there is disagreement; (2) make statements rather than ask questions vs. believe they could be wrong and ask genuine questions; (3) focus on being understood vs. see through others' eyes; (4) say "I could be wrong… but here's my opinion" as a formality vs. know when to state and when to ask; (5) block others from speaking vs. enforce the two-minute rule; (6) cannot hold two thoughts at once vs. hold conflicting views and weigh them; (7) lack humility vs. have a deep fear of being wrong. Practice tests in 3.2 a–g (pp. 136–137): accept not knowing; take in all information before deciding; do not worry about looking good; you cannot output without input; suspend judgment to empathize; seek the best answer rather than the best answer you can produce; be clear on arguing vs. understanding by believability.

### B10. Other epistemic mechanisms worth carrying over

- **Synthesis as dot-plotting through time** (5.3, pp. 169–173): categorize outcomes by type and quality; plot them over time; judge trajectories against an "acceptable" bar and an "excellent" bar; be imprecise ("by-and-large"); 80/20.
- **Levels** (5.4, pp. 174–177): keep conversations "above the line" and descend to detail only in service of the main point.
- **Principles → algorithms** (5.9, 5.11, pp. 181–186): slow down, write the criteria you used as a principle, refine when outcomes arrive; then encode principles so a computer can evaluate the criteria against data — "thinking → principles → algorithms" (p. 185). Test rules on historical data; use the machine as a GPS alongside your own judgment.
- **AI caution** (5.12, pp. 186–187): expert systems (rules you understand) vs. mimicking/data mining (rules you do not); machine-learned rules that cannot be explained are dangerous when the future differs from the past.
- **Learning well requires accurate synthesis** (5.1–5.2, pp. 167–168); **evidence-based culture** means decisions on the merits of track records and logic, in an "open-source" way (p. 225).
- **Bridgewater's self-reinforcing loop** (p. 227): independent thinkers → idea meritocracy → principles written down and encoded into computers → decisions by them → successes and failures → more principles → repeat.

### C2. Scoring mechanisms

- **Attribute-level scoring from many small observations**: each dot is an "at-bat"; evaluate on aggregates like batting average, never one event (9.5 c, p. 296). Patterns, not incidents, are the unit of judgment (3.3, p. 251; 9.6 d, pp. 297–298).
- **Process and outcome scored together over time** (9.7, p. 299): repeated bad reasoning plus bad outcomes is disqualifying; a single bad outcome is not.
- **Clear, impartial, predetermined metrics with consequences** (9.6 a, pp. 296–297): the clearer the rules, the less argument; metrics can be tied to an algorithm that "spits out consequences" (bonus points, pay), weighted by importance; formulaic outputs used with discretion and refined over time.
- **Compensation loosely tied to metrics** (8.6 b, p. 289) and paid for the person rather than the job (8.6 a, p. 289).
- **Rater-bias controls** (10.1 c, p. 312): check each grader's average grade to detect easy/hard graders; "forced rankings" (grading on a curve); metrics that allow independent grading across departments are especially valuable.
- **Self-report discounted** (9.3 f, p. 294; fn. 39, p. 309): asking everyone what share of results they were responsible for summed to 301 percent; "I think that I…" statements are discounted (5.4 c, p. 266).
- **Mandatory logging of errors with severity and owner** (Issue Log, p. 372); not facing up to a mistake is treated as one of the worst mistakes (3.4 c, p. 252).
- **Public progress accountability**: drill-down Step 4 reports planned vs. actual at least monthly and holds people publicly accountable (p. 337); transparency itself is used to "help enforce justice" because everyone can follow the discussion and the tape (1.4 a, p. 239).
- **Double-do over double-check** (13.5 l, p. 344): two people independently perform mission-critical tasks to produce two answers; differences reveal performance and ability; an auditor must be capable of doing the work.
- **Sampling instead of surveillance** (9.6 h, pp. 298–299): a statistically reliable sample of cases, with enough probing that people cannot hide.
- **Time limit on sorting**: six to twelve months to roughly assess; eighteen months for confidence (9.8 b, p. 301); then train, guardrail, or remove (9.9, p. 302).
- **Interviewer/assessor track records** (8.1 b, p. 285): capture data on the track records of the people making subjective interpretations.
- **The override as a scored bet** (5.5 b, p. 267): an RP who goes against the believability-weighted consensus is making a statement that results will prove right or wrong.

### C3. Incentive mechanisms

- Individual incentives must be aligned with the group's goals (Part II 1.5 a, p. 106); reward what works for the whole (1.4 b, p. 99).
- Loyalty to the mission, not to persons (2.1, p. 245); tough love (p. 221–223); pay north of fair (2.2 c–d, pp. 247–248).
- Everyone thinks like an owner and feels the consequences of their actions (10.7, p. 318); the ultimate Responsible Party is the one who bears consequences (7.2, p. 281).
- Culture makes covering up a mistake worse than making one (3.1–3.5, pp. 250–252); "accurate/inaccurate" replaces "blame/credit" (3.2 a, p. 251).
- Radical transparency deters bad behavior because little can hide (1.4, pp. 238–239); exceptions are few and justified as expected-value calculations (1.4 c, p. 240); "public hangings" to deter bad behavior (13.9 e, p. 348).
- Controls against dishonesty assume some fraction of people will cheat if they can (13.9, p. 347): investigate and let people know you investigate (13.9 a); auditors reporting outside the audited unit with procedures kept secret — one of the few transparency exceptions (13.9 b, p. 348); audit the auditors; beware rubber-stamping (13.9 c).
- Conflicts of interest: most people operate in their own interest while pretending otherwise, so beware advice from people who gain from it (2.4, p. 248).

## (d) Governance mechanisms

**Who decides.** Decisions sit with Responsible Parties (RPs), chosen by believability for the goal, outcome, or machine at hand (7.1 a, p. 281); the ultimate RP is whoever bears the consequences (7.2, p. 281); everyone reports to someone (7.2 a, p. 281). It must be known in advance where ultimate decision authority lies, i.e., how far the power of argument carries relative to assigned authority (4.2, p. 255). Decision rights and vote weights must be explicit; quote: "Make sure it's clear how much weight each person's vote has" (16.1 f, p. 360). The right to complain, advise, or debate is not the right to decide (6.2, p. 270), and when challenging a decision one must consider the broader context the decision serves (6.2 a, p. 270).

**How authority maps to believability.** Opinions are weighted by evidence of believability (p. 267); believability-weighted voting can supersede an RP in some decision types; where it does not, the RP may overrule "but only at their peril" (p. 267). Management itself is subject to believability (10.3 c, p. 314). Yet merit cannot be the only factor in assigning authority — owners' vested interests also count, and the resolution is explicit, not implied (16.1 a, p. 359). Hierarchy is retained but is not the basis of truth: those higher in the hierarchy must get in sync with those lower about what makes sense (5.3 b, p. 265); appeals go to a mutually agreed, more believable arbiter (4.3 d, p. 256; fn. 38, p. 268).

**Guarding against the tyranny of the boss.**
- Everyone has the right to understand what makes sense and the duty to speak up (1.3, p. 237); nothing said about a person that is not said to them (1.2 a, p. 236).
- Radical transparency: meetings recorded; people can follow the discussion and the tape, so justice is enforced by visibility (1.4 a, p. 239); those in charge must explain their thinking transparently (4.3 d, p. 256); probing is transparent, not private (10.6 i, p. 317).
- Principles are like laws; they cannot be ignored by mutual agreement, and the same standards apply to everyone (6.1, p. 269–270).
- The Dispute Resolver exists so that the person with more power cannot pull rank (p. 373).
- Governance must be more powerful than any individual; quote: "no one is more powerful than the system or so important that they are irreplaceable" (16.1 b, p. 359). Beware fiefdoms (16.1 c, p. 359).
- Structure: a board that selects and replaces CEOs and checks that the company operates by principles, independent of the CEO's reporting lines (p. 360 diagram: 7–15 board members, 1–3 chairmen, 1–3 CEOs, 5–7 cabinet members, a succession pyramid of ~50); co-CEOs rather than one (16.2, p. 361); assessors must have time to be informed, ability, and no conflict of interest (16.1 g, p. 360); decision makers must have access to information and be trustworthy with it (16.1 h, p. 361). Bridgewater's shift from an informal "Articles of Confederation" to a written "Constitution" with a judicial system (p. 358).
- Ultimately, power rules: if the people with power do not want principles, the system fails, so power must be given to people who value the principled system (6.6, p. 273); no rulebook substitutes for a great partnership (16.3, p. 361).

**Guarding against the tyranny of the majority.**
- Equal-weighted polling is reported but not followed when it diverges from the believability-weighted result (p. 262, 371); random polling of everyone is explicitly rejected (5.4 b, p. 266).
- Never let the idea meritocracy slip into anarchy — the right to argue is bounded by rules (6.4 b, p. 272); no lynch mobs or mob rule — crowds seek control and are emotional (6.4 c, p. 272).
- The leader should not simply do what the average person wants, nor ignore them, but surface the disagreement and resolve it on the merits (10.10 b, p. 320).
- Once a decision is made, everyone gets behind it even if they still disagree (6.4, p. 271); the fairness of the system outranks any individual's wish (5.7, p. 268).
- Emergency powers: "martial law" (suspending principles) only in rare, declared, temporary circumstances (6.5 a, p. 272); be wary of anyone arguing for suspension "for the good of the organization" (6.5 b, p. 273).

**Guarding against bad incentives.** Loyalty to persons cannot override truth (1.2 b, p. 237); exceptions to transparency are expected-value decisions with three narrow grounds (p. 240); auditors independent of the audited (13.9 b, p. 348); purchasers will not spend your money wisely (13.9 d, p. 348); believable-people triangles are chosen for caring about the outcome and willingness to disagree (5.5 c, p. 267); compensation tied to metrics only loosely so metrics do not become the goal (8.6 b, p. 289); checklists are not responsibility (14.3 a, p. 354); problem-perceivers get independent reporting lines so they can report without fear (11.2 a, p. 326).
