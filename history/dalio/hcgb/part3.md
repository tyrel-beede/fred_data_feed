### 1865–1945 (Ch. 9, plus flashbacks elsewhere)

- 1839 — China — First Opium War begins the "Century of Humiliation" (he calls it roughly 100 years, 1839 to end of WWII on p. 216; "110-year period" 1839–1949 on p. 268) — (216, 268).
- 1853 — Japan — Commodore Perry's fleet arrives; leads to collapse of the 250-year Tokugawa domestic order and the old monetary order — (290).
- 1861–65 — US — Civil War financed by debt that grew too great to repay. Federal debt rose from **2% of GDP to 40% of GDP**; **interest alone consumed over half the budget** (excluding Confederate debts, which defaulted). Dollar had been linked to gold at **$20.67/oz**; government suspended convertibility, printed greenbacks; gold in greenbacks rose to **≈$250/oz**; **inflation hit 80% in 1865** — [debt bust resolved by devaluation/default/monetary stimulation; "It was all classic"] — (188).
- 1865 — China — start of his "China Big Debt Cycles since 1865" chart (26 years after the Century of Humiliation began) — (268).
- 1868 — Japan — Meiji government replaces the shogunate; copies Western approaches; constitutional monarchy; modernization (parallel drawn to Deng's reforms ~100 years later) — (290–291).
- 1870–1914 — US — Second Industrial Revolution "productivity miracle": railroads, steel, electricity (Edison), telephone (Bell), oil, automobile. Wealth gaps → "robber barons", Gilded Age, left/right class conflict. No central bank; dollar fixed to gold by commercial banks, so busts could not be eased by printing — [classic post-war productivity/leveraging boom] — (188–189).
- 1873 — US — Panic of 1873; start of the Long Depression and national/regional panics lasting until 1896 — (189).
- 1893, 1907 — US — further debt-bust panics; gold standard becomes political issue (Bryan, "cross of gold"); Panic of 1907 prompts creation of the Federal Reserve in 1913 — (189).
- 1894–95, 1904–05 — Japan — defeats China, then Russia; becomes leading imperial power in Asia; annexes Korea 1910 — (190, 291).
- 1900–1914 — US/Europe — "all the classic late Big Cycle symptoms": overindebtedness; capitalist-right vs socialist/anarchist-left; capitalism vs Marxism; McKinley assassinated by an anarchist, Theodore Roosevelt moves left; Germany rising vs UK/France — [late Big Cycle] — (190).
- 1908 — Greece — debases coinage, expelled from an early European currency union — (193).
- 1912–1926 — Japan — parliamentary democracy; 1921 PM assassinated by a young nationalist; 1925 "Peace Preservation Law" — (291).
- 1914 — Europe — Franz Ferdinand assassinated; WWI begins. Outcome: US becomes world's richest and largest creditor (financed and supplied the war, entered late); UK and France weakened and indebted; Germany terribly indebted and defaults immediately on war loans; Austria-Hungary and Ottoman Empire broken up — (190–191).
- 1917–1922 — Russia — monarchy vs masses → civil war → Marxist-communist order; Soviet Union created 1922 — (191).
- 1918 — world — war ends; "a big pandemic happened"; winners design new order (League of Nations, which failed) — (191).
- 1918–~1930 — West — peace, inventiveness, debt- and equity-financed speculation; "Roaring '20s" (autos, airplanes, radio, TV, talkies, refrigerators, drugs) — [productive lending → bubble] — (191–192).
- 1929 — US — bubble bursts with debt defaults and stock crash → depression; changes monetary, political, geopolitical orders — [bust] — (192). Japan: after the 1929 crash the nationalist military seized control (291).
- 1931, 1937 — Japan — takes Manchuria (1931), more of China (1937); US/UK/Netherlands impose export restrictions, freeze Japanese assets, cut off oil — he says this is "similar to what's happening with the US-China conflict today" — (291).
- 1933 — US — FDR outlaws private gold ownership, defaults on gold convertibility, moves official rate from $1 = 1/20.67 oz to $1 = 1/35 oz (**≈40% devaluation**), imposes strict FX controls and foreign-account restrictions; interest rates hit 0% post-1933 and money printing begins (first of the two big waves) — [debt-crisis money printing; devaluation → big rallies in stocks and gold] — (192, 207, 221). Germany: Hitler defaults on Versailles reparations (191, 193).
- 1933–38 — world — the geopolitical resets after 2008 are "analogous to those that occurred in the 1933–38 period" — (254).
- 1935 — China — abandons silver standard for paper currency — (193).
- 1920s–30s — Spain, Italy, Japan, Germany — turn to demagogic leaders / rightist autocracies; squaring off against leftists; retreat from multilateralism, broken agreements, strongman unilateralism — "just as we are now seeing in the US and several other countries" — (193).
- 1937–1940 — Japan — all political parties dissolved; military autocracy — (291).
- 1939, 1941 — Germany attacks Poland; Japan attacks Pearl Harbor; pre-war maneuvers were economic warfare, asset freezes, military buildups — (194).
- 1945 — world — unconditional surrenders; new monetary, internal and external orders; "the one we are now in the late stages of" — (194). Japan: all Japanese money and debt destroyed; occupied/reconstructed by US 1945–1952; "JPN effectively defaults on wartime debt" (chart annotation) — (292, 314).

### 1945–1971 (Ch. 11; MP0)

- 1944 — US/UK/allies — Bretton Woods created; gold-linked system he labels **Monetary Policy 0 (MP0)**; dollars convertible to gold at fixed rate (central banks only); other currencies pegged to dollar at adjustable rates; US held ~**two-thirds of world gold**, ~**half of world GDP** — (213–215).
- 1945–1971 — US — MP0 lasts 27 years, containing **five short-term debt/economic cycles** as wiggles around an uptrend in debt/income — (214).
- 1945–49 — China — civil war Kuomintang vs CCP; communists win, KMT to Taiwan; Japan had taken Taiwan in 1895, returned 1945; both sides agreed on "one China" — (216, 271).
- 1949 — UK — managed **30% devaluation** of sterling, followed by a series of further devaluations; UK had colonies/bases in over 40 countries — [classic reserve-currency decline; "important to keep in mind when looking at the US now"] — (216).
- 1949–1976 — China — Mao/Zhou isolated communist era; Great Leap Forward, Cultural Revolution — (271–272).
- 1950–2000 — US — government debt service roughly flat vs revenue (debt levels flat/falling); interest payments rose slightly 1950–1990 as average rate rose, then fell 1990–~2022 — (205–206).
- 1956 — Egypt/UK — Suez: loyal holders of UK bonds sold; "artificial intelligence" coined (1956); first satellite 1957; internet foundations mid-1950s — (216–217).
- 1962 — US/USSR — Cuban Missile Crisis; markets did not fall as much as he (age 13) expected; both sides withdrew missiles (Cuba, Turkey) — lesson: markets care about cash flows, not headlines — (218).
- 1963 — US — JFK assassinated (Nov); brief market effect; "guns" (Vietnam) and "butter" (social programs) spending begins — (218).
- 1965 — France — de Gaulle openly calls for reform of the monetary system, seeing US gold running out; the run accelerates — (220). US labor share of revenue **68%** (229).
- 1965–66 — US — inflation rises to **3.8%**; Fed tightens; **yield curve inverts for the first time since 1929** — (217).
- 1967 — UK — another crisis and major devaluation; abandonment of UK debt as a storehold — (217).
- 1968 — US — peak inflation-adjusted S&P 500 price **for the next 25 years**; followed by **1969–70 recession** — (217).
- Late 1960s — US/UK — runs on both central banks; US gold reserves steadily decline; US overspent on Vietnam and "war on poverty"; Germany/Japan recovered, US balance of payments worsened — (219).
- 1971 — US/China — Kissinger's secret visit to China (1971); Nixon visits early 1972; UN General Assembly recognizes PRC as "the only legitimate representative of China" (1971) — (219, 270).
- **Sunday, Aug 15, 1971** — US — Nixon ends dollar-gold convertibility on TV: "That ended the monetary system"; immediate devaluation, higher inflation, easier debt payment. He was clerking on the NYSE floor, expected a crash; instead **Monday was the best day of the year, stocks up more than 3%** — [MP0 ends; MP1 begins] — (220–221).

Fed increased money supply **100%**; **M2 +180%**; **CPI +~140%**; **stocks +~30% nominal, −45% real**; **gold ≈10x**; 10-year Treasury holder lost **~40% real**; Baa corporate holders slightly negative real — [decade of debt monetization/restructuring; paid to be a borrower-debtor] — (224–225).

- 1979 — Iran — Shah overthrown, embassy seized, hostages; second oil shock; "began the conflict with Iran that remains with us" — (227). US — **Volcker appointed Fed chair (1979)**, tightest money and highest rates "since the birth of Jesus Christ" (Helmut Schmidt) — (230).

- 1982 — US/EM — EM debt crisis; Dalio "dead wrong" expecting it to sink US banks/markets; failed to anticipate capital flows into US and Fed/regulator protection of banks; resolved to become an expert on capital flows — (232).

- 1990 — Japan — 12% of population over 65, 69% working age — (294).

## (c) Cases: mechanism, stage sequence, lessons, analogies to today

### US 1865–1945 (Ch. 9, pp. 187–194)

**Mechanism.** War → unpayable debt → suspension of gold convertibility, printing, inflation (1865: debt 2%→40% GDP, inflation 80%, gold $20.67→≈$250) → debt burdens reduced → post-war productivity miracle (1870–1914) financed by debt and equity → wealth gaps (robber barons) and left/right conflict → no central bank, gold-fixed dollar, so busts (1873, 1893, 1907) were long and deep → Fed created 1913 → late-cycle symptoms 1900–14 (overindebtedness, capitalism vs Marxism, rising Germany/Japan) → WWI → new order with US as top creditor → Roaring '20s bubble → 1929 bust → 1933 FDR gold default/≈40% devaluation/FX controls, rates to 0%, first big money-printing wave → 1930s hard-right/hard-left fights, strongman unilateralism, Versailles defaults → economic warfare, asset freezes, military buildups → WWII → 1945 new order.

**Stage sequence he assigns.** (1) post-war debt bust resolved by devaluation; (2) productivity boom and leveraging; (3) bubbles/busts with big wealth gaps; (4) "all the classic late Big Cycle symptoms" (1900–14); (5) world war and new order (1918); (6) repeat: peace/inventiveness (1920s) → bubble (1929) → bust → money printing and devaluation (1933) → internal extremism and international squaring off (1930s) → war (1939–45) → new order (1945), "the one we are now in the late stages of" (p. 194).

**Lessons.** Too much debt relative to money forces governments to break promises via (a) more money/credit, (b) restructuring, (c) blocking free ownership/movement of hard money (p. 188, 192). Without a central bank, busts are not cushioned (p. 189). Weak countries get exploited by rivals (p. 193). Gold-standard breakdowns (1865, 1933) are classic and recur.

**Analogies to today.** 1930s turn to rightist governments, squaring off against leftists, retreat from multilateralism and rise of strongman unilateralism is "just as we are now seeing in the US and several other countries" (p. 193). The Big Cycle template "has been working in essentially the same way for thousands of years" (p. 185).

### US 1945–1971 — MP0 (Ch. 11, pp. 213–221)

**Mechanism.** Bretton Woods gold-dollar link with US holding ~2/3 of world gold; citizens barred from gold so savings go into debt assets; US overspends (Vietnam, war on poverty) and borrows; foreigners initially happy to accumulate dollars; Germany/Japan recover → US balance of payments deteriorates → paper claims far exceed gold → runs on US and UK central banks (de Gaulle 1965) → selling drives rates up and currency down → Aug 15, 1971 Nixon suspends convertibility → devaluation, inflation, easier debt repayment, stocks/gold rally. Exact repeat of 1933.

**Stage sequence.** New monetary/geopolitical/domestic order (1945) → early expansion with five short-term cycles → inflation 3.8% and first curve inversion since 1929 (1965–66) → 1968 real S&P peak for 25 years → 1969–70 recession → run on gold → break of the link (1971). UK as the parallel reserve-currency decline: 1949 (30%), 1956 Suez, 1967, 1976 IMF.

**Lessons.** Hard-money systems "never worked in the long term" because governments still over-create money and debt (p. 214). Red-dot: intolerably painful debt problems make central banks print and devalue money and debt vs other assets (p. 221). Markets move on cash flows, not headlines; most brinkmanship ends with leaders stepping back, but buy insurance against the rare blow-up (p. 218–219). Investors who've only known equity bull markets misunderstand regime change (p. 217).

**Analogies to today.** The UK's post-war devaluation sequence is "important to keep in mind when looking at the US now" (p. 216). Taiwan's chips today are even more important than oil was last cycle (p. 216). Small seeds (tech, China opening) grow into big forces (p. 219).

### US 1971–2008 — MP1 (Ch. 12, pp. 223–244)

**Mechanism.** Fiat money steered by interest rates. 1971–81: monetization (money +100%, M2 +180%, CPI +140%, gold 10x, bonds −40% real, stocks −45% real), two oil shocks, negative real rates (to −4%), labor share to 74%, left-leaning governments → voters' revolt → Volcker (1979) and Reagan/Thatcher/Kohl → 1980s high real rates, falling inflation and nominal rates, widening margins, falling taxes → strong developed-market stocks/bonds, EM debt busts (inflationary depressions, "lost decade", 1991 resolution) → Plaza Accord 1985 → Soviet collapse (cheap oil) → globalization, China's rise under Deng, internet → 1995–99 bubble → March 2000 burst (Nasdaq −80%) → 9/11 → easing → 2006–07 mortgage/bank bubble → 2008 crisis and rates to 0%.

**Stage sequence.** MP1 era 1971–2008; two short-term cycles in the 1970s (1971–74 easy/tight; 1975–80 easy/tight), then four short-term cycles 1981–2008 with each cyclical high and low in rates lower than the last until 0% (p. 242). Sub-periods: 1971–82 stagflation/tightening/left-to-right; 1982–90 disinflation and levering up; 1990–2000 disinflation, bubble; 2000–08 bust → deleveraging → releveraging → bubble → GFC and monetization.

**Lessons.** Fiat money is "the easiest and subtlest way" for governments to relieve debt burdens and confiscate wealth (p. 225). Watch capital flows; diversify; be humble (1982 mistake, p. 232). Decades tend to be opposites of their predecessors (p. 195, 231). Real yield + yield curve determine whether to be a debtor or creditor (p. 203). "Everything seems bigger up close" (p. 239). Great companies ≠ great investments at any price; bubbles burst when central banks tighten (p. 241). Bridgewater rule: with a debt contraction and short rates near 0%, bet on a bad contraction until policy turns very stimulative as in March 1933 (p. 242). Policy prescription: target ~2% real 10-year yield (±1%) and a curve with short rate ~1% below / ~70% of the long rate (p. 204).

**Analogies to today.** The China-funds-US-deficits dynamic repeated the Japan-funds-US pattern (p. 244); the private-credit market today is "the modern-day version of the junk bond market of the late 1970s and early 1980s" (p. 264); Congress was far more bipartisan then than now (p. 244); Clinton's surplus is a case to remember for handling debt (p. 241).

### US 2008–2020 — MP2 (Ch. 13, pp. 245–254)

**Mechanism.** Mortgage-led deleveraging spreads to banks, companies, individuals; unemployment 10%, stocks −50%; rates hit 0% so MP1 is exhausted; Fed prints and buys debt (QE) — first such move since 1933; central bank becomes the big creditor, willing to run losses/negative net worth; quick response → quick rebound; asset prices up, yields down; benefits the rich; bailouts breed resentment; imports and technology hollow out the middle class → populism of right and left → Trump 2016 → aggressive, nationalist, protectionist policies "analogous" to pre-WWI/WWII; unemployment to 3.5% by end-2019; COVID 2020. Europe followed the template, with Greece unable to print: a 9-step playbook (deep debt → private crisis → government squeeze → selling/tightening/austerity, short rates >70% → ECB printing → (CB losses not seen) → Troika restructuring ~50% of GDP in 2012 → bank run, new taxes, capital controls 2015 → stabilization within the euro).

**Stage sequence.** MP2 2008–2020 with two short-term cycles, each with more debt creation and monetization than the prior (p. 248). Monetization is "symptomatic of being in the late phase of the long-term debt cycle" (p. 246); private-sector deleveraging with government levering up is "typical of the beginning of the Big Cycle's late stage" (p. 197).

**Lessons.** When rates hit zero the central bank must replace inadequate private demand for debt (p. 245–246). The 2008–20 recovery kicked the public-debt can down the road (p. 249). Countries that can't print (Greece) take the explicit-restructuring route, "usually the more painful, drawn-out path" (p. 254). Central bank losses arise when it holds fixed-rate bonds financed by floating reserves and must raise rates (p. 253).

**Analogies to today.** Political shifts after 2016 are analogous to the pre-WWI/WWII periods and to the early 1930s (p. 250–251); the 1933–38 geopolitical resets (p. 254).

### US 2020–now — MP3 (Ch. 14, pp. 255–266)

**Mechanism.** Pandemic → coordinated fiscal deficits monetized (MP3), chosen because MP1/MP2 no longer reach most people; debt sales far exceeded free-market demand so the Fed, banks and Japanese institutions bought; 10-year to 0.5% nominal / −1% real; bubble indicator 75%; paper wealth $32T→$99T; inflation surge (plus supply chains and conflict) → Fed tightening and balance-sheet runoff (yields to >4% nominal / 2.5% real; bubble indicator 35%; bubble stocks −75%; wealth −12% nominal/−18% real; FAANG −45%, Nasdaq −33%) → economy not hurt much because the government, not the private sector, holds the debt and the Fed eats the losses → inflation falls, prices stay high, modest easing → AI as the new hot thing; private credit keeps spreads low → 2024 Trump victory and mandate; five forces all intensifying.

**Stage sequence.** Short-term cycle: easing 2020 → inflation → tightening late 2021 → "relatively modest easing" (p. 260). Long-term: MP3 "late in the long-term debt cycle" (p. 256); the US is "in the leveraging-up phase of the long-term debt cycle" and has "not yet entered the deleveraging part" (p. 197); ~two-thirds through the 13th short-term cycle since 1945 (p. 197); domestic order/disorder cycle at "the same stage it last was in the 1930s" (p. 266); "near the end of these orders and our current Big Cycle" (p. 213).

**Lessons.** "Nearly every sacrosanct rule is inevitably tested by reality and starts to break down later in the Big Cycle" (p. 255). Debt service at 100% of revenue projected to 150% requires belief in rollover, new sales and no holder selling (p. 199). Foreign and bank holders have become sellers; future demand depends on US conduct, sanctions risk, returns and prominence (p. 206–207). Fed MTM losses ($700B, 2.5% GDP) are a red flag that becomes a problem if US debt is sold, forcing either intolerable rate rises or printing/devaluation, and threatening central-bank independence (p. 208–209).

**Analogies to today.** "This looks quite like the 1930s period" (p. 266); 2020 bubble analogous to Nifty Fifty, Japan 1989–90, dot-com (p. 262); US-China squaring off "looks a lot like what we saw in the 1930s" (p. 280).

### China 1945/49–now (Ch. 15, pp. 267–288)

**Mechanism.** Century of Humiliation (1839–1949) shapes leaders' psyche; 1945–49 civil war wipes out old debts ("PRC repudiates previous debts"); Mao's isolated communism 1949–76; Soviet threat drives 1972 US-China friendship; Deng 1978 reform/open door → cheap manufacturing exports, lending earnings back to US → trade/capital imbalances; 1989 capital markets begin; 2008 crisis and G20 stimulus → China ramps debt from 2009 (real estate, local governments, companies; one-child demographics; quantity-over-profit lending) → Xi 2012 reform/anti-corruption then consolidation; 2015 "2025 plan"; 2017–18 conflict in earnest; COVID; 2021 debt bubble bursts; Oct 2022 third term with Mao-like turn; five forces now mostly threatening. Per-capita income 20x since 1984, life expectancy +12 years, poverty 81% → <1% (p. 276). Debt/GDP ≈290% (chart p. 269).

**Stage sequence (his six steps, p. 273–274).** (1) classic unproductive communist country → (2) effective "socialist market economy" → (3) development of capital markets and its version of capitalism → (4) classic debt bubble → (5) classic debt bust of the own-currency, domestic-creditor type → (6) classic great power conflict. Current position: deleveraging since 2021, "in the late part of the Big Debt Cycle that is most analogous to Japan in the 1990s" (p. 282); non-central-government debt excessive so central government/central bank must help (p. 284); "pushing on a string" (p. 286–287).

**Lessons.** Local-currency, domestically held debt allows a "beautiful deleveraging," which China has been slow to engineer and Japan failed to do (p. 282, 287). Renminbi isn't a reserve currency, so not an effective storehold (p. 284). Nominal bond rates near zero → unconventional fiscal/monetary policies likely (p. 286). Liquidity rising without a real rebound = pushing on a string (p. 287). Real yields ≈0.5%: unattractive normally, attractive vs deflation, unattractive vs US (p. 285). Non-debt-burdened sectors flourish; "Chinese assets are now very cheap" (p. 288). Chinese leaders know the Big Cycle well (p. 270).

**Analogies to today.** China now ≈ Japan 1990s (p. 282, 284, 287). US-China squaring off ≈ 1930s (p. 280). Chinese leaders view Taiwan as more intrusive than Americans viewed Cuba in the 1960s (p. 269). Both countries face the same five issues in parallel lists (p. 279–280). Xi's "100-year storm" (p. 278).

### Japan (Ch. 16, pp. 289–317)

**Mechanism.** Pre-1945: Perry 1853 → Meiji 1868 → wins vs China/Russia → 1912–26 democracy → debt crisis + dysfunction → hard-right military takeover → sanctions/asset freezes/oil embargo → Pearl Harbor → defeat; all money and debt destroyed. 1945–90: rebuilt to #2 economy, huge debt-funded bubble bursting 1989–90. 1990–2013: did the opposite of a beautiful deleveraging (no restructuring for nine years, no rates below growth/inflation for 23 years, zombie institutions, rigid cost structures) → deflation and stagnation; aging. 2013–: Kuroda/Abe three arrows → massive monetization (BoJ >90% of GDP of JGBs), deficits 5% of GDP, rates 0.9% below growth and 1% below inflation, yen depreciation → debt service falls, ex-BoJ debt falls, government net worth improves in dollars, workers 58% cheaper vs US, FDI rises, equities cheapen → the cost borne by bondholders and savers (JGBs −45% vs UST, −60% vs gold; yen holders −29%/−84%; Japanese 60% poorer per capita in dollars).

**Stage sequence.** Two Big Debt Cycles since 1870 (chart p. 292): run-up 1930–45 → wipeout/default → low debt until 1970 → bubble → 1989–90 bust → ratio rising to ≈250%+. Post-1990: (i) 1990–2013 "exactly what not to do" (p. 293); (ii) 2013–2019 healing via monetization (p. 294); (iii) now: stimulative "pushing on a string" with large BoJ balance-sheet risk if rates normalize (p. 299, 315–317). Debt service as % revenue has risen toward, but stayed below, the 150% level at which past busts occurred (p. 314).

**Lessons and analogies.** See section (d). He offers Japan as the case for US, China and Europe (p. 293), and tells Chinese policymakers to read it (p. 288).

## (d) The Japanese case — every lesson and number

**Framing (p. 289, 293).** A heavily indebted reserve-currency country; policy makers "did the exact opposite" of a beautiful deleveraging for more than two decades: no debt restructuring for nine years; did not drive rates below inflation and nominal growth for 23 years. Japan had the capacity for a beautiful deleveraging because almost all debt was yen-denominated, nearly all debtor-creditor relationships were between Japanese parties, and Japan was a net creditor to the world.

**What went wrong 1990–2013 (p. 293–294).** No restructuring → zombie institutions; rigid employment/cost policies; rates not low relative to nominal growth and inflation; no meaningful monetization until deflation and near-zero rates in 1995; NPLs not addressed until 1999 (nine years after bubble popped), when the government forced bank restructuring and injected capital; no monetization that pushed rates significantly below growth/inflation until 2013; continuous deflation and stagnation until mid-2013; "psychological overhang of 23 years of debt depression." Demographics: 1990 — 12% over 65, 69% working-age; now — 29% over 65, 59% working-age.

**What went right from 2013 (p. 294–295).** Kuroda (BoJ) + Abe late 2012/early 2013 "three arrows": (1) increase money supply, (2) boost central-government spending, (3) economic and regulatory reform — "classically the best policies to negate deflationary, depressionary forces." Results 2013–2019: no deflation; growth 0.9%/yr; healing began though psychology lingered. Tools: extremely large debt monetization; deficits averaging 5% of GDP; BoJ now holds government bonds worth more than 90% of GDP; rates pushed 0.9% below nominal growth and 1% below inflation on average; yen depreciated.

**Investor's return arithmetic (p. 294–295, 298, 301–302, 309, 311).**
- JGBs lost 45% relative to US bonds and 60% relative to gold (2013–2025 chart p. 298: local-currency real return ≈+10% peak then ≈−6%; unhedged vs USA bond ≈−45%; vs gold ≈−60%).
- Average Japanese interest rate ≈2.2% below the US rate; yen depreciated ≈5.5%/yr in real terms vs the dollar; the ≈−45% relative return was "almost entirely attributable to currency depreciation" because the lower carry was offset by ≈20% price gains from falling yields. Japanese inflation averaged 1.1%/yr vs US 2.7%.
- Japanese bondholders lost a total of 6% in real terms (real yields generally negative), 45% vs US bonds, 60% vs gold; "the big losers were the Japanese debt holders, including the Japanese central bank" (p. 298).
- Since 2000: yen down 30%; a US investor holding yen (forgoing the dollar rate) is down 84%; unhedged Japanese bonds vs US bonds ≈−70%; unhedged Japanese equities vs US equities ≈−67% (p. 301). Table p. 302 bottom lines: "Return of Holding Yen in a Bank, Converting at End: −29%"; "Return of Holding Yen Financed with Borrowed USD, Converting at End: −84%."
- Yen bonds vs dollar bonds and yen vs USD: in both, yen holdings "lost more than half of the value. This is not dissimilar to a default" (p. 309). Charts (idx to 2001): Japan vs USA unhedged bond diff ≈−50% by 2024; Japan vs USA FX returns ≈−70% by 2024.
- Hedged lens (p. 311): JGB 10-yr cumulative real return in yen (idx 2001) rose to ≈+35% around 2020, worsening since COVID-era inflation to ≈+25%; in USD terms ≈+50% in 2012 → ≈−50% by 2024.
- Red-dot principle (p. 295): don't own government bonds when there are extreme amounts of debt monetization.

**BoJ balance-sheet risk (p. 299–300, 317).** Losses "will be very large" if real and nominal yields rise to reasonable levels (e.g., 2% and 3%). Scenario of a 3% rise in real rates (from −0.3% to 2.7%): BoJ MTM loss ≈30% of GDP and negative cash flow ≈−2.5% of GDP; government deficit widens from ≈4% to ≈8% of GDP over 10 years from interest costs (excluding CB losses); government debt would surpass its post-WWII peak, rising from 220% to 300% over 20 years; combined cash-flow need of CB plus government ≈5–6% of GDP per year — "huge" — to be met by issuance, printing (equivalent to another QE round) and/or deficit cuts; resolution would require "even greater write-downs in debt and devaluations of the currency," leaving Japanese relatively poorer until competitive enough for a new cycle. So far the BoJ has stayed profitable (no big sell-offs; low interest on excess reserves); Fed losses were moderate (up to 0.5% of GDP), but the BoJ monetary base is ≈5x the Fed's so losses "could be much more meaningful."

**Winners/losers and prices (p. 300–306).** Non-tradables (local wages, services, housing) ≈ no price rise in yen since 2000 and large deflation in global-currency terms; rent/wage affordability barely moved (0.6 months of labor for a typical ≈700 sq ft apartment); tradables way up (key commodities >3x in yen; tradable commodities up 2–10x, 3x average). Dollar GDP per capita: Japanese were richer than Americans, now ≈60% poorer (chart: JPN ≈$32K vs USA ≈$82K, 2024). Aggregate Japanese CPI +10% since 2000 vs US +90%; dollar itself down ≈50% vs goods in 25 years. Worker: wages flat in yen at just under ¥400,000/month; ≈$3,500/month then vs ≈$2,500 now; 13 oz of gold/month then vs 1 oz now. Cost of a Japanese worker vs an American worker down 58% since 2013; worker compensation in yen +0.8%/yr since 2013. Domestic car: 8 → 9 months of labor; convenience-store lunch: 10 → 16 minutes of work (+60%); theme park: 1/3 → 1/2 day of labor. The 1980–90 lead-up was the inverse: non-tradables inflation +40%, yen +70%. Full price table (p. 302) in section (e).

**The five dynamics (p. 306–312).** (1) Government deficit spending floods the private sector with cash, aiding private deleveraging (public debt rose ~1990–2020 as private debt fell; Japan ended with the highest government debt of any major country). (2) Central bank monetizes to keep long rates low, cut debt service and boost demand; debt ex-CB falls as % GDP. (3) Currency depreciation taxes foreign holders of unhedged bonds and domestic investors who stayed home, and cuts the debt burden in FX and gold. (4) Domestic savers taxed to a lesser degree (hedged JGB holders "have done OK"); weak economy kept yen weakness from becoming domestic inflation (import prices rose, services CPI flat). (5) Country becomes more competitive as assets and factors of production cheapen: home prices in USD (idx 2001) ≈−25%, productivity-adjusted wages in USD ≈−50%, FDI inflows up to ≈1% of GDP, forward P/E from ≈70 (early 1990s) to inexpensive vs US (≈15 vs ≈22), with cheapening reversing in the past few years.

**Appendix numbers (p. 314–317).** Two Big Debt Cycles since 1900; wartime default; govt debt ≈250% with 1990 and 2024 IMF projections; debt service % revenue busts when >150%, now risen toward but below 150% (≈120%); post-1950 "pushing on a string": nominal rates below 0%, real rates slightly negative, big printing, slightly upward-sloping curve; Baa spreads ≈1% US, 0.6% Japan; inflation below target, in and out of deflation; Japanese inflation-linked bonds since 2004.

**Conflict note (p. 306).** No big internal or external conflicts in this period, "though Japan is now preparing for war with China (though it doesn't want it) as the United States' most important ally in the region."

## (g) Red-dot principles (paraphrased, with page)

1. To understand big events, don't focus on small ones; precision-seekers miss the most important things; pay attention to the big things. (p. 185)
2. Everything happens for reasons; build a cause/effect template that explains the past and matches the present, and resolve discrepancies when they appear. (p. 185)
3. When debt is too large relative to the money needed to service it, governments break their promises through some mix of (a) creating more money and credit, (b) reducing debt (restructuring), (c) restricting free ownership and movement of hard money; a run from bad money to good money follows, which governments try to stop by banning good money. (p. 188; repeated p. 192 and p. 219–220)
4. When countries are weak, opposing countries exploit their weakness for gains. (p. 193)
5. Central banks stimulate by cutting rates and/or creating money and credit, extending the expansion while raising debt relative to income; when they can no longer cut rates they print money and buy debt, especially government debt, letting debtors keep borrowing until debt assets and liabilities become too great to balance, at which point restructuring and/or monetization must occur. (p. 196)
6. The interest rate vs inflation relationship matters: rates high relative to inflation reward saving and lending; rates low relative to inflation reward borrowing and holding inflation-benefiting assets. (p. 199–200)
7. The yield curve matters: short rates high relative to long rates mean tight money and make holding/lending cash more attractive than borrowing and investing in other assets. (p. 203)
8. When a debt problem is intolerably painful, central banks will "print money" and distribute it to make debts easier to pay, devaluing money and debt relative to other assets. (p. 221)
9. "Everything seems bigger up close." (p. 239)
10. Great companies in great industries are not automatically great investments because price matters; bubbles form when such thinking is leveraged with borrowing and typically burst when central banks tighten and rates rise. (p. 241)
11. Don't own government bonds when there are extreme amounts of debt monetization. (p. 295)

(Related non-red-dot maxims: the real bond yield is "the most important number to watch in the financial world" and the best indicator of whether to be a borrower-debtor or a lender-creditor (p. 200–201); the 10-year rate and its two parts are "the most important governor of all capital markets" (p. 202); markets react to cash flows, not headlines (p. 218); buy insurance against improbable, unacceptable losses (p. 219); watch capital flows, diversify, be humble (p. 232); decades tend to be opposites of their predecessors and crowd expectations at decade-ends are the time to bet against the crowd (p. 195).)
