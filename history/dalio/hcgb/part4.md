## (b) INDICATORS (ch. 17) — every gauge and table row

Framing (p. 325–326): the dashboard table shows his most important (not all) indicators across major countries "as of my writing this in March 2025" (p. 325). It covers existing and projected debt and debt service, plus reserve-currency status because being a reserve currency is a major risk mitigator. His narrative reads: the US has very large central-government debt and little liquid savings/reserves but the dominant reserve currency, which the US is itself undermining — its well-being "hinges on maintaining its existing reserve currency status" (p. 326). Japan: very large debt, own-currency, relatively large FX reserves. China: relatively big debt (general government + LGFVs), own currency, big reserves, currency not widely accepted as a storehold of wealth, foreign use of its capital markets falling fast. Singapore, Norway, Saudi Arabia: good income statements and balance sheets with more liquid assets than debt (p. 326).

Narrative readings attached to them:
- Long-term government risk "very high": current and projected debt, debt service, new-debt sales and rollovers are the highest ever; he judges the US government's debt situation "to be nearing the point of no return" (p. 330), meaning debt and debt-service levels near those that cannot be reduced without big losses to investors because a self-reinforcing "death spiral" (borrowing to service debt while rates rise on perceived risk) sets in.
- Short-term government risk "low": inflation and growth moderate, credit spreads low, real rates high enough for lenders but not too high for borrowers, private-sector balance sheets good enough to tax if needed. But if demand for new debt sales and rollovers falls off and/or holders sell, this gauge "can change very quickly—e.g., overnight" (p. 330).
- Fed long-term risk: elevated because (a) Fed holdings of government debt are high, (b) Fed losses are the highest ever, (c) Fed net worth is poor — but the numbers are not yet large; "the long-term risk is small but is in a place where it could accelerate very quickly" (p. 332). Fed short-term risk low because the economy and markets are near equilibrium: neither high rising inflation nor deflation; the Fed is not monetizing but slowly shrinking holdings; no destabilizing currency moves (p. 332).
- Two big red flags to watch (p. 333): (1) another round of QE to add liquidity and force real rates down; (2) the central government gaining control over the central bank. Either would signal the real value of money and debt is at great risk.
- Footnote 51 (p. 331): the central-bank risk gauge is built from (1) size of exposures; (2) balance-sheet size and cash-flow sensitivity to rate changes (profitability now and if rates move adversely); (3) balance-sheet strength, e.g. months of reserve sales before running out; (4) the currency/debt's value as a storehold of wealth (reserve status, track record); (5) the country/currency's share of world reserves, trade, capital flows and capital markets.

## (c) The 3% 3-Part Solution (ch. 18, pp. 335–358)

**Target (p. 335):** cut the US federal budget deficit to **3% of GDP**, from the CBO's projected **~6% of GDP**. Cuts come from **three sources**: spending cuts, tax increases and interest-rate cuts — "with interest rate cuts being the most impactful." If the president and Congress agree to the target and to a bipartisan fallback mechanism, the odds of the US going broke fall greatly.

**Diagnosis (p. 336):** policy makers work bottom-up (which cuts/taxes) instead of top-down (how big the total must be, then which of the three levers). They should (1) agree top-down on the size of the deficit cut as % of GDP needed to stabilize debt, and (2) agree a **fallback plan** that triggers automatically if they cannot agree on particulars — e.g., equal percentage cuts to all spending and equal percentage increases to all taxes that together hit the target (pp. 336, 340, 348). Later a bipartisan fiscal commission can propose better alternatives (p. 348).

**What the 3% cut does to the debt path (p. 337; chart "USA Central Govt Debt Level (% Govt Revenue)", Current Path (CBO) vs 3% Plan):** debt burden about **17% lower in 10 years** than the CBO path (**$9 trillion** lower); **31% lower in 20 years** (**$26 trillion** lower).

**Single-lever arithmetic (p. 338; detailed in table p. 358):** to stabilize debt relative to income using one lever alone: about an **11% increase in taxes**, or about a **12% cut in spending**, or about a **3% cut in interest rates**. Each alone is too large, so use two or three in combination.

**Relative power of levers (p. 338):** a 1-point fall in interest rates is about **4×** as effective at reducing debt-to-income over 20 years as a 1% rise in tax revenue; a 1% rise in tax revenue is **1.2×** as effective as a 1% cut in spending. Direct effects understate the total: rate cuts also lift asset prices (capital-gains receipts), stimulate the economy and raise inflation (more tax revenue); spending cuts and tax rises have negative second-order effects on growth and income taxes. Two takeaways: the biggest influence on the deficit is the Fed, not Congress; fiscal tightening and rate cuts offset each other on growth/inflation, so balanced well the deficit can be cut without unacceptable economic effects.

**His preferred package (p. 340):** roughly equal contributions — about a **4% cut in spending, a 4% increase in taxes, and a 1% cut in real interest rates** — **phased in over three years**; proposed as the bipartisan fallback. Who loses from lower rates: bondholders get lower real yields but gain on price and hold a safer bond; equities and other markets benefit (p. 340).

**Fiscal tightening with monetary easing (p. 339):** he expects the president and Congress to pressure the Fed to cut; if on the Fed board he would cooperate because a fiscal tightening (deficit-reducing, growth-negative) paired with monetary easing (deficit-reducing, growth-positive) "looks like a great plan." A significant enacted deficit reduction would itself trigger a bond rally and lower rates. The core imbalance is between an over-indebted government and a healthy private sector that was deficit-funded.

**If the Fed does not go along (p. 341):** achieve the 3% of GDP from spending cuts and tax increases only, each about **6%** (cut spending 6%, raise taxes 6%). Large by historical standards but manageable if balanced; if too depressing, the Fed would respond by cutting rates anyway. Chart p. 341 adds a third line, "3% Plan (Phased In over 3 Yrs)", which tracks close to the unphased 3% plan.

**Fly in the ointment (p. 342):** CBO numbers assume the 2017 Trump tax cuts expire; if extended as promised, the deficit is larger by an estimated **1.5% of GDP**, so the cut must be **over 4% of GDP** rather than ~3% to stabilize debt-to-income.

**Historical conditions for success (p. 342, tables pp. 343–347):** big fiscal tightenings (3% of GDP or much more) since 1960 went well when (1) growth was strong, (2) monetary/currency policy was easy, (3) debts were in a currency the central bank could print; they lowered bond yields and, where they weakened the economy, prompted monetary easings. Most successful US case: **1993–98**, deficit from 4% of GDP to a 1% surplus (5% of GDP improvement) ≈ cutting the deficit by **$1.5 trillion** today; his plan cuts by much less. The case-study medians (p. 343): length 4 years (all) / 5 (painless) / 4 (painful); change in primary structural deficit 5.7% / 5.4% / 6.3% of GDP; revenue share 59/59/54%, spending-cut share 41/41/46%; growth vs potential -0.3 / +0.9 / -2.3%; unemployment vs 10-yr avg 1.0 / 0.4 / 2.6%; slack -1.1 / -0.5 / -1.7%; inflation vs target -0.2 / -0.5 / +0.4%; avg bond yield vs start -0.6 / -1.2 / +0.6%; hard-currency debts 10 of 40 / 0 of 21 / 10 of 19; into strong economy 17 of 40 / 17 of 21 / 0 of 19; easier financial conditions 25 of 40 / 17 of 21 / 8 of 19; productivity reforms 23 of 40 / 10 of 21 / 13 of 19. (Full per-case tables in section f.)

**Timing condition — "Do it now! Do it counter-cyclically!" (p. 353–354):** better to cut deficits in good times than wait for a crisis in bad times; the US is near full employment, growth moderately strong, inflation a bit high, private balance sheets in good shape (because the government took on the burden); if not done now the problem compounds because the cycle is at the stage where more borrowing is needed to service existing debt.

**Which items (pp. 348–353):** he declines to rank specific cuts (fn 52: protect those least able to afford it and high-productivity functions like education; cut taxes/regulation that frees productive activity). CBO-derived menus (tables pp. 350–351) show a 3% of GDP goal is reachable with moderate tweaks: about 70% of non-interest spending is "mandatory"; the ~30% discretionary part (defense almost half) would need **15%** average cuts if the ~4% spending cut came only from it (p. 353); two Social Security changes (retirement age 67→70 phased; chained inflation) ≈ a tenth of required cuts (p. 352). CBO: **10% tariffs on all imports ≈ 0.6% of GDP**; if Elon Musk's $2 trillion cut claim is half true (**$1 trillion** via DOGE) that is **3% of GDP** (p. 349). Any grand bargain cutting the deficit to ~3% of GDP is acceptable to him; failure would reflect unreasonableness, and the electorate should hold Congress accountable for a debt-limit deal (p. 349).

**Other ideas (p. 354):** mark government assets to market, create a US sovereign wealth fund, explore a US-backed stablecoin. Big uncertainties (wars, productivity gains) argue for being more, not less, conservative.

**Appendix arithmetic (pp. 355–358):** status quo with CBO assumptions: US government debt over **130% of GDP in 20 years**; as a share of revenue **7.2×** government income, up from about **5.8×** now (p. 355); the grids (p. 356–357) show debt-to-income in 20 years for combinations of spending change (+6% to -6%, i.e. +1.2% to -1.2% of GDP) and income change (-6% to +6%, i.e. -1.0% to +1.0% of GDP) under CBO rates, real rates -1%, and real rates -2% (full grids in section f). Lever-by-lever table (p. 358): debt today 100% of GDP / 583% of revenue; CBO 2035 projection 118% of GDP / 648% of revenue; CBO nominal growth 3.9% (real 1.9%, inflation 2.0%); CBO effective nominal interest rate 3.5%; current rate (avg of 3M and 10Y) 4.5%. Rates alone: 1.0% required (-3.5 pts vs current; -2.5 pts vs CBO average). Inflation alone: 4.5% (+2.5 pts). Spending alone: 12% cut (= 47% of discretionary). Revenue alone: +11%. Text (p. 357): discretionary-only would need "nearly 50%" cuts; rates-only "around 3%".

**Red-dot principle attached (p. 342):** when large, fast-growing government debts require big deficit cuts: (1) cut by enough to fix the problem, (2) cut when conditions are good so the cuts are counter-cyclical, (3) keep monetary policy stimulative enough to hold the economy up.

**Also recommended (p. 367):** the 3% solution combined with a well-coordinated "beautiful deleveraging" balancing deflationary (fiscal tightening, restructuring) and inflationary (monetary easing, monetization) deleveraging.

## (e) Scenarios / paths with conditions

Dalio does not present a formal branch tree; these are the alternative paths he explicitly lays out.

1. **Debt path — controlled vs uncontrolled (p. 365, 367).** *Controlled:* the 3% 3-part solution plus a coordinated "beautiful deleveraging" that balances deflationary (fiscal tightening, restructuring) with inflationary (monetary easing, monetization) tools; outcome: risks greatly reduced. *Uncontrolled:* ~65% probability within 5 years / ~80% within 10 years of an unwanted major restructuring/monetization of reserve-currency debt; possible extreme measures (payment freezes, asset seizures, confiscatory taxes, capital/FX controls, defaults/extensions, new money type) rated as unlikely possibilities. Tell: significant selling of government bonds by existing holders (p. 366); red flags: new QE, government control of the Fed (p. 333).
2. **Fiscal-package paths (ch. 18).** (a) Fed cooperates: ~4% spending cut + ~4% tax increase + ~1% real-rate cut, phased over 3 years (p. 340). (b) Fed does not cooperate: ~6% spending cut + ~6% tax increase (p. 341); if that depresses the economy, the Fed cuts anyway. (c) Tax cuts extended: required cut >4% of GDP instead of ~3% (p. 342). (d) No deal: debt problem compounds; electorate should hold Congress accountable (pp. 349, 354). Fallback: automatic equal-percentage cuts/increases (p. 336).
3. **Appendix sensitivity matrix (pp. 356–357):** 20-year debt-to-income outcomes under three rate regimes (CBO rates; real rates -1%; real rates -2%) × spending (+6%…-6%) × income (-6%…+6%); CBO current path = 723% (see section f).
4. **Short-term cycle (pp. 363, 367, 370):** base case: near equilibrium now, two-thirds through the 13th cycle; risk case: simultaneous debt squeeze and downturn in 2–3 years (~2027–28).
5. **Markets (pp. 370–371):** consensus "American exceptionalism" meme is more than priced; alternative: other big problems ahead; bonds become relatively undesirable vs stores of value that hold purchasing power.
6. **Domestic order (pp. 375–378):** path toward more autocratic governance and hard-right shift; executive wins most power struggles; judiciary loses limits-of-power battles; risk of laws/punishments targeting opposition if conflict becomes severe; conditioned on how the bottom 60% fare.
7. **US-China (pp. 380–382):** base: no military war (MAD), ongoing diplomatic/cyber/trade war; China restrains itself outside its region but pursues Taiwan unification; alternatives: (a) Taiwan negotiated away by Trump for a big price (conditional on removing chip vulnerability), (b) a secretly developed overwhelming technology forces capitulation (not ruled out), (c) alliance realignment: US-Russia alignment isolating China; Europe-China closer than Europe-US.
8. **Europe (p. 379):** weak, easy prey; Russia likely an enemy; "America First" likely means the US does not defend Europe.
9. **Technology vs headwinds (pp. 386–387):** base: tech impact ~150% of the last 30 years but insufficient to overcome debt, internal conflict, external conflict, climate and demographic headwinds; the decisive variable is whether the other forces are managed well. Cooperation path: possible (the US could cut deficits to 3% of GDP) but "chances … are not good" (p. 388).

## (g) Red-dot principles (paraphrased)

1. p. 342 — When large, fast-growing government debts require big deficit cuts: cut by enough to fix the problem; cut when the economy is strong so the cuts are counter-cyclical; keep monetary policy stimulative enough to hold the economy up. (Restated in bold on p. 353.)
2. p. 366 — When there is too much debt relative to the money needed to service it, governments break promises through some mix of (a) creating more money and credit, (b) reducing debt (restructuring), and/or (c) blocking free ownership and movement of hard money such as gold; a run from bad money to good money follows, which governments try to stop by prohibiting good money from being freely held or moved.
3. p. 370 — There is always a dominant popular meme that is already in the price and bound to be wrong in some way; it comes from extrapolation and emotion; most investors judge an investment "great" without weighing its price, and most are long, often with leverage.
4. p. 372 — Globalization is an unstoppable force.
5. pp. 372–373 — A newly elected leader enjoys a honeymoon of optimism (e.g., the first 100 days); as promises prove hard to deliver, disappointment sets in, critics grow bolder, support wanes, and staying in power requires more extreme actions.
6. p. 375 — When democracies fail, autocracies come in.
7. pp. 375–376 — In times of disorder, financial, political and military power matter more than laws, and authoritarianism works better than weak, disorganized collectivism.
8. p. 376 — New leaders in periods of great conflict consolidate power; the more autocratic do so more forcefully.
9. p. 377 — In great conflicts, aggressive leaders eliminate opposition through threats and damaging actions, legal changes granting themselves special powers, and control of media for propaganda; if conflict becomes severe, laws and punishments targeting the opposition follow.
10. p. 378 — To succeed, a system must produce adequate conditions for most people.
11. p. 379 — The strong prey on the weak.
12. p. 379 — Alliances change fast when circumstances change quickly, because winning matters more than loyalty.
13. p. 384 — By and large, intellectual property protections don't work (openly used ideas get replicated; assume ~six months).
14. p. 387 — The biggest, most important force is how people deal with each other.
15. p. 388 — Final principle: if you're not worried, you need to worry; if you're worried, you don't need to worry — worrying about what can go wrong protects you.

Other stated rules of his method (not red-dotted): "He who lives by the crystal ball is destined to eat ground glass" (p. 359); he looks for big unsustainable conditions and bets they won't be sustained (p. 360); he is wrong at least one-third of the time relative to market expectations, so he diversifies across 15 or more great uncorrelated bets (the "Holy Grail of Investing") rather than avoiding risky bets (p. 361).
