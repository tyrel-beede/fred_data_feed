## Chapter 4 framing (pp. 97–103)

**Hard money vs. fiat money (pp. 97–99).** He flags one determinant that changes how cases play out: whether the government's debt is a promise to deliver something it cannot print (gold, silver, or a currency seen as relatively hard, e.g., the dollar) or a fiat currency whose value rests on the faith and incentives the central bank provides. In hard-currency cases the government almost always reneges when delivering the hard money becomes hard, and the money and the debt in it drop in value at the moment the promise is broken. In fiat cases the central bank manages incentives with interest rates, debt monetization and money tightness; the same over-creation of debt occurs, but the devaluation comes gradually rather than at one break point (pp. 98–99). The last broad hard→fiat shift began August 15, 1971; the same thing happened in April 1933 (p. 98). Japan example: since the start of 2013 holders of JGBs have lost 60% vs. gold, 45% vs. US-dollar debt and 6% in domestic purchasing power (average inflation ≈1%) — gradual because the yen is fiat, but for the same reason (too much debt to monetize) (p. 99). Big Debt Cycles historically alternate between hard and fiat regimes: hard regimes break because debt growth can't be kept inside the monetary constraint; fiat regimes break through loss of faith in debt/money as a storehold of wealth (p. 99).

**Nine stages of the final crisis (pp. 99–103).** The final phase — when government and central bank both go broke — "typically transpires in nine steps"; big variations exist and stages need not occur in exact order. He frames the list as unhealthy conditions plus the classic steps taken to get out: "The more of these unhealthy things exist, the greater the risk of a 'heart attack'" (p. 100) — i.e., the checklist is a risk gauge: the more items present, the higher the probability of a debt/currency crisis. Causes vary (chronic overspending and debt accumulation; costly wars; costly shocks like droughts, floods, pandemics; mixes) (p. 100). Within stage 3 there are two exits: (a) a monetary/fiscal shift that restores balance between supply and demand for money and credit, or (b) self-reinforcing net selling that produces a severe liquidation crisis which runs its course and cuts debt and debt service relative to income — "Big net selling of the debt is the big red flag." (p. 100). Stage 4 is "easy to see in the market action": rates rising, led by long rates rising faster than short rates, with the currency weakening at the same time (p. 101). The stage-9 end state: tight money and very high real rates for a while to re-establish the creditability of money and credit by rewarding lender-creditors and penalizing borrower-debtors; how painful depends on whether debt is in a currency the central bank can create and whether debtors/creditors are mostly domestic; reserve-currency status raises the marginal inclination to hold the currency/debt, but such governments tend to abuse the privilege by over-borrowing, making their decline more abrupt and painful (pp. 102–103).

## (b) THE STAGES

### Stage 1 — "The Private Sector and Government Get Deep in Debt" (heading p. 106; list p. 100; detail pp. 106–110)

**Entry conditions**
- Years of chronic deficit spending leave a large and growing stock of government debt; a rising share of spending goes to consumption/the social safety net and a falling share to productivity-enhancing investment, so debt rises without matching income. The safety net becomes a political "third rail" (his examples: Brazil and the US today) (p. 106).
- Debt is high relative to the government's capacity to repay from tax revenue; debt service is high relative to income and begins crowding out essential spending; more debt must be sold than the private sector wants to buy, pushing rates up and raising debt service further (p. 106).
- Heavy private-sector borrowing that the public sector will have to back (implicit guarantees of banks it can't let fail) is effectively a public balance-sheet problem even when the public accounts look fine (p. 108).
- The buildup requires large foreign lending — in local currency (raises devaluation risk) or a reserve currency (raises default risk) — increasing vulnerability to a pullback of foreign capital (p. 108). A current-account deficit alone is not a problem (it reflects capital inflows/attractive markets), but when attractiveness is impaired by the need to issue lots of debt and money quickly, potential foreign selling adds vulnerability (p. 109).
- Years of foreign borrowing leave a substantial accumulated debt to foreigners and, by the time of devaluation, very few liquid assets to cover external debt service (p. 110).

**Dynamics / fixed vs. fiat.** Fixed-rate cases: restructuring after the default/devaluation is more severe and definitive, setting up a more abrupt and larger rebound. Fiat cases: a gradual rise in debt because central-bank printing lets government spending continue or accelerate (p. 106). Footnote 22 (p. 107): the government-balance-sheet charts marked "ex-Ongoing" exclude a handful of recent cases still playing out — the US, Europe, the UK and Japan post-financial-crisis.

**Duration:** deficits and external deficits build over the prior several years; "steadily increasing current account and twin deficits typically lead the crisis by several years" (p. 109).
**Transition to Stage 2:** the private sector hits the limit of its ability to carry debt, forcing the government to step in (pp. 111–112).
**Markets:** upward pressure on interest rates from excess supply of government debt (p. 106); foreign inflows still strong (≈2–3% GDP) but about to turn (p. 109).

### Stage 2 — "The Private Sector Suffers a Debt Crisis, and the Central Government Gets Deeper in Debt to Help the Private Sector" (heading p. 111; list p. 100; detail pp. 111–113)

**Entry conditions:** the government's balance sheet goes from moderately stretched (years before devaluation) to extremely stretched as it is forced to address private-sector debt problems (p. 111).

**Dynamics**
- The government takes the larger role because it can get money and credit far more easily than private borrowers: lenders know the central bank can print and route money to the government, and the government can tax. This is most true for the most established reserve-currency governments (p. 111).
- Government debt rises faster than private debt. Typical pattern: government debt soars while private debt plunges about a year before the crisis, and government debt rises a lot relative to private debt; he saw it in 15 of the 21 cases with data on both balance sheets (p. 111). "When private debt is falling sharply and government debt is rising sharply, it is a short leading indicator of trouble." (p. 111)
- Government debt problems intensify: the stock of government debt grows relative to (1) its revenues, (2) the hard assets (usually reserves) available to repay, and (3) the quantity of money in the economy available to finance it — until the central bank steps in with more money and credit (p. 112).

Annotations: private sector at the limits of its ability to support higher debt burdens … forcing government to step in and borrow/spend (p. 112) |

**Policy responses:** government borrows and spends to backstop the private sector (p. 112).
**Duration:** private debt plunge ≈ one year before the crisis (p. 111).
**Transition to Stage 3:** free-market demand for the government's debt falls short of its supply (p. 113).
**Markets:** not separately described; the leading signal is the balance-sheet crossover (private debt falling sharply, government debt rising sharply) (p. 111).

### Stage 3 — "The Central Government Experiences a Debt Squeeze in Which the Free-Market Demand for Its Debt Falls Short of the Supply of It" (heading p. 113; list p. 100; detail pp. 113–117)

**Entry conditions:** the government is in trouble when (1) its finances are squeezed by debt and debt-service expense that limit essential spending and (2) holders of the debt assets want to sell them (p. 113). The squeeze itself is a debt problem; net selling makes it much worse — he italicizes that net selling of the debt is a big red flag (p. 113).

**Dynamics**
- Upward pressure on rates raises financing costs, forcing painful spending cuts or still more borrowing (p. 113).
- Debt service at a very high share of income (his example: 100%) is a red flag because it (a) squeezes out spending and/or (b) requires large borrowing and rollovers that may not happen as lender-creditors worry and stop lending or sell (pp. 113–114). There comes a point in the long-term debt cycle when debt service is so large relative to income that it either crowds out other spending or creates a big demand shortage. "In 25 of the 35 cases I studied, I saw government debt service as a percent of government revenues accelerate going into the crisis." (p. 114)
- Given accumulated debt and ongoing deficits run to compensate for a weak private sector, debt and debt service are on a path to keep climbing; at the time of the eventual devaluation the government was typically on a path toward indefinitely increasing debt and debt service absent devaluation (pp. 114–115).
- Two exits (from ch. 4): a policy shift that rebalances supply and demand for money/credit, or self-reinforcing net selling and a severe liquidation crisis (p. 100).
- Faced with a large, growing burden and financing need, the classic next step is papering over and "creative ways to source financing, including accounting tricks" (p. 115).

| Use of the eight financing tricks below | Markers that the squeeze has arrived (pp. 115–117) |

**Policy responses — the eight "creative" financing measures (pp. 116–117)**
1. Policy and development banks for off-balance-sheet financing (frequent in Asian crises, e.g., Japan and the Asian financial crisis).
2. Debt guarantees instead of direct spending (Peru 1980s; Turkey recently — a 2017 business loan-guarantee program during balance-of-payments pressure). Doesn't show in spending until losses appear, so it looks "free".
3. Requiring or heavily incentivizing domestic banks, pensions and insurers to finance the government (Turkey and Brazil recently); very favorable regulatory treatment of government debt; manipulating the yield curve and financing rates (US in World War II) — effectively backdoor monetary financing because it induces banks to lever at short rates to lend to the government.
4. Patriotic campaigns (Turkey: sell dollars for lira; WWII bond drives; Korea 1990s gold campaign to repay the IMF).
5. "Paying" for spending with future cuts/tax rises that may never come (Brazil's constitutional spending limit with plenty of outs).
6. Calling in favors from international creditors and/or geopolitical financing deals (Turkey recently; UK's Sterling Area after WWII).
7. Shortening debt maturities (lenders more willing to lend short than long).
8. Capital controls to keep money from leaving, in relatively severe situations.

**Duration:** not stated.
**Transition to Stage 4:** selling of the debt becomes large enough to drive a free-market tightening (p. 117).
**Markets:** rates under upward pressure; yield-curve management to make government debt artificially attractive (p. 116); maturities shortening (p. 116).

### Stage 4 — "The Selling of the Government's Debt Leads to a) a Free-Market-Driven Tightening of Money and Credit, Which Leads to b) a Weakening of the Economy" … (heading continues: c) downward pressure on the currency, and d) declining reserves as the central bank tries to defend the currency — paraphrased) (heading p. 117; list pp. 100–101; detail pp. 117–133)

**Entry conditions**
- Net selling of government debt tightens money and credit via the market; the central bank attempts to defend the currency with monetary tightening and reserve sales (p. 117).
- A relatively large red flag: debts have risen so far relative to the income needed to service them that smart investors see losses as inevitable — either default or lots of printing, currency weakness and inflation (p. 117).
- Lender-creditors lose faith that they will be adequately paid (the debtor can't afford debt service, or the return won't compensate for inflation), so buying falls short of selling and the price of debt must fall (rates rise) until there is less borrowing or more saving (p. 117). War or sanction risk worsens this (confiscation of debt assets, more borrowing, default, devaluation) (pp. 117–118).

**Dynamics**
- The doom loop: upward pressure on rates weakens the economy and raises future borrowing needs (or requires tax rises/cuts too painful at that point), which widens the bond-market supply/demand mismatch and pushes rates up further; that is when central banks step in by printing and buying — quantitative easing (p. 118).
- Simultaneous plunge in foreign inflows into local government and corporate bonds and a spike in real rates from a classic failed attempt to support the currency with higher rates and tighter credit (p. 118); governments shorten issuance maturities to make bonds more palatable (p. 118).
- Central bank's choice: (a) let rates rise enough to curtail borrowing and attract lenders by redirecting money from other uses, or (b) print and buy the debt. His rule: "the central bank will always choose b) over a), and that the best path is to balance a) and b)" (p. 119). Damned if it prints (currency weakness, inflation) and damned if it doesn't (extremely tight money, very high rates, very bad economy) (p. 119).
- The spiral starts when debt service becomes intolerable for the debtor and/or the creditor won't hold the debt (insufficient real return; high perceived default risk; high risk of printing/devaluation); the value of government debt falls until a new equilibrium when enough debt is destroyed or devalued that burdens are no longer excessive (pp. 119–120). "This hasn't yet happened in the US, Europe, Japan, or China." (p. 120)
- Tightening and/or currency intervention is abandoned because tightening is too harmful and intervention doesn't work and is too costly, so the debt/currency devalues; investors and savers logically abandon the country's assets and currency (p. 120).
- Weak economy plus rising inflation puts the currency under pressure. Fixed-rate policy makers fight depreciation — with high inflation the currency is getting more expensive in real terms exactly when a devaluation is needed; floating cases sell off gradually into the weakness (p. 122).
- Hard-currency debtors: credit spreads widen as default odds are priced; risky assets price higher risk premiums (sell off), adding to the downward pressure (p. 123).
- Reserves: governments can hold liquid savings (reserves, sovereign wealth funds); watch their size, the speed of drawdown and how close they are to running out. Selling foreign currency to buy local currency shrinks the money supply, i.e., a form of tightening (p. 124). In the most severe cases reserves are already low relative to central-bank liabilities (the money savers hold), so the defense is seen to be doomed, inviting more betting against the currency and flight from the debt (p. 124).
- Reserve-intervention statistics (pp. 125–126): pre-intervention war chest typically ≈5% of GDP, covering ≈a tenth of the money supply and of government debt; during the intervention the central bank typically spends over half its reserves, concentrated in a short period — in the worst six-month window reserves decline 49% in the median case; the currency generally falls during the defense (gold rallies 42%, median), though some defenses prop it up temporarily; after roughly a two-year defense (varies by case) the central bank gives up, with reserves backing only ≈6% of the money stock and 3% of government debt; after it stops, the currency sells off (gold rallies a further 51%, median).
- At this stage the currency is "at best highly risky and at worst a very bad deal", and not just investors but banks, corporations and households de-risk out of the debt and currency (p. 130). The 19 classic late-stage behaviors (pp. 130–133):
  - Corporate treasury: (1) keep international revenue offshore in foreign FX (dollars) rather than converting; (2) increase hedging of the local currency, esp. firms with hard-currency debt (forward sales push the forward rate down and drag the spot rate); (3) foreign corporations sweep subsidiaries' cash out promptly; (4) foreign subsidiaries judged not worth the hassle — new FDI put on hold.
  - Domestic banks: (5) banks forced to buy government debt must sell it when liquidity dries up, accelerating the sell-off; (6) central-bank tactics to stay stimulative (multiple interest rates, capital controls) make offshore more attractive than onshore; money leaving the banking system usually means selling government debt.
  - International banks: (7) close headache lines (trade finance, working-capital lines); (8) sell or give away subsidiaries.
  - Large international investors: (9) more debt ends up held by players who can't sell (banks) while the dollar value falls; liquidity dries up, pushing out big foreign investors; (10) large government reserve holders move out of the currency, often for geopolitical reasons; (11) reserve managers can't sell without disrupting markets so they accumulate all new reserves in another currency, drying up demand; (12) investors can't sell but don't roll exposures. "The outflows from foreigners are classic and tend to lead the devaluation." (p. 132)
  - Domestic savers: (13) seek diversification and inflation hedges; convert deposits to hard currency; (14) buy real goods ahead of inflation (imports → currency sales, fuels inflation); (15) high-net-worth individuals move money abroad (wealth preservation, rising taxes, confiscation fears); (16) see foreign stocks as the more reliable bet; (17) open foreign bank accounts (governments often make this hard).
  - Speculative trading: (18) bond-vigilante action emerges and becomes self-reinforcing; (19) equity investors pull out, hitting the currency.

### Stage 5 — "When There Is a Debt Crisis and Interest Rates Can't Be Lowered (e.g., They Hit 0%), the Central Bank 'Prints' (Creates) Money and Buys Bonds" … (heading continues: to ease credit and make it easier to service debt — paraphrased) (heading p. 135; list p. 101; detail pp. 135–138)

**Entry conditions:** a debt crisis in which rates cannot be lowered (at 0%, or long rates limit the fall in short rates) (p. 101); the government needs much more financing but faces rates higher than it can afford, often because of mechanical selling of the currency and debt, so it turns to the central bank — "This puts the problem in the central bank's court." (p. 135)

**Dynamics**
- The central bank doesn't literally print; it borrows reserves from commercial banks and pays a very short-term rate on them (pp. 101, 135). It relieves pressure on the government's finances (or other systemically important entities) by direct asset purchases or by guarantees and backstops, often taking losses on assets bought at uneconomical prices through defaults, inflation and/or rising rates — the balance-sheet hit moves from the government to the central bank and to holders of the currency (p. 136).
- He rates heavy money/credit creation to buy bonds "a red flag, but not yet a big red flag" because of the central bank's power over money and credit (p. 136). If (a) QE continues for a long time, (b) maturities are shortened for lack of long-term demand, and (c) total debt and the share held by the central bank rise because free-market demand is inadequate, each is an early-stage red flag (p. 136). These trends — rising central-bank holdings and shortening maturities — typically begin nearly a decade before the crisis and reverse after it (p. 136).
- Lenders lend to the government in stress because the central bank can print, so default risk looks nil; the real risk is that the central bank creates so much money and credit to prevent defaults that inflation makes repayment in devalued money the big risk for lender-creditors — again a red flag but not a big one, "because history shows that it can happen a lot before the supply-and-demand imbalance becomes a problem" (p. 137).
- Scale of QE since 2008 (previously called debt monetization): US — four waves totaling 18% of potential GDP, 5% of total debt, 16% of government debt; Europe — four waves totaling 30% of potential GDP, 10% of total debt, 36% of government debt; Japan — three waves totaling 95% of potential GDP, 22% of total debt, 46% of government debt (pp. 137–138).
- Buying bonds exposes the central bank to the same risks as commercial banks and investors; the only differences are that it can print and can account for losses less visibly. Mechanically it pays for a bond by crediting the seller bank's deposit at the central bank and pays interest on that deposit; if deposit rates rise above the yield on its bonds it has negative cash flow; marked to market it would show losses and, if losses exceed capital, negative net worth. At this stage no one cares much, but should (p. 138).

**Policy responses:** QE (money creation and bond purchases), guarantees and backstops, maturity shortening (pp. 135–136).
**Duration:** the central-bank-holdings and maturity trends start nearly a decade before the crisis and reverse after it (p. 136).
**Transition to Stage 6:** rates keep rising so that what the central bank pays on its liabilities exceeds what it earns on its bonds (p. 138).
**Markets:** long rates held down by purchases; risk shifts from default to repayment in devalued money (inflation/currency) (p. 137).

### Stage 6 — "If Interest Rates Rise, the Central Bank Loses Money Because the Interest Rate That It Has to Pay on Its Liabilities Is Greater than" … (heading continues: the rate it receives on the debt assets it bought — paraphrased) (heading p. 138; list p. 101; detail pp. 138–144)

**Entry conditions:** after large bond purchases, rates have risen, bond prices fallen, and the central bank's short-term cost of funds exceeds the return on its holdings, producing losses large enough for negative net worth (p. 139). Not a big red flag until the bank has a significant negative net worth and must print to cover its negative cash flow — that is the big red flag of the central bank's "death spiral" (pp. 101, 138–139).

**Dynamics**
- Death spiral: rising rates create problems creditors see → they won't hold the debt → higher rates or more printing → money devalues → more selling of debt and currency, and so on. "Going broke" for a central bank means it can't make its debt-service payments, though it doesn't default because it prints; in large amounts that devalues the money and creates inflationary recessions or depressions (pp. 101, 139).
- The central bank is caught between policy easy enough to support a weak economy and a fiscally weak government and tight enough to stop savers and investors fleeing the currency — the hallmark of an unsustainable situation (p. 139). It manifests in two ways:
  1. **Losses and negative net worth** (pp. 139–140). Another red flag, but not the end of the Big Debt Cycle: the bank can still print to fund losses. Where governments won't tolerate "flimflam finances", the government recapitalizes the central bank by taxing, cutting or borrowing — which adds to the squeeze (p. 139). Buying a lot of debt lowers the value of the debt because it lowers the value of the money it promises (p. 139). Negative net worth is a moderate red flag at first: "several central banks have negative net equity (or equivalent) today" without much operational hindrance, but at larger loss levels a spiral can start (p. 140). Advantages of central-bank buying: it supplies credit that keeps rates lower than otherwise, and when rates rise the losses fall on the central bank rather than private investors — which matters less than private investors needing to look creditworthy (p. 140). Big central-bank losses "signify a step toward a more advanced stage near the end of the Big Debt Cycle", so he treats them as a flag (p. 140). As losses go from small to very large, cash-flow needs can only be met by printing, putting significant downward pressure on the currency: a large interest bill on liabilities (to keep savers in the currency) against little income on assets (bought to support the government), with the difference printed (p. 140).
  2. **Forced printing to monetize its own and others' losses despite the currency pressure** (p. 142). Explicitly via direct purchases or implicitly via guarantees/backstops (e.g., the Emergency Banking Act of 1933; the Bank of Amsterdam's backstop of the Dutch East India Company — both ultimately required monetization). Hallmarks (pp. 142–144):
     - An expanding central-bank balance sheet as money is printed to finance the government or roll the debts of other stressed entities; actions can extend to private assets (corporate bonds, equities) and off-balance-sheet guarantees that still transfer purchasing power to stressed debtors (p. 142).
     - Sale of reserves to defend the currency while simultaneously supplying money and credit, shifting the asset mix from hard assets (gold, FX reserves) to soft assets (claims on the government or financials); this feeds the run (especially with a peg) as investors watch the defense resources shrink, forcing faster reserve sales until a defense is no longer feasible — far more pronounced in fixed than floating cases (p. 143).
     - Monetization plus reserve sales lowers the ratio of the central bank's hard assets (reserves) to its liabilities (money), weakening its ability to defend the currency; pegged countries carry a more-backed money supply but hit trouble sooner when the ratio falls, and spend more reserves in the defense stage (pp. 143–144).

### Stage 7 — "Debts Are Restructured and Devalued" (heading p. 145; list pp. 101–102; detail pp. 145–152)

**Entry conditions:** debt burdens have become too great; a big restructuring and/or devaluation that substantially reduces their size and value happens, by itself or with good management (p. 145). Chapter 7 preface: the cycle ends when a mix of market forces and policy-maker actions create a bottom and an upswing (p. 145).

**Definition — "beautiful deleveraging":** the best-managed case, in which "the deflationary ways of reducing debt burdens (e.g., through debt restructurings) are balanced with the inflationary ways of reducing debt burdens (e.g., by monetizing them)" so that deleveraging happens without unacceptable deflation or inflation (pp. 101–102, 145).

**Dynamics**
- The currency devalues and the remaining holders of the currency and the debt take big real losses; the loss of purchasing power continues until a new monetary system with enough credibility to entice investors and savers back is established, typically after a substantial write-down and restructuring (pp. 145–146).
- Government debts devalue relative to real assets — gold, stocks, commodities; "Perhaps this time, digital currencies like Bitcoin will benefit." (p. 146). "On average, gold outperforms holding the local currency in these cases by roughly 60% from the start of the devaluation until the currency bottoms." (p. 146) Big difference between fixed and fiat cases.
- A terrible time in markets and economies, but it reduces debt burdens and lays the foundation for improvement (p. 150). In the run-up, debt rises a lot relative to the monetary base, so the private sector must absorb much more government debt with the same base money (likely part of why rates rise first); eventually the central bank monetizes, expanding the base and lowering the debt-to-money ratio (p. 150).
- Reserves/debt falls first (debt rising fast; reserves sold to defend) and then rises after policy makers let the currency go, because devaluation mechanically shrinks local-currency debt relative to hard-currency assets and improves competitiveness/hard-currency earnings (p. 150).
- Sequence: government debt rises first (crisis response) while money growth is broadly unchanged (it even slows when the central bank mounts a currency defense); the government tries FX controls or a managed/official exchange rate different from the market rate — distortions that do more harm than good; once the central bank lets the currency go, printing accelerates and the resulting inflation lifts nominal incomes relative to debts. Similar across pegged and non-pegged cases (p. 151).
- Reserves/debt falls mainly because debt rises, and additionally because reserves are sold late in the cycle to fight the currency's collapse; after selling stops and the currency devalues, the ratio improves (p. 151).

### Stage 9 — "The Deleveraging Process Inevitably Creates a Reduction in the Debt Burdens That Creates the Return to Equilibrium" (heading p. 154; list p. 102; detail pp. 154–161)

**Entry conditions:** the currency has been devalued and remaining holders of the currency and the debt have taken big real losses, relieving much of the debtors' burden (p. 154). Quite often the end comes through inflationary depressions that devalue the debt; then government reserves are raised through asset sales and a strictly enforced transition from a rapidly falling to a relatively stable currency is achieved by linking the currency to a hard currency or hard asset (e.g., gold), with very tight money and a very high real interest rate that severely penalizes borrower-debtors and rewards lender-creditors, which brings buying of the debt/currency and stabilizes it (pp. 102, 154).

**Dynamics**
- It now doesn't take much to back up the debt. Managed well, the government raises reserves by selling government-owned assets and/or borrowing from the IMF or others on conditions of sound policy including austerity. The interest rate is still very high relative to prospective inflation and prospective depreciation, so the central bank can make the debt/money an attractive investment again — and debt in that currency very expensive — if it manages well (p. 155).
- A new and more stable monetary system with enough credibility to bring investors and savers back is established, typically after a substantial write-down and restructuring and with a return to some form of hard money, requiring fundamental adjustments to the country's balance sheet and income statement (p. 155).
- Debt-to-GDP attribution after the devaluation (average across cases): central government debt is 89% of GDP around the devaluation; reductions come 7% from central-bank purchases, 38% from inflation, 26% from positive real GDP growth, 16% from primary surpluses, 8% from defaults/restructuring; continued interest payments add 76%; net: 89% → 70% of GDP. Rising inflation and rising real growth from aggressive stimulation are the big forces (pp. 156–157). Ex-ongoing cases: 90% start; −2% CB purchases, −49% inflation, −35% real growth, −28% primary surplus, −8% restructuring/default, +92% interest; 59% end (p. 158). Governments with own-currency debt (1) service interest and principal by having central banks create money and credit, raise inflation and stimulate real growth, and by restructuring debts, which lifts nominal income growth relative to debt service, and (2) restructure defaulted debts in the amounts shown. "In most such cases, the debt problems never go away as much as they remain a manageable burden" (p. 157). Averages with large ranges but consistent patterns.
- "When these conditions are met, these are among the best times to hold the country's currency and debt." (p. 161)

**The five classic steps of the transition (pp. 155–160)**
1. Restructure the country's debts to manageable levels — reserve assets cover a substantial portion of liabilities and government debt service no longer exceeds revenue growth; typically defaulting on and restructuring foreign-currency debts and some local-currency debts (p. 155).
2. A deep, painful fiscal adjustment so finances are sustainable without printing; healthy balance-of-payments adjustment; the primary deficit typically improves before interest costs fall by rolling into lower rates (p. 158).
3. Obtain sufficient reserves to defend the currency (or back a replacement currency): devaluation helps by raising the value of reserves relative to nominal liabilities and improving competitiveness (exports vs imports); asset sales to build reserves; occasional borrowing from official creditors (among the few still willing); sale of state-owned companies and assets, which also improves their efficiency (pp. 158–159).
4. High real interest rates that more than compensate investors for the risks of holding the currency (p. 160).
5. Limits on what the central bank can do that would undermine the sustainable finances of the new stable money (p. 160).

## (c) Transmission mechanics: private-sector crisis → central-government crisis → central-bank crisis

1. **Private sector → central government (Stages 1–2).** Private debts that the public sector must back (implicit guarantees of banks it cannot let fail) are public liabilities in substance (p. 108). When the private sector reaches the limit of its ability to carry debt, the government steps in because it can borrow far more easily — lenders know the central bank can print and route money to the government, and the government can tax — so government debt rises as private debt plunges (about a year before the crisis), and the government/private debt ratio inflects up (pp. 111–112). The hand-off is visible as the crossover of the two debt lines (p. 112).
2. **Central government → market squeeze (Stages 3–4).** The government's debt and debt service grow relative to revenues, reserves and the money stock (p. 112). When free-market demand for its debt falls short of supply, holders sell; rates rise; the higher rates weaken the economy and raise borrowing needs, widening the supply/demand gap — the bond-market doom loop (pp. 113, 118). Rising rates with a weakening currency (long rates leading short rates) is the market signature (p. 101). The central bank's reserve defense (selling FX, buying local currency) is itself a tightening that shrinks the money supply (p. 124); it typically burns over half its reserves over ≈2 years and then gives up (pp. 125–126).
3. **Central government → central bank (Stage 5).** Facing rates it can't afford, the government turns to the central bank: the choice is (a) let rates rise enough to ration credit or (b) create money and buy the debt; central banks always choose (b) and the best path balances the two (pp. 119, 135–136). Purchases at uneconomical prices transfer the balance-sheet hit from the government to the central bank and to holders of the currency (pp. 136, 142). Lenders keep lending to the government because printing removes default risk — the risk migrates to repayment in devalued money (p. 137).
4. **Central bank → currency holders (Stage 6).** The central bank funds its purchases by paying short-term interest on bank reserves; when that cost exceeds the yield on its bonds it runs cash-flow losses and, marked to market, negative net worth (p. 138). Small losses don't matter; large ones can only be funded by printing, which weakens the currency, feeds inflation, and causes more selling of the debt and currency — the death spiral (pp. 101, 139–140). Reserve sales during the defense convert hard assets into soft claims, lowering reserves/money and undermining the defense (pp. 143–144). If the government recapitalizes the central bank, it must tax, cut or borrow, which loops the squeeze back to the government (p. 139). A central bank "goes broke" when it can't service its liabilities without printing; it doesn't default, it devalues (pp. 101, 139).
5. **Private actors' feedback (Stage 4 behaviors).** Corporates keep FX offshore and hedge; banks sell forced holdings when liquidity dries up and move money offshore; international banks close lines and dump subsidiaries; reserve managers stop accumulating the currency; savers convert to hard currency, buy real goods, and move money abroad; bond vigilantes and equity outflows reinforce the currency decline (pp. 130–133). Foreign outflows lead the devaluation (p. 132).
6. **Resolution feedback (Stages 7–9).** Devaluation and inflation lift nominal incomes relative to debts and shrink local-currency debt relative to hard-currency reserves, improving reserve coverage (pp. 150–151); restructuring, inflation, real growth and primary surpluses bring debt/GDP from 89% to 70% on average while interest costs add 76% (p. 157); very high real rates and a hard-money link restore the creditability of money (pp. 102, 154–155).

Two structural modifiers run through the whole chain: (i) fixed-rate cases build pressure then break abruptly (more severe, definitive restructuring; bigger rebound), fiat cases adjust gradually (pp. 105–106); (ii) debt in a currency the central bank can create, with mostly domestic creditors, gives the authorities more control and a less painful path; reserve-currency status adds demand for the debt but tempts over-borrowing that makes the eventual decline more abrupt (pp. 102–103).

## (d) The Overall Big Cycle (Chapter 8, pp. 163–179)

He calls this the most important chapter in the book (p. 163). The debt/credit/money/economic cycle can't be understood in isolation; together, five big forces produce the Overall Big Cycle that leads to radical changes in monetary, domestic and/or world orders (pp. 163–164). The full treatment is in *Principles for Dealing with the Changing World Order* (last 500 years); here he gives a summary to compare against Part III (what happened) and Part IV (the future) (p. 164).

**How the machine works (pp. 164–166).** Everything changes like a perpetual-motion machine because everything that happens has causes; the mechanics are complex because everything affects everything else. For about 35 years he has built computerized expert systems that make decisions as he would, based on the principle that decision rules must rest on timeless, universal cause/effect relationships that explain all big developments in all time frames and countries; a failure to explain indicates a missing influence to add to the template (pp. 164–165). With AI breakthroughs he thinks we are on the brink of understanding all cause/effect relationships, but for now the work is done the old-fashioned way (p. 165).

**The five forces — exactly as he lists them (p. 165):**
1. "The debt/credit/money/economic cycle"
2. "The internal order and disorder cycle"
3. "The external geopolitical order and disorder cycle (i.e., the changing world order)"
4. "Acts of nature (droughts, floods, and pandemics)"
5. "Human inventiveness, most importantly of new technologies"
Footnote 26 adds a sixth consideration, the demographic force: many old people who don't work and are expensive to support (high late-life healthcare costs), a shrinking workforce in developed countries, large population increases in less-developed countries, and only a small share of people being truly productive (p. 165).

**Shape (pp. 165–166).** The forces interact to create cycles that move markets and economies around an upward-sloping trend line whose slope is set by the inventiveness of practical people (entrepreneurs) given adequate resources (capital) and working well with others. Over short periods (1–10 years) the short-term cycles, especially the debt and political cycles, dominate; over long periods (10 years and beyond) the long-term cycles and the productivity trend have much bigger effects. Diagram (p. 166): a rising productivity line; an oscillating line of short-term debt cycles riding a long swell that breaks sharply down at "The Big Debt Cycle" and then resumes.

**Where we are (pp. 166–169).** "We are now 80 years into the Overall Big Cycle that began at the end of World War II" and it is "by and large unfolding in the classic ways" that will produce dramatic changes (pp. 166–167). The current Big Cycle began in 1945 (p. 178).

### Force 1 — The debt/credit/money/economic cycle (pp. 167–170)
- Key influences on the Big Debt Cycle: debt-service payments relative to income; the amount of new debt sold relative to demand for it; the desirability and willingness of holders to keep holding existing debt assets; and other factors covered earlier (p. 167).
- Main points: short-term cycles have always added up to Big Debt Cycles; the average short-term debt cycle has taken about six years, give or take about three; the average long-term Big Debt Cycle about 80 years, give or take 25 — both with durations driven by monitorable influences that allow rough estimates of remaining length; debt cycles influence and are influenced by the other four forces (p. 167).
- Summary mechanism: creation of unsustainably large debt assets and liabilities relative to the money, goods, services and investment assets in existence always leads to big debt crises and runs on banks — a run being holders turning in debt assets (which have no intrinsic value) to get real money the bank doesn't have enough of; the run feeds on itself and shifts market values and wealth until debts are defaulted on, restructured and/or monetized, cutting burdens relative to incomes and reaching a new equilibrium. Debts are almost always monetized (pp. 167–168).
- **Stage label for the Overall Big Cycle:** when the debt/money force, the internal-order force and the external-order force are all late in their cycles (lots of debt, lots of internal and external conflict), it is typically just before big conflicts and revolutionary changes in monetary, internal and world orders. Like a life cycle the Big Cycle has stages; "This late-cycle stage, which I call Stage 5, comes just before the depression and war stage that brings about the end of the Big Cycle." "I believe that we are now in this late-cycle stage." (p. 168)
- Existing circumstances (p. 169): great overindebtedness in the US and all other major countries simultaneously; increasingly nationalistic and fragmented internal orders; increasingly contentious relations between countries; adverse and expensive acts of nature; amazing new technologies.
- Extraordinary actions countries (including the US) have taken when faced with excessive debt (p. 169): exerting great pressure on other countries to buy the country's debt (as the British did); selectively freezing debt and/or seizing assets of "enemy" countries (US vs Japan in 1941; Russia more recently); defaulting on/restructuring debts by extending maturities and/or monetizing (Germany after Hitler came to power); imposing confiscatory taxes and capital/FX controls to keep assets from leaving; revaluing/managing government assets; creating new types of money. He is not predicting these, but considers it irresponsible not to convey what past cases show (pp. 169–170).

### Force 2 — The internal order and disorder cycle (pp. 170–174)
- Timing: short-term political swings of about six years, give or take three, add up to big shifts in domestic orders lasting about 80 years, give or take 25; durations vary but are driven by monitorable influences (p. 170; footnote 27 refers to ch. 5 of *Principles for Dealing with the Changing World Order* for the full cycle).
- Mechanism: orders change when those outside power acquire more power than those running the order and want to change it. Fights occur when (a) a powerful group wants change and (b) it is unclear which side is stronger; no fight when there is no such group or when one side is so much stronger that change happens with little fighting (pp. 170–171). The same dynamics apply to all governments, organizations and families because they are embedded in human nature (p. 170).
- Democracies: the election cycle roughly coincides with the economic cycle because bad economic conditions drive political change. Leader arc (red-dot principle): a honeymoon of great optimism at the start (e.g., first 100 days), then promises prove hard to deliver, disappointment sets in, critics and enemies grow bolder, support wanes, and staying in power gets harder — often leading to more extreme actions. "These dynamics are at play in the US at my time of writing this book in March 2025." How things go depends mostly on the economy, which depends mostly on where the short- and long-term debt/credit/money/economic cycles are, though exogenous events (droughts, floods, pandemics, big conflicts) matter (p. 171).
- All governance orders change type (democracy ↔ autocracy), each in well- and poorly-managed flavors. **Stages of democracy failing (pp. 171–174):**
  1. Conditions: large gaps in wealth and values; bad and worsening conditions; weak, fragmented leadership in the republic-style representative democracy (p. 172).
  2. Compromise — which democracy intrinsically relies on — breaks down; opposing sides become willing to fight to win at all costs; intensifying populist conflicts among the hard right, the weak middle and the hard left; conflict rises especially in economic stress, leading to fights for the power to control (p. 172).
  3. Leaders appeal to constituents' desire for immediate benefits and relief rather than fixing deep systemic issues; leadership becomes weak, decadent and corrupt (especially after long prosperity); growing corruption, inequality and institutional failure; the system loses legitimacy when it no longer serves a large share of the people — Plato's account and China's "losing the mandate of heaven" (as far back as 1046 BCE) (p. 172).
  4. A demagogue (Plato's term) leads the change: manipulating opinion, stirring emotion, promising easy solutions to complex problems, using propaganda and bullying; typically from the well-educated class, gathering powerful allies. Hard-right demagogues are backed by the rich and powerful (nobles historically, capitalists since the Industrial Revolution) who believe great leadership needs strong top-down partnership; left demagogues draw support from the unprivileged masses. Tactics: propaganda, coercion, consolidation of power to undermine enemies and the democratic institutions/bureaucracies that support them (p. 173).
  5. Replacement of democracy with a more centralized, dictatorial government. The transfer usually happens within the democracy's rules and becomes increasingly extreme over a few years, usually around three to five; the new leaders make radical changes to the monetary, political and geopolitical orders and become very nationalistic, militaristic, expansionist and autocratic (p. 174).
  - Historical examples (pp. 171–172, 174): Julius Caesar in Rome (49–44 BCE), Napoleon in France (1799–1815), Mussolini in Italy (1922–1943), Hitler in Germany (1933–1945), a consortium of leaders in Japan (1931–1945), Franco in Spain (1936–1975), Erdogan in Turkey (2016–now); Plato's *Republic* (~375 BCE).
  - Controls: for companies, a strong board and effective regulators; for governments, oversight functions and separation of powers — the less controlled, the more dictatorial the leader becomes (p. 173). Strong CEOs can be hard to distinguish from demagogues (p. 173).
  - Dated read: these shifts are now taking place, "mostly toward the hard right", for the same reasons as in the past (p. 174).
- Gauges he names for the internal cycle: wealth and values gaps; whether conditions are bad and worsening; strength/fragmentation of leadership; the state of the economy (via the debt cycles); whether compromise is functioning; populist polarization (hard right / weak middle / hard left); the leader's honeymoon-to-disappointment arc; corruption, inequality and institutional effectiveness/legitimacy; oversight and separation of powers (pp. 171–173).

### Force 3 — The international order and disorder cycle, i.e., the changing world order (pp. 174–177)
- Periods of order (harmony, productivity, prosperity) alternate with periods of disorder (great conflict, destruction, depression) between countries, as within them; disorder comes with fights over which country or countries get to set the order. With no effective global governance system, the world order is more prone to disorder and conflict than domestic orders (pp. 174–175).
- Big swings between (a) unilateralism — fighting for self-interest, the strong winning over the weak, the law of the jungle/survival of the fittest — and (b) multilateralism — striving for global harmony, peaceful coexistence and egalitarianism. Historically multilateralism worked only after wars, when people were sick of fighting and a dominant power could enforce it; brutal unilateralism has been the norm and sustained multilateral periods extremely rare (p. 175). Markers: Peace of Westphalia 1648 (borders and pledges to enforce them after the Thirty Years' War); the League of Nations after WWI (Wilson, president from 1913) failed to prevent WWII; the post-WWII American world order created the UN, IMF, World Bank, WHO, WTO, International Court of Justice, WIPO etc., with the US as linchpin of a liberal order promoting democracy, free markets and human rights, which has so far prevented another world war (p. 175).
- Dated read: "multilateralism is now fading into irrelevance and unilateralism is rising"; the powers of multilateral organizations are declining rapidly and shifting to the major powers; the aspiration for and existence of global cooperation are eroding as the pendulum swings to self-interested unilateralism — typical of the stage of the Big Cycle we are in (p. 176). The transition is shocking at first then quickly normalized (examples: Trump's statements on Greenland, Canada and the Panama Canal, unimaginable months before his writing; Russia's invasion of Ukraine) (p. 176).
- Longer arc: tribes seizing wealth; winners grow richer, more civilized, then decadent and weak, and are taken down by stronger "barbarians" (Rome and the Gauls; most dynasties); alternating ages of barbarism and civility produce wars when barbarians are strong and civilizations weak (pp. 176–177). The peaceful modern version is business competition and creative destruction; the destructive version produces lack of cooperation in politics, geopolitics, acts of nature (climate change) and new technologies (p. 177).
- Gauges he names: whether there is a dominant power to enforce order; direction of the unilateral/multilateral pendulum and the power of multilateral institutions; speed of alliance changes; relative strength vs decadence of the leading power (pp. 175–177).

### Force 4 — Acts of nature: droughts, floods and pandemics (pp. 177–178)
- Historically have killed more people than wars and toppled more orders than the other forces. "droughts, floods, and pandemics are increasing and increasingly costly" — why is debated, that it is happening is not; nor is it debatable that pollution and disruption of nature, higher population density, closer contact across the world (international travel) and closer contact with other species via land development (animal-to-human disease) are all causes; recent example: the Los Angeles wildfires; "almost certain that these problems will get worse" (p. 177).
- Interactions: migration pressures in developed countries from climate change; worsening living conditions in underdeveloped countries; and, with nearly all nations facing debt problems, not enough money for climate mitigation or adaptation (pp. 177–178).

### Force 5 — Human inventiveness, most importantly of new technologies (pp. 178–179)
- Great advances, particularly in AI, will dramatically affect all thinking in all areas, for good or bad (p. 178). Technology has raised living standards and life expectancy, generated economic and military power, and caused great destruction in wars; it is tied to the other four forces — advancing faster under good financial, economic and social conditions, and producing bubbles and busts when financed by unsustainable credit growth: the South Sea Bubble (1720, as the Dutch Empire began to decline), the Railway Mania (1830s–1840s), the electricity/utilities bubbles ("War of the Currents", 1870s–1890s), the dot-com bubble and telecoms crash (1990–2001) (p. 178).
- Gauge he implies: whether technological advance is financed by sustainable or unsustainable credit growth (p. 178).

**Closing principle (p. 179):** the biggest force is how people deal with each other; cooperation gives the best results, but technology has evolved while human nature hasn't, so this is probably still beyond humankind's capability.

**Note on "stages" of the internal and external cycles:** within this chapter he does not enumerate numbered stages for the internal or external cycles (footnote 27 defers that to the earlier book); what he gives is the characteristics and sequence above, plus the overall "Stage 5" late-cycle label for the whole Big Cycle (p. 168). I have not added stage numbers he does not give here.

**Other rule-like statements in Part II (not red-dot; useful as scoring rules)**
- The nine-item checklist is a risk gauge: the more of the unhealthy conditions present, the higher the probability of a debt/currency crisis ("heart attack") (p. 100).
- Net selling of government debt is the big red flag; stage 4 shows up as rates rising, long rates leading, with the currency weakening at the same time (pp. 100–101, 113).
- Private debt falling sharply while government debt rises sharply is a short leading indicator of trouble (p. 111).
- Debt service near 100% of government income is a red flag (p. 113).
- Debts rising relative to incomes until smart investors see losses as inevitable is a large red flag (p. 117).
- The central bank will always choose printing over letting rates clear the market; the best path balances the two (p. 119).
- Watch reserve/SWF size, drawdown speed and distance from empty for timing; selling FX to buy local currency is a tightening (p. 124).
- Foreign outflows classically lead the devaluation (p. 132).
- Long-running QE, maturity shortening, and rising central-bank share of government debt are early-stage red flags; they start about a decade before the crisis (p. 136).
- Central-bank losses are a flag; negative net worth is moderate; printing to cover negative cash flow is the big red flag (pp. 139–140).
- Gold beats local cash by roughly 60% on average from devaluation to currency bottom (p. 146); after the stage-9 conditions are met it is one of the best times to hold the currency and debt (p. 161).
- Fiat devaluations are gradual, hard-money devaluations abrupt; reserve-currency issuers tend to over-borrow and fall more abruptly (pp. 98–99, 103).
