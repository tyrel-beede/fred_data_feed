## (c) Mechanics (cause → effect, one line each)

Credit, spending and the cycle
- One person's spending is another's earnings, so credit creation raises spending, earnings and asset prices; repayment does the reverse — hence credit/spending/repayment is inherently cyclical (red dot). p. 13–14
- Governments and central banks have a bias toward creating credit because the expansion phase is popular. p. 13
- Short-term cycle: cheap money when activity/inflation low and rates low vs inflation and vs other returns → borrowing → asset prices, activity, inflation rise → restraint, rates high vs inflation/returns → less borrowing → lower prices, slower growth, lower inflation → rates fall → repeat. p. 14
- Because credit is a stimulant people want more of, each short-term cycle's highs and lows in debt sit above the previous one's, so short cycles add up to the long cycle. p. 14, 55
- Early in the Big Debt Cycle debt burdens are low and productive uses plentiful, so borrowing is safe; late, burdens are high and lenders have fewer productive options, so default risk is higher. p. 15
- Risk premia reflect memory of pain: early-cycle yields and expected returns on risky assets are very high relative to low-risk assets (fn. 2); late-1940s/early-1950s stock earnings yields were roughly 4x bond yields. p. 15, 19
- Short-term cycles end on economic restraint; the long-term cycle ends because debt burdens are too great to sustain. p. 15
- Debt and debt service "can grow like a cancer", eating buying power and squeezing out other consumption. p. 15
- Main difference between the cycles: a short-term contraction can be reversed with a heavy dose of money and credit; a long-term contraction cannot because debt levels are unsustainable and holders want out of debt assets. p. 17
- Sequence of overborrowing: private sector → central government (to help) → central bank (prints and buys government debt, then has its own losses) → more monetization if the debt is in a printable currency. p. 17, 29–30

Money, credit and prices
- More money in existence allows more credit and spending (not exactly proportional); less money, less. p. 39
- Price = total spending / total quantity sold; cycles are driven more by changes in money and credit (the $ side) than by changes in quantity (Q), and most goods are produced in response to demand. p. 40, 43
- If the rate of buying falls from X to X−10% with all else unchanged, price falls 10%; unsustainable rates of buying/selling identify unsustainable prices and conditions (red dot). p. 43
- More money/credit + spare productive capacity → non-inflationary growth (both $ and Q rise); more money/credit + no spare capacity → little real growth and much more inflation (red dots). Explains strong growth/low inflation early in the cycle and weak growth/big price rises late. p. 44
- High productivity growth lets Q rise with money and credit, extending non-inflationary growth. p. 44
- Debt busts are mechanical, not primarily psychological; before 2008 and 2010–12 the pace of debt buying had to slow as leveraged buyers hit regulatory leverage limits while debt supply rose. p. 42–43
- Relying on "confidence" from out-year deficit control is naive because it ignores who will actually buy the bonds and in what amounts. p. 43

Investment pricing and capital flows
- All investments compete on total return (yield + price change) because investors care about total return and can arbitrage. p. 45–46
- Gold vs bonds: with Treasuries yielding X% (e.g., 5%) and gold yielding nothing, the market prices gold to rise by ~X% per year relative to Treasuries. p. 46
- Expected real returns, not nominal, drive allocation: when bond yields are low relative to inflation, bonds are sold and inflation-hedge assets bought, and vice versa; heavy money creation pushes investors toward inflation hedges. p. 47
- Short-term vs long-term government debt is priced largely on the projected pace of central bank rate changes. p. 48
- Debt is currency: disliking a currency implies disliking the bonds in it (given relative yields), and vice versa; relative yields + expected price changes = relative total returns, which drives capital flows. p. 48
- Interest parity: if one country's (default-risk-free) government rate is X% below another's, its currency must be expected to appreciate X%/yr; otherwise near-risk-free profits exist and investors sell the lower-yielding debt/currency. p. 48–49
- When the expected currency move does not offset the rate gap, adjustment comes via the spot currency falling, the bond yield rising (bond prices falling), or, under capital controls, a compounding loss to holders. p. 49
- From a central banker's view currency weakness and inflation can be "good" because they reduce debt burdens when nominal rates are below nominal growth and especially when below inflation (negative real rates) (fn. 13). p. 49
- Bond returns go bad through a) local-currency price falls as rates rise, b) currency decline, or c) interest plus currency not compensating for inflation. p. 49–50
- Inflation differences across countries are more due to changes in money/currency values (from money and credit supplies) than to changes in the items' values (fn. 14). p. 50

Players and the balancing act
- Debt expansion requires both borrower-debtors and lender-creditors to be willing, so the deal must suit both; what is good for one is often bad for the other. p. 50–51
- Rates too high for borrowers → spending cuts/asset sales/defaults → markets and economy fall; rates too low for lenders → they stop lending and sell debt assets → rates rise or the central bank prints to hold them down → inflation and contraction in wealth. p. 51
- Banks create bubbles and crises by lending far more than they have, borrowing cheaply and lending for more; crises come when loans aren't repaid or depositors want more back than banks can give. p. 51–52
- Over the long run debts cannot rise faster than the incomes that service them, and rates cannot stay too high for borrowers or too low for lenders for long — "it pays to watch these ratios" (red dot). p. 52
- Total money and credit created determines total spending on goods, services and financial assets; all three tend to rise and fall together with money and credit. p. 52
- Central banks exist to smooth cycles and handle big debt crises (US: 1913); they print to lend in crises, which alleviates debt problems but reduces buying power of money and debt and raises inflation. p. 53
- Central banks raise rates and tighten when debt/demand grow too fast or inflation too high, and ease when slow/low; this changes who wins — borrower-debtors when easing, lender-creditor-savers when tightening. p. 53–54
- Central governments give people what they want without paying for it, so they borrow, reinforcing credit stimulation early and debt depressants later. p. 53–54
- The bigger debt assets/liabilities are relative to real incomes, the harder the balancing act between lenders and borrowers and the greater the chance of a debt-caused downturn (red dot). p. 54
- Sustainable vs unsustainable: if incomes fail to grow as fast as debt and debt service, debt/income grows mechanically and more borrowing is needed just to service debt (red dot). p. 56
- Four reasons central bankers don't contain debt: everyone wants markets up; risky debt levels are unclear ex ante; opportunity cost of not providing credit; crises can usually be managed. p. 57
- Too little credit can be as costly as too much (forgone improvements); losses can be spread tolerably if the government controls restructuring and the debt is in its currency. p. 57

Late-cycle choices, deleveraging and monetization
- Late in the cycle, holders of debt want to sell, so debt will have a bad return one way or another; central bankers must choose between not monetizing (rates rise to the equilibrium that balances bond buying and selling → cash valuable, stocks and hard assets down, deflation, defaults, depression) and monetizing (money plentiful and devalued, inflation, other assets up, fewer defaults). The first typically happens first and is intolerable; the second eventually. p. 58
- Bubble pops from the combination of monetary tightening and an unsustainable prior rate of debt growth; the contraction is self-reinforcing and spreads "like an aggressive cancer". p. 21
- A debt contraction can be temporarily reversed with more credit and debt — until it can't, when the big deleveraging occurs. p. 22
- Debt problems spread from the private sector to the central government and then to the central bank. p. 22
- Selling of debt assets drives rates up, which makes debt harder to service and riskier, which drives rates higher still; the central bank then supplies money and credit to fill the demand gap, lowering the value of money and credit while lowering credit risk. p. 22
- Selling of government debt → a) market-driven tightening, b) weaker economy, c) downward pressure on the currency, d) falling reserves as the central bank defends the currency; eventually it eases and allows devaluation. p. 22–23
- Cutting spending in a contraction raises debt-to-income ratios because spending is income; the policy mix shifts to restructurings plus monetizations, with the mix depending on how much debt is in the local currency. p. 23
- Equilibrium is reached through "a few painful adjustment spasms" because borderline soundness is reached before secure soundness. p. 23–24
- To stimulate, the central bank must get rates below nominal growth, inflation and bond rates — hard near 0%; when rates can't fall, demand for government debt is short and easing is insufficient, it switches to new tools (printing/buying). p. 24
- At the same time the government's deficit widens (revenue down, spending up) while private demand for its debt is insufficient — the debt squeeze; net selling makes it much worse. p. 24
- Central bank buying is aimed at government debt and systemically important private debt to make up for inadequate demand and keep rates artificially low; sometimes equities; devaluation is used because it is stimulative and raises inflation; gold links are broken, fiat follows. p. 25
- Central bank actions can raise nominal rates either by tightening against inflation or by not tightening so that holders refuse new issuance — watch real and nominal rates and the supply/demand for debt. p. 25
- Extraordinary measures become common at this stage: extraordinary taxes, capital controls, freezing/seizing "enemy" assets, new forms of money. p. 26
- Deleveraging reduces debt burdens by defaults, restructurings and/or devaluations; typically debt-to-income must fall by roughly 50% ± ~20%. p. 26
- Restructurings reduce burdens and are deflationary; monetary stimulation reduces burdens (money to buy debt) and is inflationary; balanced, they produce positive growth, falling burdens and acceptable inflation. p. 26
- A new cycle requires a) sound money/debt, b) debt and debt service in line with incomes, c) belief by creditors and debtors that this will hold, d) money/credit availability and real rates in line with both sides' needs; credibility is rebuilt by the government earning more than it spends and/or the central bank offering high real yields, raising reserves, or linking to gold or a strong currency. p. 27
- The type of monetary policy in use reveals the stage of the cycle (MP0 → MP1 → MP2 → MP3 → deleveraging → return to hard money). p. 27–31
- A modern central bank "prints" by borrowing reserves from commercial banks at a very short-term rate; it loses money when the interest it earns on bought debt is below what it pays on reserves; large losses force more printing, which is the central bank's death spiral and devalues money, creating inflationary recessions or depressions. p. 30
- Restructurings and monetizations eventually shrink debts relative to incomes and the cycle runs its course; for great empires the end of the cycle has typically meant the end of their prominence. p. 30–31
- Austerity is tried first and fails: cutting debts and spending cuts incomes and net worths commensurately, revenue falls while demands rise, deficits widen; tax hikes at that point squeeze further. p. 59
- Spending greater than revenue plus liquid liabilities greater than liquid assets → need to borrow and sell debt assets → without enough demand, either a deflationary or an inflationary crisis. p. 59
- Beautiful deleveraging makes nominal growth (inflation + real growth) exceed nominal interest rates so debt burdens fall relative to income. p. 59
- Own-currency big debt crises were mostly restructured quickly, typically in one to three years. p. 60
- Healthy: private credit for profitable uses; government borrowing for productivity gains repaid from taxes, or countercyclical borrowing repaid in good times. Unhealthy: the central bank chronically printing to cover the shortfall in demand for debt; the government chronically running large deficits with debt and debt service rising faster than tax revenue. p. 60–61
- At the beginning of the end of the Big Debt Cycle both the tight-money contraction and the loose-money inflation occur, "the only question is in what order"; owning the debt/currency of overly indebted governments then is a bad investment (red dot). p. 62

Ch. 3 mechanics in words
- As debt/income rises, more interest and rollover payments each year → rollover risk and higher debt service as a share of income, which crowds out spending. p. 66
- High debt service relative to income leads investors to expect credit problems, lend less and sell debt assets, which creates the credit problems. p. 67
- Nominal rates relative to inflation and nominal growth determine how debt and debt service grow relative to income and how attractive lending is vs borrowing; high rates vs growth/inflation favor lenders and discourage borrowing (signal of risk among debtors who can't print). p. 67
- Large savings/reserves buffer default risk even when the other ratios are bad. p. 67–68
- The four ratios tell how likely and how severe a squeeze will be, not exactly when, because lead times for selling differ. p. 68
- Rising rates raise interest costs only gradually as bonds roll into new rates; stressed debtors see maturities shorten, raising principal payments for the same debt level. p. 71
- Interest costs are the product of debt level and effective rate, so when rates rise above revenue growth both inputs rise and debt service grows faster than debt. p. 72
- Falling rates make debt grow more slowly and debt service shrink (Japan over the last 20 years). p. 72
- Issuance need = primary deficit + interest + maturing principal; if the public won't buy at current rates, rates rise until someone will, which worsens the deficit and the desire to sell — the classic death spiral; rising rates also constrict income growth; forced extra issuance (war, recession benefits) drives rates up much more. p. 80
- Governments under squeeze are the largest part of the economy and the only payer of large non-economic social expenses, so cutting spending then is self-damaging and can cause social turmoil; the fight over taxes and left/right conflict follows. p. 82
- Private demand for government debt depends on expected real bond returns vs other assets, total money and credit, and the sense of impending debt/currency crisis — hard to project but observable as a) rates rising while the economy and currency are weak or b) the central bank spending reserves and/or printing to buy debt. p. 83
- Monetization: the central bank creates reserves/cash, gives it to private players for bonds, raising M0; the more private demand falls short at the rates needed, the more the central bank must step in and the more money is created, which devalues money and makes holding debt less attractive. p. 84, 86
- All else equal, central bank money and credit creation lowers the value of money, raising inflation and weakening the currency (not precise; depends on transmission). p. 86
- Lowering rates weakens the spot currency: long-run (10-year forward) currency expectations barely move, so less interest in the meantime makes the deal worse, and the spot must fall so appreciation toward the same forward point compensates. p. 87
- Technically: a rise in one country's 10-year risk-free yield relative to another's raises its 10-year forward currency; the interest differential is offset by the forward premium (A 2% above B → A's forward at a 2%/yr discount); lowering A's rate by 1% with the forward fixed weakens the spot correspondingly. p. 87–88
- Printed money can also flow directly out of the currency as bond sellers buy other currencies. p. 88
- The worse a currency is viewed as a storehold of value, the more sensitive it becomes to money supply; e.g., if printing 1% of GDP causes ~1% weakness, the current-example scenario implies ~10% depreciation; sensitivity rises as private players lend less. p. 88
- A central bank can stop debt service from rising, or make it fall, relative to inflation and incomes by pushing nominal rates below nominal growth; this hurts lender-creditors via lower nominal and real returns. p. 79, 89
- A government can prevent the spiral by reducing its debt burdens via the four levers; historically a mix is used with "a strong bias to print money and buy debt" when the squeeze is big. p. 82
- The easiest (not best) path is for the central bank to print and buy bonds to hold rates at tolerable levels; governments "will unfailingly do" this when debts are in their own currency (red dot). p. 82–83

## (d) Numbers and equations (ch. 3 plus quantitative statements elsewhere)

### Core identities and formulas

1. **Price identity** (p. 40): Price (P) = amount spent ($) / total quantity sold (Q); P = $/Q. Estimating price means estimating total spending and total quantity sold, by understanding the motivations of the biggest buyers and sellers. Buying rate from X to X−10%, all else equal → price −10% (p. 43).

2. **Total return** (p. 45): total return = yield + price change.

3. **Interest parity / currency arbitrage** (pp. 48–49, fns. 11–13):
   - If Country A's government rate is below Country B's by X%/yr, A's currency must be expected to appreciate by X%/yr.
   - Worked example: A's 10-year rate 3% below B's but A's currency expected to *fall* 2%/yr → near-risk-free trade; investors sell A's debt/currency. Adjustments: 1) A's spot currency falls ~40%, or 2) A's 10-year rate rises by 5%, sending bond prices down ~40%; or if capital controls block both, holders lose 5%/yr, compounding to ~40% over 10 years.
   - Fn. 11 math: 2% depreciation for 10 years → forward = 82% of current; spot must fall to 0.61 since 0.61 × 1.03^10 = 0.82 (≈40% move).
   - Fn. 12: bond price impact = change in yield × duration; duration of 10-year government bonds is 7–8 years; 8 × 5% = 40%.
   - Fn. 13: currency weakness and inflation reduce the debt burden when nominal rate < nominal growth, especially when nominal rate < inflation (negative real rates).

4. **Formula 1 — Future debt relative to future income** (p. 69):
   Future Debt / Future Revenue = [ (Future Expenses Excluding Interest − Future Revenue) + Current Debt × (1 + Interest Rate) ] / [ Current Revenue × (1 + Growth Rate) ].
   Drivers: 1) spending more or less than revenue (first numerator term), 2) compounding of existing debt (second numerator term), 3) revenue growth (denominator). Uses debt-to-*revenue*, not debt-to-GDP, because GDP only matters if it is tapped (p. 70). Restated on p. 90.

5. **Footnote-17 identity** (p. 70): Debt/Income_t − Debt/Income_(t−1) = (i_t − g_t) × Debt/Income_(t−1) + (Primary Deficit/Income)_t, where g = income growth rate, i = interest rate.
   Implication: to keep debt/income constant, Primary Deficit/Income = (g − i) × Debt/Income.

6. **Formula 2 — Future debt service relative to future income** (p. 71):
   Future Debt Service / Future Revenue = (Future Interest Costs + Future Principal Payments) / [ Current Revenue × (1 + Growth Rate) ];
   Future Interest Costs = Future Debt Level × Average Effective Interest Rate on Debt;
   Future Principal Payments = Future Debt Level × Share of Debts Coming Due.
   Rough estimate of principal payments: use the average maturity of existing debts.

7. **Formula 3 — Interest rate required to keep debt flat** (p. 72):
   Interest Rate Required to Keep Debt Flat = Revenue Growth Rate − (Future Expenses Excluding Interest − Future Revenue) / Starting Debt Level.
   If primary deficit = 0, debt stays flat when interest rate = revenue growth rate; if primary deficit = 5% of current debt level, rates must be 5% below revenue growth. Can be rearranged to solve for the required deficit/surplus, revenue growth, etc.

8. **Formula 4 — Debt and debt service relative to savings/reserves** (p. 73, fn. 18 notes it is inexact):
   Future Debt / Future Savings = [ (Current Expenses Excluding Interest − Current Revenue) + Current Debt × (1 + Interest Rate) ] / [ Current Savings + Expected Savings ];
   Future Debt Service / Future Savings = (Future Interest Costs + Future Principal Payments) / (Current Savings + Expected Savings).

### Rules of thumb and worked examples

- Debt 100% of income, nominal rate 5%, nominal income growth 3%, spending = income → owes about 102% of income next year (p. 67).
- If nominal rates = nominal income growth and no primary deficit, debt/income is unchanged; if rates exceed income growth, existing debt burdens rise — "probably the single most important variable" (red dot, p. 74).
- Rates 2% above income growth, no primary deficit → debt/income rises ~50% over 20 years: 50% → 75%, 400% → 600% (p. 74). (Check: 1.02^20 ≈ 1.49.)
- Typical deleveraging must cut debt-to-income by roughly 50%, give or take about 20% (p. 26); spreading a ~50% reduction at 3% or 4% per year is far less traumatic than 50% in one year (p. 32).
- Bubble sign: debt growth significantly faster than income growth for an extended period, e.g., three years (p. 21). Unicorn example: >$1 billion valuation on $50 million raised (p. 21).
- Central government takes in and uses typically about 15–30% of money and credit (p. 37); fn. 6: government spending is typically 35–55% of all spending in developed countries including state and local.
- Short-term debt cycle: about six years, give or take three (pp. 14, 55). Long-term: about 80 years, give or take 25 (pp. 3, 55). Short-term dominant over 1–10 years; long-term and productivity trend dominant over 10+ years (p. 56).
- Only about 20% of the roughly 750 debt/currency markets that have existed since 1700 remain; the rest were severely devalued (p. 3). Fn. 1: prior work covered 48 biggest debt crises 1918–2018 and 500 years of reserve-currency history.
- US ratios: in 1945 government debt / US gold holdings = 7x and money supply / gold = 1.3x; now 37x and 6x (p. 19).
- Late 1940s–early 1950s: stock earnings yields roughly 4x bond yields (p. 19).
- Japan: bondholders lost 45% relative to holding US dollar debt and 60% relative to gold since 2013 (p. 33).
- Central banks: US central bank dates from 1913 (p. 53). US monetary phases: MP0 1945–1971; MP1 1971–2008; MP2 2008–2020 (pp. 28–29).
- Dalio tracks about 35 indicators of debt risk; the four most important are listed in (e) (p. 66). Part II template is derived from 35 cases (pp. 6, 33). *Changing World Order* used 18 measures (p. 63).
- Own-currency big debt crises typically restructured in one to three years (p. 60).

### Example 1 — Debts relative to incomes (pp. 75–77)
- US government expenditures excluding interest projected to average ~112% of income over the next decade → primary deficit ~12% of income (CBO baseline, fn. 20).
- US borrows ~20% of its income each year to cover interest on existing debt.
- US government debt / money coming in (mostly tax income) ≈ 580% "as of this writing".
- With rates = growth and a 12% primary deficit, debt/income rises by about 120 points, from 580% to 700%, over 10 years; interest and debt-service burdens rise proportionally.
- Fn. 20/21: CBO baseline assumes the Trump tax cuts expire; extending them adds 1.5% of GDP or 8% of government revenue per year.
- Tables on p. 76 (see (f)).

### Example 2 — Nominal rate minus nominal growth (pp. 77–79)
- Assumes a starting total deficit of 32% of income (CBO projected deficit over the next decade), i.e., 12% primary deficit + interest.
- CBO: effective interest rates average 3.45% and nominal growth 3.9% over the next decade; difference ≈ −0.4%.
- Incorporating that gap: US debt/income rises to ~650% (vs 700% with rates = growth).
- Tables on p. 78 (see (f)).

### Example 3 — Interest rates spiral upward (pp. 79–82; table p. 81)
Toy model inputs: income growth 3.9%/yr; spending excl. interest 112% of income; starting debt $30.1T; starting interest rate 3.5%; 35% of debt matures each year (about the US share). Year 1: revenue $5.4T, spending $6.0T (spends 32% more than income including interest; 12% primary deficit), interest $1T, rollover $10.5T → must sell $12.2T of debt. Scenario: rates rise 0.5%/yr (4.0% in year 1 to 8.5% in year 10) with nominal growth flat.
- If rates stayed flat, Year 10: debt 650% of income, interest 22% of income.
- With rates rising 50 bp/yr, Year 10: debt 865% of income, interest 67%, total debt service (incl. principal) 342% of income.
Full table in (f).

### Example 4 — Central bank steps in (pp. 83–89; table p. 85)
- Same starting point; Year 1 has $10.5T expiring and $12.2T of new issuance.
- Assumption: the central bank buys all excess issuance so the private sector holds no more than 600% of government income in debt and rates stay flat at 3.5%; Year 2 purchases $0.1T, growing thereafter.
- Money supply (M0) starts at $5.7T = 110% of starting government income, "roughly where it is today in the United States".
- Over 10 years the central bank buys $6T of bonds and M0 goes from $5.7T to $11.8T (p. 86).
- Year 10 ratios: debt/income 677%, debt service/income 247%, interest/income 22.4%.
- FX sensitivity example: if printing 1% of GDP causes ~1% currency weakness, this scenario implies ~10% depreciation (p. 88).

### Stabilization arithmetic for the US (pp. 90–91)
To stabilize debt-to-income over the next 10 years the US would need **one** of: nominal interest rates falling to about 1.0% (−3.5 pts vs the current 4.5%, −2.5 pts vs CBO's projected 3.5%); nominal growth averaging about 6.5% (inflation 4.5%, i.e., ~2.5 pts above CBO's 2.0%); a 12% spending cut (= 47% of discretionary spending); or an 11% revenue (tax) increase. Each alone is intolerably large, so a combination is needed (Chapter 18's "3% 3-Part Solution").

## (h) Red-dot principles (short paraphrases)

- Agreement on how things work is a precondition for agreeing on what is happening or likely to happen. p. 4
- The credit → spending → repayment dynamic is inherently cyclical. p. 14
- A debt is a promise to deliver money; a debt crisis is more promises than money; the central bank must print (devalue) or not print (default crisis) and in the end always prints and devalues; either way too much debt makes bonds worth less. p. 16
- There is always a popular meme that nearly everyone believes, already in the price and bound to be wrong in some way; investors extrapolate, ignore pricing, buy long and use leverage. p. 20
- Net selling of debt assets, especially government debt, is a big red flag. p. 22
- When rates rise the central bank loses on its bonds because it pays more on liabilities than it earns; this is a big red flag only once net worth is very negative and it must print to cover negative cash flow. p. 30
- Big debt crises are inevitable. p. 31
- Build savings in good times; both too much and too little saving have costs. p. 32
- Anticipate crises by watching many interrelated dynamics, not a single number like debt/GDP. p. 32
- If debts are in the country's own currency the central bank can and will print to alleviate the crisis. p. 32, 63
- All debt crises can be managed well by balancing deflationary restructuring with inflationary monetization; the key is spreading repayment over time. p. 32
- Debt crises bring great risks and great opportunities, destroying empires and rewarding prepared investors. p. 32–33
- Five most important drivers of change: debt/credit/money/economic cycle; internal political order/disorder; external geopolitical order/disorder; acts of nature; human inventiveness. p. 36
- All markets and economies are aggregates of transactions. p. 39
- Price = money/credit given by the buyer ÷ quantity given by the seller; a market is the aggregate of those transactions. p. 39
- Price (P) = $ spent / Q sold; P = $/Q. p. 40
- Knowing total spending and total quantity sold tells you the price and everything else you need. p. 40
- Identifying unsustainable rates of buying or selling identifies unsustainable prices and conditions. p. 43
- More money and credit with spare capacity → non-inflationary growth. p. 44
- More money and credit without spare capacity → little real growth and much inflation. p. 44
- People buy goods and services to use and investments to make money (storeholds of wealth). p. 44
- What people buy depends on relative appeal (item vs item; now vs later). p. 45
- Currencies are mediums of exchange and storeholds of wealth (in debt assets). p. 45
- Investments exchange money and credit today for money and credit in the future. p. 45
- All investments derive value from yield and price change; total return = yield + price change. p. 45
- All investment markets compete on total return, because investors care about total return and can arbitrage. p. 45–46
- Expected real returns relative to inflation steer how much money goes into each asset. p. 47
- Prices are linked by determinants (spot/forward relationships) one must understand to grasp relative pricing. p. 47
- Debt and currency are essentially the same thing; dislike one and you must dislike the other. p. 48
- Five types of players drive money and debt cycles: borrower-debtors, lender-creditors, banks, central governments, central banks. p. 50
- Credit expansion needs willing borrowers and lenders, so the deal must be good for both. p. 50–51
- Over the long run debts cannot outgrow the incomes that service them, and rates cannot be too high for borrowers or too low for lenders for long. p. 52
- Big debt crises come when debt assets and liabilities are too large relative to money and/or goods and services. p. 52
- Central banks want debt growth, economic growth and inflation at acceptable levels. p. 53
- Central governments are political organizations that want to give people what they want. p. 53
- The larger debt assets/liabilities are relative to real incomes, the harder the lender/borrower balancing act and the likelier a debt-caused downturn. p. 54
- Short-term debt cycles add up to the Big Debt Cycle. p. 55
- Sustainability hinges on whether debt generates enough income to pay its service; otherwise debt/income grows mechanically and borrowing is needed to service debt. p. 56
- A sure sign of an approaching debt crisis is a large and rising amount of borrowing used to pay debt service. p. 56
- Debt isn't always bad even when uneconomic; too little credit can be worse than too much, and losses can be spread if the government controls restructuring and the debt is printable. p. 57
- From cycle to cycle debt has virtually always increased until burdens were unsustainable or debt assets intolerably low-returning. p. 57
- Goods, services and investment assets are produced, bought and sold with money and credit. p. 61
- Central banks can produce money and influence credit in whatever quantities they want. p. 61
- Borrower-debtors need enough money and low enough rates to borrow and service debt. p. 61
- Lender-creditors need high enough rates and low enough default rates to earn adequate returns. p. 61
- The balancing act gets harder as debt assets and liabilities grow relative to incomes; eventually a deleveraging reduces them. p. 61
- The best deleveraging is a beautiful one, which governments and central banks can engineer if debts are in their own currency; foreign-currency debt makes deleveraging ugly. p. 61
- Long-term financial health = productivity, earning more than spending, and more assets than liabilities. p. 61
- Knowing where each country is in the cycle and how players behave lets you navigate cycles well. p. 62
- The past is prologue. p. 62
- Debt crises are inevitable because lending is never done perfectly and psychology produces bubbles and busts. p. 62
- Most debt crises, even big ones, can be managed well if their negative impacts are spread out. p. 62
- All debt crises provide investment opportunities for those who understand them. p. 62
- At the beginning of the end of the Big Debt Cycle, real rates cannot satisfy both lenders and borrowers; both tight-money contraction and loose-money inflation occur, only the order is uncertain; owning the debt/currency of overly indebted governments then is a bad investment. p. 62
- Central banks must choose hard money (defaults, deflationary depression) or soft money (printing, devaluation of money and debt); they always eventually print; each can print only its own currency. p. 62–63
- An unsustainable debt burden exists when inflows are less than outflows so that savings fall and/or borrowing rises until one runs out of savings and/or cannot borrow, when a debt failure occurs. p. 65–66
- Equilibrium levels of debt/income, debt service/income, nominal rates vs inflation and growth, and debt/savings will be approached; the ratios go to extremes and return one way or another; the deleveraging can be handled well or badly. p. 68
- Countries with very high debt, very large deficits, low savings and very high, fast-rising rates have a very high risk of a default or devaluation crisis. p. 68
- With nominal rates equal to nominal income growth and no primary deficit, debt/income is unchanged; rates above growth raise existing burdens. p. 74
- Accumulating debt service is like arterial plaque, squeezing out the flow of nutrients to the economy. p. 74
- The main effect of high debt is vulnerability to being unable to roll it forward. p. 74
- The easiest (not best) path is for governments to have the central bank print and buy bonds to hold rates down; they will unfailingly do this when debts are in their own currency. p. 82–83
- Buying bonds and increasing the money supply are stimulative and push the currency down. p. 87
