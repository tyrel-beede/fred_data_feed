# Credit Crossed Its Line. The Index Crossed Back.

*A 5.6 percent rebound on a reinstated chief executive while every rung of the corporate-bond ladder widened into its stress zone — and the dashboard's bust weight rose from 11 to 19 on spreads alone, with nothing in its confirmation set fired.*

## Lede

On Monday morning Nvidia's board announced that Jensen Huang, whom it had said five days earlier would not return, would return. The stock rose 31.5 percent in a session and 20 percent on the week; the S&P 500, which had closed the previous Friday at 6,910 after seven straight losses, ended the holiday-shortened week at 7,297, up 5.6 percent, and the fear gauge fell from 36 to 24. By any reading of the tape, the correction paused.

By the reading of the bond market it did not. The extra yield that investment-grade companies pay over Treasuries — the spread that the dashboard has watched for two months as the one price that had not moved — printed 99 basis points on Wednesday and again on Friday, up from 94 the week before and 89 a month ago, one point under the line the dashboard treats as an event. Every tier below it crossed its own line in the same week: triple-B 124, double-B 228, single-B 413, the high-yield index 377, the weakest CCC tier 1,417 — each at or near the widest of its three-year file. CoreWeave's bonds fell to 74 cents on the dollar, a yield near 18 percent; its five-year default insurance reached 1,314 basis points; the $4.5 billion loan it was meant to launch before Thanksgiving slipped to the week of November 30. Lenders in Asia froze data-center loans under a new American export rule, and Nvidia halted server shipments to four of the countries affected.

That is the week's argument: the stock market and the credit market moved in opposite directions, and the dashboard — which scores credit and not price — moved with credit. Its computed weight on a capex bust at the six-week horizon rose from 11 to 19 percent, its state reading moved from the edge of "healthy" back to the middle, and it did so without a single one of its confirmation events firing: no principal cut its spending, no bond deal was pulled, no refinancing failed. The bulls say the spread move is the tail of an equity de-rating that has already reversed; the bears say it is the first tier of transmission, the one that in 2007 came before lending standards and before the labor market. December 8 to 10 — two long-bond auctions, the Federal Reserve, Broadcom's call, the consumer-price index — and the CoreWeave loan on the way there will tell them apart.

## Terms used this week

> **Credit spread (OAS)** — the extra yield a corporate bond pays over a Treasury of the same maturity, in basis points (hundredths of a percentage point); the dashboard reads it as a percentile of the bond's own three-year history, with the top fifth called "stress." · **Investment grade / high yield / single-B / CCC** — the rating tiers from strongest balance sheet to weakest; "transmission" means widening moving up the ladder from CCC toward investment grade. · **Credit-default swap (CDS)** — insurance against a company's default, quoted as an annual cost in basis points; 1,314 means roughly 13 percent a year, which the market reads as about a 70 percent chance of default within five years. · **Real yield** — a Treasury yield after expected inflation, from inflation-protected bonds; the hurdle a capital project must clear. · **Indirect bidders and the tail** — at a Treasury auction, the share bought by foreign and fund accounts, and how far the clearing yield lands above the pre-auction market; both measure demand. · **Equal-weight versus cap-weight** — the same 500 stocks counted one each versus by size; the gap between them is the measure of how narrow a market is. · **Neoclouds** — the rented-GPU providers (CoreWeave, Nebius, Oracle's cloud) that borrow to buy chips and lease them to AI labs; the weakest tier of the chain. · **Convergence** — in the dashboard, several independent gauges (prices of money, equity prices, and a quantity such as flows or issuance) moving the same way in the same month, scored once and never as confirmation.

## Bulls

*The strongest honest version of the bull case is that credit spreads are catching up to a stock-market de-rating that has already ended, in a week when every quantity that would signal a bust — lending, jobs, growth, the bond market's willingness to fund the chain — said the opposite.*

### The confirmation set is still empty, and the one loan in the market is being upsized

The dashboard's rule is that a capex bust is confirmed by events, not prices: a principal cutting its 2027 spending, an investment-grade spread above 115, a hyperscaler bond deal pulled or weakly bid, Nvidia guiding below consensus, rented-GPU prices falling a fifth. After a month in which the index entered a correction and the chip index lost a quarter of its value, none has happened. This week's evidence ran the other way at the companies that sell the equipment: Dell raised its full-year AI-server guide to $78 billion from $74 billion and said no customer had rescheduled an order; Hewlett Packard Enterprise reported AI orders up 84 percent on the quarter. The one AI loan actually in the market — CoreWeave's, secured by chips and a Meta contract — was reported by Bloomberg at $4.5 billion against a $4 billion plan, with early price talk of 7 to 7.5 percent and indications of interest "in line with size." A borrower whose unsecured bonds yield 18 percent is being offered secured money at 7.5 percent, which is what a functioning credit market does with collateral: it prices the asset, not the name. The expert check is the launch itself in the week of November 30, and the dashboard's own fork gives a one-in-four chance it fails, prices above 9 percent or shrinks.

### The quantities that measure a bust are at records in the other direction

The bull's strongest point is one the dashboard makes against itself: its convergence rule insists that prices alone never confirm, because a single factor — this month, the AI de-rating — can move the bond yield, the stock spread and the equity price of the same name at once. The quantities are what separate a 2007 from a 2016. Commercial and industrial lending by banks is at $3.0 trillion, the 99th percentile of its history and up $9 billion over four weeks; the Fed's October survey of loan officers found standards essentially unchanged. Initial jobless claims printed 189,000, with the four-week average 0.4 percent above its one-year low — the dashboard's labor dial needs a 20 percent rise before it scores. Third-quarter growth was revised up to 4.3 percent. Net liquidity in the banking system rose $5 billion on the week, and the overnight funding rate sat 5 basis points over the Fed's floor at month-end, which is nothing. In 2015–16, when energy junk bonds reached distress and small caps fell 27 percent, the same quantities held and the economy never turned. The periphery-first pattern has false positives, and every one of them looked like this in its credit statistics.

### What moved this week was ownership, and the bond hedged again

The second half of the bull case is about who sold. The Bank of America survey last week recorded the largest cut to technology positioning since 2006; this week's fund-flow data showed the first weekly outflow of the episode from US equity funds, $30.6 billion, with $9.3 billion out of technology funds and money-market funds drained for a third week. Margin debt fell from its record in October. Asset managers trimmed their S&P futures longs a second week. In the dashboard's language this is the "revealed" side of the synchronization game finally moving — and when it moves, the bull notes, it is the sellers who are capitulating, not the buyers who are leaving. The sell side capitulated on one name: the average Nvidia price target fell 25 percent and the number of hold ratings went from two to sixteen, taking the Street's buy share across the principals and suppliers to 88 percent, under the 90 percent line the dashboard uses as a mania marker for the first time. And the bond market did what it does in a healthy correction: the ten-year fell 19 basis points into Thanksgiving while stocks fell, the stock–bond correlation stayed negative, the dollar rose, gold fell. The debt-crunch branch — the one where stocks, bonds and the dollar fall together — carries a weight of zero, and this week's long-end auctions were the week's one place the dashboard scored against it, at its lowest tier and on foreign demand rather than price. An expert would check the December 8 and 10 reopenings, where a tail of two basis points or more is priced at 30 percent each.

## Bears

*The strongest honest version of the bear case is that the first tier of credit transmission arrived this week — every rating tier widening into its stress zone at once, the strongest tier one point from the dashboard's line — and that the equity bounce was a governance headline in the one stock that had been punished for governance.*

### The ladder widened in a single week, and the strongest rung is the one that matters

The bear begins where the dashboard scored. Investment-grade spreads at 99 basis points are at the 86th percentile of their three-year history, in the stress band for the first time this cycle, and they got there in a straight line: 89 a month ago, 94 last Thursday, 98 on Tuesday, 99 on Wednesday and Friday. Triple-B — the lowest investment-grade rung, the one that funds most of the chain's long-dated paper — is at 124, wider than the whole investment-grade index was in October. High yield at 377 crossed its stress line; single-B at 413 crossed into "acute," the top 5 percent of its history; CCC at 1,417 is the widest in its file. The dashboard scored the move once, as a zone transition worth a factor of 1.5 on the bust's odds, because its rule counts a single channel once; the bear's point is that the rule understates a move that happened on every rung in the same five sessions. Its transmission test — single-B widening 25 basis points in a month when CCC widens 100 — missed by four basis points (CCC +96). The expert check is next week's print: investment grade at 100 on two consecutive days is a registered fork at 55 percent, and 115 — the confirmation element — at 12.

### The chain is being priced by its lenders now, not by its equity holders

Last week's column argued that the clean tests had measured demand when the question was funding. This week funding answered. CoreWeave's bonds fell to post-issue lows — 74 cents, 17.9 percent — and its default insurance to 1,314 basis points, which the market reads as roughly a 70 percent chance of default inside five years, in the same week its loan slipped and in the same week the lender's protection on that loan is a contract with Meta: the strongest unit in the chain is now the collateral for the weakest, which is the dependency map the dashboard's seventh stage indicator exists to track. Oracle's 2036 bonds yielded 8.47 percent, a cycle high. In Asia, Bloomberg reported banks freezing or stepping back from data-center loans after a US rule on third-country chips — a $480 million facility frozen, a $6.6 billion one delayed, a fund exiting a $300 million deal — while Nvidia halted AI-server shipments to Malaysia, Singapore, Thailand and Indonesia; "no cancelled deals," which is why the dashboard logged it as a near-miss and not an event. Citi and JPMorgan were reported marketing AI-loan risk to other investors; the Bank of England's stability report named AI financing as a contagion channel. S&P put Nvidia's double-A rating on watch for a downgrade over the governance episode the equity market celebrated. Paraphrasing Torsten Slok's note of November 24, software credit is the "double whammy" — loan yields above 13 percent into a 2028–29 maturity wall. None of this is a default. All of it is the stage before one.

### The bounce was one headline in one name, and the sellers have only begun

The bear reads the week's equity move as the bull reads the spread move: as a lagging reversal of something already decided. The S&P 500's 5.6 percent came from a reinstated chief executive — a decision reversed in five days, after a report of an SEC accounting inquiry that has not gone away — and the market paid 31 percent for it in a stock the average analyst now values 25 percent lower than three weeks ago. The periphery lenses did not reverse on the month: neoclouds trail hyperscalers by 28.5 points, suppliers trail principals by 16.2, semiconductors trail the Nasdaq by 12.2, and the neocloud basket sits at 70 against a base of 100 in mid-October. Paul Kedrosky wrote this week that the memory cycle is turning "even sooner than expected," with spot NAND prices down more than a fifth; BNP cut Micron to neutral with a price target less than half the Street's, citing less memory per chip in Nvidia's next product; TrendForce now dates the DRAM contract-price decline to early 2028, a year earlier than its bull case. The dashboard's memory dial does not score until contract prices fall two quarters, which is the lag the bear is pointing at: Cisco's inventory was up 29 percent before its one-penny miss. And the first outflow of the episode — $30.6 billion, the largest since September — came in the week the index bounced. Paul Krugman's November 25 column called it the deflation of the AI bubble resuming; the bear would say the index has not yet noticed what its own credit says.

## Pigs

A pig, in Stanley Druckenmiller's sense, is a position whose shape is lopsided: small if it is wrong, large if it is right. The point is never the odds of being right; it is how much a correct call pays against how much a wrong one costs. Each item below states both sides, traces every number to the dashboard's run, names the thing that would kill it, and is graded in a later column whether it paid or not.

**1. Equity insurance got cheap in the week credit got expensive.** In plain words: the cost of protecting a stock portfolio fell by a third while the cost of protecting a bond portfolio rose — and those two prices usually move together. The VIX closed at 23.81 on Friday from 35.89 a week earlier and its seven-session inversion unwound, while investment-grade spreads went from 94 to 99 and high yield from 352 to 377. The mechanism is the two markets reading one event — Huang's return — as a reversal and as nothing, respectively; one of them is wrong about December 8–10, which carries two long-bond auctions (tail probability 30 percent each), the Fed (25 basis points at 94–98 percent), Broadcom and the CPI (a 0.4 core print at 25 percent) inside three sessions. Payoff shape: equity volatility bought at the 24 handle pays several times its cost on any of those outcomes landing badly; it costs the premium if the week clears clean and the index drifts higher. **The bears need, by December 10:** investment grade at 100 on two consecutive days, which is one point away; the CoreWeave loan to print wide, late or small, or its default insurance to stay above 1,300 through a priced loan; a long-end tail into the Fed's hike (the crunch cluster's price side filling its quantity side); Broadcom to confirm a principal's order cut — the first explicit defection in the arms race, worth a factor of 4 and a confirmation element; or Oracle, whose call is still undated, to use the word "pace" about its data centers.

The dashboard scores these mechanically: each is a registered fork with a probability and a likelihood ratio written before the event, and the computed non-event rule means a bear test that passes clean lowers the bust's odds by a stated amount (a clean CoreWeave loan by 0.43, a clean Broadcom call by 0.66) just as a firing raises them. Two confirmation elements by December 9 — the layer-2 simulation puts that at 9 percent against a state weight of 19 — would label the bust confirmed; a clean sweep returns the state toward "A" within two runs.
